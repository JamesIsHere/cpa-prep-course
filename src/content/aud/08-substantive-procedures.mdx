# Substantive Procedures

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish between tests of details and substantive analytical procedures
- Understand how substantive procedures respond to assessed risks at the assertion level
- Apply substantive procedures to key financial statement accounts
- Recognize how the nature, timing, and extent of substantive procedures are determined

## What Are Substantive Procedures?

**Substantive procedures** are audit procedures designed to detect material misstatements at the assertion level. Unlike tests of controls (which evaluate whether controls are working), substantive procedures test the amounts and disclosures in the financial statements directly.

There are two categories:

1. **Tests of details** — Procedures applied to individual transactions, balances, or disclosures
2. **Substantive analytical procedures** — Evaluations of financial information through analysis of plausible relationships

The auditor must perform substantive procedures for every **material** class of transactions, account balance, and disclosure, regardless of the assessed risk. Even when controls are tested and found effective, substantive procedures are still required.

> This is a common exam trap: the auditor can never completely eliminate substantive procedures by relying on controls. At minimum, substantive procedures are required for all material accounts.

## Tests of Details

Tests of details involve examining individual items within a population to verify specific assertions. The auditor selects the type of test based on the assertion being addressed.

### Direction of Testing

The **direction of testing** is a fundamental concept. The direction depends on which assertion the auditor is targeting:

- **Testing for overstatement (existence/occurrence)** — Start with the recorded amount and trace to supporting evidence. For example, select items from the accounts receivable subsidiary ledger and trace to shipping documents and sales orders.
- **Testing for understatement (completeness)** — Start with the source documents and trace to the recorded amounts. For example, select shipping documents and verify they were recorded as sales.

This distinction matters because starting from the wrong direction will not address the targeted assertion effectively. If the auditor wants to test whether all liabilities are recorded (completeness), selecting items from the accounts payable ledger only tests what is already recorded — it misses unrecorded liabilities entirely.

### Common Tests of Details by Account

**Cash:**
- Obtain bank confirmations to verify balances
- Test bank reconciliations by tracing reconciling items
- Perform cutoff testing for receipts and disbursements around year-end
- Review bank transfers near year-end for **kiting** (improper inflation of cash by recording a transfer as a deposit without recording the corresponding disbursement)

**Accounts Receivable:**
- Send positive confirmations for large and unusual balances
- Send negative confirmations for a sample of smaller, routine balances
- Perform alternative procedures for non-responses (examine subsequent cash receipts, inspect shipping documents)
- Test the allowance for doubtful accounts by reviewing aging, historical collection rates, and specific customer circumstances

**Inventory:**
- Observe the physical inventory count
- Perform test counts and trace to the final inventory listing
- Test inventory pricing (unit costs) by vouching to purchase invoices or standard cost records
- Evaluate the reasonableness of obsolescence reserves
- Perform cutoff testing for purchases and sales near year-end

**Accounts Payable (testing for completeness):**
- Search for unrecorded liabilities by examining cash disbursements after year-end
- Review open purchase orders and receiving reports around year-end
- Confirm balances with vendors, particularly those with small or zero recorded balances

**Revenue:**
- Vouch recorded sales to shipping documents, customer orders, and invoices
- Test cutoff at period end
- Evaluate related-party transactions and side agreements
- Analyze unusual journal entries affecting revenue accounts

## Substantive Analytical Procedures

**Substantive analytical procedures** use relationships among financial and nonfinancial data to provide evidence about an assertion. They are most effective when:

- The relationship being analyzed is predictable and stable
- Data is available at a sufficient level of disaggregation
- The source data is reliable

### Developing an Expectation

The effectiveness of a substantive analytical procedure depends on the auditor's ability to develop a **precise expectation** of what the recorded amount should be. The more precise the expectation, the more assurance the procedure provides.

Consider testing payroll expense. The auditor could:

- **Low precision:** Compare total payroll this year to last year and expect it to be "in the same range." This provides limited assurance.
- **High precision:** Calculate expected payroll by multiplying the average number of employees by the average pay rate, adjusted for known raises and headcount changes. This provides significantly more assurance.

### Setting a Threshold

The auditor also establishes a **threshold** for investigating differences between the expected and recorded amounts. If the difference exceeds this threshold, the auditor must investigate to determine whether it indicates a misstatement.

### Common Substantive Analytical Procedures

- **Depreciation expense** — Calculate expected depreciation based on the asset listing, useful lives, and methods. Compare to the recorded amount.
- **Interest expense** — Multiply average outstanding debt by the weighted-average interest rate. Compare to recorded interest expense.
- **Payroll expense** — Use headcount data and average compensation to develop an independent estimate.
- **Rent expense** — Multiply monthly rent per the lease agreement by 12 months.

Substantive analytical procedures are generally more applicable to income statement accounts with predictable relationships than to balance sheet accounts where individual items may vary significantly.

## Nature, Timing, and Extent

The auditor's risk assessment drives the design of substantive procedures across three dimensions:

### Nature

The **nature** of a procedure refers to its type and purpose. For higher risks, auditors select procedures that provide more persuasive evidence:

- Confirmations instead of vouching to internal documents
- Physical inspection instead of inquiry
- Tests of details instead of (or in addition to) analytical procedures

### Timing

The **timing** of a procedure refers to when it is performed:

- **Year-end testing** provides stronger evidence because the auditor is testing the balance at the reporting date
- **Interim testing** (before year-end) is acceptable for lower-risk areas but requires the auditor to perform additional procedures to cover the **roll-forward period** between the interim date and year-end

When risk is higher, the auditor should perform procedures at or near the period end rather than at an interim date.

### Extent

The **extent** of a procedure refers to how much testing is performed — the sample size, the number of observations, or the amount of data analyzed. Higher risk generally requires larger sample sizes and more extensive testing.

> When the exam asks how an auditor should respond to a higher assessed risk, look for answers that describe more persuasive procedures (nature), testing closer to year-end (timing), and larger samples (extent).

## Auditing Accounting Estimates (AU-C 540)

**Accounting estimates** are among the highest-risk areas on any audit because they involve significant management judgment and inherent uncertainty. Common estimates include the allowance for doubtful accounts, fair value measurements, warranty reserves, depreciation useful lives, and impairment calculations.

### The Auditor's Approach

AU-C 540 requires the auditor to address three key aspects of each significant estimate:

1. **Reasonableness of the estimate** — Is management's estimate within an acceptable range given the facts and circumstances?
2. **Adequacy of disclosures** — Has management properly disclosed the estimation uncertainty and methodology?
3. **Indicators of management bias** — Are there signs that management is systematically over- or understating estimates?

### Methods for Testing Estimates

The auditor may use one or a combination of the following approaches:

- **Review and test management's process** — Evaluate the data, assumptions, and methodology used by management. Test the completeness and accuracy of the underlying data.
- **Develop an independent estimate** — Calculate an independent expectation and compare it to management's recorded amount. Any significant difference requires investigation.
- **Review subsequent events** — Examine events after the balance sheet date that may provide evidence about the reasonableness of the estimate (e.g., actual bad debt experience after year-end).

### Estimation Uncertainty and Significant Risks

When estimation uncertainty is high, the auditor should:
- Evaluate alternative assumptions or outcomes to determine a reasonable range
- Consider whether specialized skills are needed (actuaries, appraisers)
- Evaluate management's sensitivity analysis, if available
- Assess whether the selected estimate falls within the reasonable range or indicates potential bias

> AU-C 540 is heavily tested on the CPA exam. The key concept is that the auditor's job is not to determine the "correct" estimate but rather to evaluate whether management's estimate is reasonable and free from material misstatement, including bias.

## Going Concern Evaluation

The auditor has a responsibility to evaluate whether there is **substantial doubt about the entity's ability to continue as a going concern** for a reasonable period of time (one year from the date the financial statements are issued).

### Conditions and Events

The auditor should consider whether conditions or events, in the aggregate, raise substantial doubt. Common indicators include:

**Financial indicators:**
- Recurring operating losses
- Working capital deficiencies
- Negative cash flows from operations
- Inability to meet debt covenants
- Denial of trade credit from suppliers

**Operating indicators:**
- Loss of a major customer or supplier
- Loss of key personnel without replacement
- Work stoppages or labor difficulties
- Legal proceedings that could result in judgments exceeding the entity's resources

### Evaluation Process

1. **Identify conditions** — Assess whether conditions or events raise substantial doubt
2. **Evaluate management's plans** — If doubt exists, evaluate management's plans to mitigate the conditions (obtaining financing, restructuring debt, selling assets, reducing costs)
3. **Assess the feasibility of plans** — Determine whether management's plans are both realistic and likely to be effectively implemented
4. **Conclude and report** — Determine the impact on the audit opinion

### Reporting Implications

| Situation | Report Treatment |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Substantial doubt exists, adequate disclosure | Unmodified opinion with emphasis-of-matter paragraph |
| Substantial doubt exists, inadequate disclosure | Qualified or adverse opinion (departure from GAAP) |
| Substantial doubt alleviated by management plans | No modification needed, but consider disclosure |

> The going concern period runs from the date the financial statements are **issued** (or available to be issued), not from the balance sheet date. This is a common exam distinction.

## Dual-Purpose Tests

A **dual-purpose test** serves as both a test of controls and a substantive test simultaneously. For example, when inspecting a purchase order for an authorized signature (test of controls) and also verifying the accuracy of the recorded amount (substantive test), the auditor accomplishes two objectives with one procedure.

Dual-purpose tests are efficient, but the auditor must evaluate the results separately for each purpose. A control deviation does not necessarily mean a misstatement exists, and vice versa.

## Key Terms

- **Tests of details** — Substantive procedures applied to individual transactions, balances, or disclosures to detect material misstatements
- **Substantive analytical procedures** — Analysis of plausible relationships among financial and nonfinancial data used as substantive evidence
- **Direction of testing** — Whether the auditor traces from the recorded amount to supporting documents (existence) or from source documents to the records (completeness)
- **Roll-forward period** — The period between an interim testing date and the balance sheet date, requiring additional audit procedures
- **Dual-purpose test** — A single procedure that serves as both a test of controls and a substantive test

## Practice Problems

**1.** An auditor wants to test accounts payable for completeness. Which of the following procedures is most appropriate?

a) Select recorded payables and vouch to vendor invoices
b) Confirm large payable balances with vendors
c) Examine cash disbursements made after year-end and trace to recorded payables
d) Recalculate the accounts payable aging schedule

**Answer:** c) Testing for completeness requires starting with evidence outside the accounting records and tracing to the recorded amounts. Examining subsequent cash disbursements may reveal liabilities that existed at year-end but were not recorded.

---

**2.** Substantive analytical procedures are most effective when applied to:

a) Balance sheet accounts with significant individual transactions
b) Income statement accounts with predictable, stable relationships
c) Accounts affected by significant management estimates
d) Accounts with few transactions but large dollar amounts

**Answer:** b) Substantive analytical procedures work best when the relationship between the data is predictable and stable. Income statement accounts like depreciation, interest expense, and rent often meet this criterion because they can be estimated from known variables.

---

**3.** When the assessed risk of material misstatement for an assertion is high, the auditor should most likely:

a) Perform substantive procedures at an interim date to allow more time for investigation
b) Rely primarily on substantive analytical procedures rather than tests of details
c) Test at or near year-end with more extensive procedures using more persuasive evidence
d) Reduce the extent of testing and focus on the most material items only

**Answer:** c) Higher assessed risk calls for more persuasive evidence (nature), testing closer to year-end (timing), and larger sample sizes (extent). Interim testing is more appropriate for lower-risk areas.
