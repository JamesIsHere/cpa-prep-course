# Sampling

## Learning Objectives

- Understand the difference between statistical and nonstatistical sampling
- Distinguish between attributes sampling and variables sampling
- Apply sampling concepts to tests of controls and substantive procedures
- Recognize the types of sampling risk and their implications

## Why Auditors Sample

In most audits, it is impractical to examine every transaction or balance. **Audit sampling** allows the auditor to examine a subset of items within a population and draw conclusions about the entire population based on the results.

Sampling applies to both tests of controls and substantive tests of details. However, not all audit procedures involve sampling. Analytical procedures, inquiry, and observation, for example, are not sampling techniques.

> The exam distinguishes between procedures that involve sampling and those that do not. If the auditor is testing 100% of items in a population (such as all transactions over $1 million), that is not sampling — it is complete examination.

## Statistical vs. Nonstatistical Sampling

**Statistical sampling** uses the laws of probability to select the sample and evaluate results. It has two defining characteristics:

1. Random selection of the sample items
2. The use of probability theory to evaluate results, including measuring sampling risk

**Nonstatistical sampling** relies on the auditor's professional judgment for sample selection and evaluation. While nonstatistical methods can be effective, they do not allow the auditor to quantify sampling risk.

Both approaches can provide sufficient appropriate evidence. Neither is required by auditing standards — the choice is a matter of professional judgment. However, all samples (statistical or not) must be representative of the population.

### Sample Selection Methods

| Method | Description | Type |
|--------|-------------|------|
| Random selection | Every item has a known, nonzero probability of selection | Statistical |
| Systematic selection | Every nth item, with a random start | Can be statistical |
| Haphazard selection | Without any conscious bias, but not mathematically random | Nonstatistical |
| Block selection | Consecutive items (e.g., all March transactions) | Nonstatistical |

Block selection is generally discouraged because a single block may not represent the full population.

## Sampling Risk

**Sampling risk** is the risk that the auditor's conclusion based on a sample differs from the conclusion that would be reached if the entire population were tested. It exists whenever the auditor examines less than 100% of the population.

There are four types of sampling risk, separated by the type of testing:

### For Tests of Controls

- **Risk of assessing control risk too low** (risk of overreliance) — The sample suggests controls are effective when they are not. This affects the **effectiveness** of the audit because the auditor may rely on controls that are actually weak, leading to insufficient substantive testing.
- **Risk of assessing control risk too high** (risk of underreliance) — The sample suggests controls are ineffective when they actually work well. This affects the **efficiency** of the audit because the auditor performs more substantive testing than necessary.

### For Substantive Tests of Details

- **Risk of incorrect acceptance** — The sample suggests an account balance is not materially misstated when it actually is. This affects **effectiveness** and is the more dangerous error.
- **Risk of incorrect rejection** — The sample suggests an account balance is materially misstated when it is not. This affects **efficiency** because the auditor performs additional procedures unnecessarily.

> Focus on the effectiveness risks: risk of overreliance and risk of incorrect acceptance. These are the risks that can lead to an inappropriate audit opinion and are tested more frequently on the exam.

**Nonsampling risk** is the risk of error from factors unrelated to sampling, such as using inappropriate procedures, failing to recognize a misstatement in a document, or misinterpreting results. Nonsampling risk cannot be measured mathematically and is reduced through proper training, supervision, and quality control.

## Attributes Sampling

**Attributes sampling** is used for **tests of controls**. The auditor is testing a binary question: did the control operate as designed (yes or no)?

### Key Parameters

- **Expected deviation rate** — The rate of control deviations the auditor expects to find in the population. Based on prior experience or a preliminary sample.
- **Tolerable deviation rate** — The maximum rate of deviation from a control that the auditor is willing to accept and still rely on the control. Typically set between 2% and 7%.
- **Allowable risk of overreliance** — The risk the auditor is willing to accept that the sample results will support reliance when the true deviation rate exceeds the tolerable rate.

### How It Works

1. Define the control and the deviation (what constitutes a failure)
2. Define the population and sampling unit
3. Determine the sample size based on the three parameters above
4. Select the sample randomly
5. Test each item for deviations
6. Calculate the **sample deviation rate** (deviations found / sample size)
7. Evaluate whether the sample deviation rate supports reliance on the control

**Sample size relationships:**

| Factor | Effect on Sample Size |
|--------|----------------------|
| Higher expected deviation rate | Larger sample |
| Lower tolerable deviation rate | Larger sample |
| Lower allowable risk of overreliance | Larger sample |
| Larger population | Minimal effect (except very small populations) |

If the sample deviation rate exceeds the tolerable rate, the auditor cannot rely on the control and must revise the audit approach — typically by increasing substantive testing.

## Variables Sampling

**Variables sampling** is used for **substantive tests of details**. The auditor is estimating a monetary amount or testing whether an account balance is materially misstated.

### Common Methods

**Classical variables sampling** methods include:

- **Mean-per-unit estimation** — Estimates the total population value by multiplying the average value of the sample items by the number of items in the population.
- **Difference estimation** — Estimates the total population misstatement by multiplying the average difference between audited and book values in the sample by the population size. Requires that differences exist in the sample.
- **Ratio estimation** — Uses the ratio of audited values to book values in the sample to estimate the total audited value of the population. Works well when misstatements are proportional to book values.

**Monetary-unit sampling (MUS)** — also called **probability-proportional-to-size (PPS)** sampling — is a widely used approach that combines attributes and variables sampling concepts:

- The sampling unit is each individual dollar in the population
- Larger account balances have a greater probability of selection
- Particularly effective for testing for overstatement
- Does not require the auditor to estimate the standard deviation of the population

> MUS is especially popular for accounts receivable and inventory testing because it naturally emphasizes larger balances. It is less effective for testing understatement because items with zero or small recorded balances have little chance of selection.

## Evaluating Sample Results

After completing the testing, the auditor projects the sample results to the population:

1. **Calculate the projected misstatement** — Extrapolate the misstatements found in the sample to the population
2. **Consider sampling risk** — Add an allowance for sampling risk to the projected misstatement
3. **Compare to tolerable misstatement** — If the projected misstatement plus the allowance for sampling risk exceeds the tolerable misstatement (performance materiality), the auditor concludes the population is materially misstated

If results are unacceptable, the auditor may:

- Increase the sample size
- Perform alternative procedures
- Request management to investigate and correct the misstatements
- Consider the impact on the audit opinion

## Key Terms

- **Sampling risk** — The risk that a sample-based conclusion differs from the conclusion that would result from testing the entire population
- **Attributes sampling** — A sampling method used for tests of controls that evaluates the rate of deviations from a prescribed control
- **Variables sampling** — A sampling method used for substantive testing that estimates monetary amounts or misstatements
- **Tolerable deviation rate** — The maximum rate of control deviations the auditor will accept while still relying on the control
- **Monetary-unit sampling** — A sampling approach where each dollar is a sampling unit, giving larger balances a higher probability of selection

## Practice Problems

**1.** An auditor uses attributes sampling to test a control and finds a sample deviation rate of 6%. The tolerable deviation rate was set at 5%. The auditor should most likely:

a) Conclude the control is operating effectively since the difference is only 1%
b) Increase the reliance on the control to reduce substantive testing
c) Not rely on the control and increase the planned substantive procedures
d) Expand the sample to reduce sampling risk to zero

**Answer:** c) When the sample deviation rate exceeds the tolerable deviation rate, the auditor cannot rely on the control. The appropriate response is to revise the audit strategy and increase substantive testing for the related assertions.

---

**2.** Which of the following sampling risks affects the effectiveness of the audit in substantive testing?

a) Risk of incorrect rejection
b) Risk of assessing control risk too high
c) Risk of incorrect acceptance
d) Risk of underreliance

**Answer:** c) Risk of incorrect acceptance means the auditor concludes a balance is not materially misstated when it is. This affects audit effectiveness because it could lead to an inappropriate opinion. The other options affect audit efficiency.

---

**3.** An auditor wants to test accounts receivable for overstatement and expects that misstatements, if any, will be in the larger balances. Which sampling method is most appropriate?

a) Mean-per-unit estimation
b) Block selection
c) Monetary-unit sampling
d) Haphazard selection

**Answer:** c) Monetary-unit sampling (probability-proportional-to-size) is ideal for testing overstatement because it weights selection toward larger dollar balances, giving them a higher probability of being included in the sample.
