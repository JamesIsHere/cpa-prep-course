# Risk Assessment

## Learning Objectives

- Understand the audit risk model and its components
- Identify and assess risks of material misstatement at the financial statement and assertion levels
- Recognize fraud risk factors and the auditor's responsibilities regarding fraud
- Describe how risk assessment drives the design of further audit procedures

## The Audit Risk Model

**Audit risk** is the risk that the auditor expresses an inappropriate opinion when the financial statements are materially misstated. The audit risk model provides the conceptual framework for managing this risk:

**Audit Risk = Inherent Risk x Control Risk x Detection Risk**

Each component represents a distinct source of risk:

- **Inherent risk** — The susceptibility of an assertion to material misstatement, assuming no related controls exist. Complex estimates, for example, have higher inherent risk than simple cash transactions.
- **Control risk** — The risk that a material misstatement will not be prevented or detected on a timely basis by the entity's internal controls.
- **Detection risk** — The risk that the auditor's procedures will fail to detect a material misstatement. This is the only component the auditor directly controls.

The auditor's goal is to reduce audit risk to an **acceptably low level**. Since inherent risk and control risk are characteristics of the client (not within the auditor's control), the auditor manages audit risk by adjusting detection risk — which means adjusting the nature, timing, and extent of audit procedures.

> The inverse relationship between the assessed risk of material misstatement (inherent risk x control risk) and detection risk is a critical exam concept. When the assessed risk is high, the auditor must reduce detection risk by performing more effective and extensive procedures.

## Identifying Risks of Material Misstatement

Risk assessment is an ongoing process throughout the audit. The auditor identifies risks at two levels:

### Financial Statement Level

Financial statement-level risks are pervasive — they affect the financial statements as a whole rather than specific accounts. Examples include:

- Management's lack of integrity
- Weak control environment or poor tone at the top
- Going concern uncertainties
- Significant economic downturns affecting the entity
- Complex organizational structures designed to obscure ownership

These risks often require an overall response, such as assigning more experienced staff, increasing the level of supervision, or incorporating additional unpredictability into audit procedures.

### Assertion Level

Assertion-level risks relate to specific classes of transactions, account balances, or disclosures. Assertions are the representations embedded in the financial statements. The key assertions include:

**For classes of transactions:**
- **Occurrence** — Transactions actually occurred
- **Completeness** — All transactions are recorded
- **Accuracy** — Transactions are recorded at correct amounts
- **Cutoff** — Transactions are recorded in the correct period
- **Classification** — Transactions are recorded in the proper accounts

**For account balances:**
- **Existence** — Assets and liabilities exist
- **Rights and obligations** — The entity holds rights to assets and has obligations for liabilities
- **Completeness** — All items that should be recorded are recorded
- **Valuation and allocation** — Amounts are recorded at appropriate values

**For disclosures:**
- **Occurrence and rights and obligations** — Disclosed events occurred and pertain to the entity
- **Completeness** — All required disclosures are included
- **Classification and understandability** — Information is clearly presented
- **Accuracy and valuation** — Amounts and descriptions are appropriate

## Significant Risks

Certain risks warrant special audit consideration. A **significant risk** is an identified risk of material misstatement that, in the auditor's judgment, requires special attention. Factors that may indicate a significant risk include:

- The risk involves fraud
- The risk relates to recent significant economic, accounting, or other developments
- The risk involves complex transactions
- The risk relates to related-party transactions
- The risk involves significant management judgment or estimates
- The risk relates to transactions outside the normal course of business

For significant risks, the auditor must:

1. Evaluate the design and implementation of related controls
2. Perform substantive procedures specifically responsive to the risk
3. Not rely solely on analytical procedures as substantive evidence

> Revenue recognition is presumed to be a fraud risk (and therefore a significant risk) on every audit unless the auditor documents why it is not applicable. This is one of the most commonly tested fraud-related concepts.

## Fraud Risk

The auditor has a responsibility to plan and perform the audit to obtain reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements are free from material misstatement, whether caused by **error** or **fraud**.

### Two Types of Fraud

- **Fraudulent financial reporting** — Intentional misstatements or omissions in financial statements (e.g., overstating revenue, understating liabilities)
- **Misappropriation of assets** — Theft of entity assets (e.g., embezzlement, payroll fraud, inventory theft)

### The Fraud Triangle

The **fraud triangle** identifies three conditions that are generally present when fraud occurs:

1. **Incentive/Pressure** — A reason to commit fraud (meeting earnings targets, personal financial difficulties)
2. **Opportunity** — Circumstances that allow fraud to occur (weak controls, override of controls by management)
3. **Rationalization** — The ability to justify the fraudulent behavior ("I'll pay it back," "The company owes me")

### Required Fraud-Related Procedures

Regardless of the assessed risk, the auditor must:

- Make inquiries of management regarding fraud risks and management's fraud prevention programs
- Discuss fraud risks with the engagement team (brainstorming session)
- Evaluate whether analytical procedures indicate previously unrecognized fraud risks
- Consider the risk of management override of controls — this is always a presumed risk and cannot be reduced to below significant

To address management override, the auditor must:

- Test journal entries and other adjustments for appropriateness
- Review accounting estimates for bias
- Evaluate the business rationale for significant unusual transactions

## Responding to Assessed Risks

Once risks are identified and assessed, the auditor designs **further audit procedures** whose nature, timing, and extent are responsive to those risks.

| Assessed Risk Level | Response |
|---------------------|----------|
| Higher risk | More reliable evidence, larger samples, testing closer to year-end, more experienced staff |
| Lower risk | May accept less persuasive evidence, smaller samples, interim testing may be acceptable |

The auditor's responses should have a clear linkage to the assessed risks. This connection between risk assessment and the planned procedures is sometimes called the **audit risk response** and is a frequent exam topic.

## Key Terms

- **Inherent risk** — The susceptibility of an assertion to material misstatement before considering internal controls
- **Control risk** — The risk that internal controls will not prevent or detect a material misstatement
- **Detection risk** — The risk that audit procedures will not detect a material misstatement
- **Significant risk** — A risk of material misstatement that requires special audit attention due to its nature or magnitude
- **Fraud triangle** — The three conditions (incentive, opportunity, rationalization) commonly present when fraud occurs
- **Assertion** — A representation by management embedded in the financial statements about recognition, measurement, presentation, or disclosure
