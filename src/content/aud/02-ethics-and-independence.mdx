# Ethics and Independence

## Learning Objectives

- Understand the structure and key principles of the AICPA Code of Professional Conduct
- Apply the conceptual framework for evaluating threats to independence
- Distinguish between independence in mind and independence in appearance
- Identify common scenarios that impair auditor independence

## The AICPA Code of Professional Conduct

The **AICPA Code of Professional Conduct** is the ethical foundation for all CPAs. It establishes the standards of behavior that the profession demands, and it is one of the most heavily tested topics on the AUD exam.

The Code is organized into three parts:

1. **Part 1** — Members in Public Practice (auditors, tax practitioners, consultants)
2. **Part 2** — Members in Business (CPAs working in industry)
3. **Part 3** — Other Members (retired, between jobs, etc.)

Each part contains rules organized around six core principles:

- **Responsibilities** — Exercise professional judgment in all activities
- **The Public Interest** — Act in a way that serves the public trust
- **Integrity** — Be honest and candid within the constraints of confidentiality
- **Objectivity and Independence** — Maintain impartiality and freedom from conflicts of interest
- **Due Care** — Strive for competence and diligence in professional services
- **Scope and Nature of Services** — Consider whether services might create conflicts with audit obligations

> The exam frequently tests your ability to apply these principles to fact patterns rather than simply recite them. Focus on understanding how the principles interact in real-world scenarios.

## Independence: The Core Requirement

**Independence** is the hallmark of the auditing profession. Without it, the auditor's opinion carries no credibility. The Code distinguishes between two dimensions:

- **Independence in mind** — The state of mind that permits an auditor to act with integrity, objectivity, and professional skepticism. This is the auditor's actual ability to be unbiased.
- **Independence in appearance** — The avoidance of circumstances that would cause a reasonable and informed third party to conclude that the auditor's integrity, objectivity, or skepticism has been compromised.

Both dimensions must be satisfied. An auditor who is personally unbiased but has a financial relationship with the client that looks problematic to outsiders has failed the appearance test.

### Common Independence Impairments

Several categories of relationships and interests can impair independence:

**Financial interests** — Owning stock in an audit client, whether directly or indirectly, is the most straightforward impairment. A **direct financial interest** in an attest client (any amount) is always an impairment. An **indirect financial interest** (such as a mutual fund that holds client stock) is only an impairment if it is material to the CPA.

**Business relationships** — Serving as a director or officer of an audit client, or having a joint business venture with the client, impairs independence.

**Family relationships** — The independence rules extend to certain family members. A **covered member's** immediate family (spouse and dependents) is subject to the same restrictions. Close relatives are subject to a narrower set of rules.

**Employment relationships** — If a partner on the engagement team has a spouse who works in a key position at the audit client, independence is impaired.

> A **covered member** is any individual on the attest engagement team, anyone in a position to influence the engagement, or a partner in the office where the lead engagement partner practices. This definition comes up repeatedly on the exam.

## The Conceptual Framework Approach

Not every situation is addressed by a specific rule. When the Code does not have a bright-line prohibition, CPAs must use the **conceptual framework** to evaluate threats and apply safeguards.

The framework follows three steps:

1. **Identify threats** to independence or compliance with the rules
2. **Evaluate** whether the threats are at an acceptable level
3. **Apply safeguards** to reduce threats to an acceptable level, or decline the engagement if safeguards are insufficient

### Categories of Threats

The Code identifies seven categories of threats:

| Threat | Description | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| Self-interest | Financial or other interest could influence judgment | CPA owns stock in client |
| Self-review | Auditing your own work product | CPA prepares client's financial statements then audits them |
| Advocacy | Promoting a client's position | CPA lobbies on behalf of an audit client |
| Familiarity | Close relationship clouds objectivity | Long-tenured engagement partner becomes too trusting of client management |
| Undue influence | Pressure from client or third party | Client threatens to change auditors if the CPA doesn't agree with their position |
| Management participation | Acting in a management role for the client | CPA makes operating decisions for the client |
| Intimidation | Being deterred from acting objectively through threats | Client management threatens litigation against the auditor |

### Safeguards

Safeguards are controls that reduce threats to an acceptable level. Examples include:

- Removing a team member who has a conflict
- Engaging an independent partner to review the work
- Rotating engagement personnel
- Having the audit committee pre-approve non-audit services

If no combination of safeguards can reduce the threat to an acceptable level, the CPA must decline or withdraw from the engagement.

## Non-Audit Services and Independence

Providing **non-audit services** to an audit client is one of the most nuanced areas on the exam. The general principle is that a CPA can provide certain non-audit services without impairing independence, provided that:

1. The CPA does not assume a **management responsibility** (making decisions, executing transactions, having custody of assets)
2. The client agrees to **oversee** the service and accept responsibility for results
3. The CPA's work does not become the subject of the audit engagement in a self-review situation

Common non-audit services tested on the exam include tax compliance, bookkeeping assistance, and IT consulting. The key question is always whether the CPA is performing a management function or merely providing advice and information for management's use.

## SEC and PCAOB Independence Requirements

When auditing **public companies (issuers)**, the auditor must comply with stricter independence rules imposed by the **SEC** and **PCAOB**, in addition to the AICPA Code.

### Key SEC Independence Rules

The SEC's independence rules (Regulation S-X, Rule 2-01) apply to auditors of public companies and include several provisions that go beyond AICPA requirements:

**Prohibited non-audit services** — The following services are prohibited for audit clients that are SEC registrants:
- Bookkeeping and related services
- Financial information systems design and implementation
- Appraisal or valuation services, fairness opinions, or contribution-in-kind reports
- Actuarial services
- Internal audit outsourcing
- Management functions or human resources
- Broker-dealer, investment adviser, or investment banking services
- Legal services unrelated to the audit
- Expert services unrelated to the audit

**Audit committee pre-approval** — All audit and permitted non-audit services must be pre-approved by the audit committee. There is a limited exception for de minimis non-audit services (under 5% of total fees paid to the auditor) if they are promptly brought to the committee's attention and approved before the audit is completed.

### SOX Partner Rotation Requirements

The **Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX)** requires mandatory rotation of certain key audit partners for public company engagements:

- The **lead engagement partner** and the **concurring review partner** must rotate off the engagement after **five consecutive years**
- After rotation, a **five-year cooling-off period** is required before the partner can return to that engagement
- Other significant audit partners (such as those responsible for major subsidiaries) must rotate after **seven consecutive years** with a **two-year cooling-off period**

> The AICPA has its own rotation requirements for non-issuers, but they are less restrictive. For the exam, know both the SOX requirements (issuers) and the AICPA requirements (non-issuers).

### Cooling-Off Periods for Employment

When a former member of the audit team accepts employment with the audit client, both the SEC and AICPA impose cooling-off requirements:

- **SEC rule:** A one-year cooling-off period is required before a former engagement team member can serve in a **financial reporting oversight role** (FROR) at the audit client. This applies to the lead partner, concurring partner, and any partner providing more than 10 hours of service.
- **AICPA rule:** The firm must evaluate threats created by a former team member joining the client. If the individual was a partner, the firm's independence may be impaired if the former partner has significant ongoing interaction with the audit team.

A **financial reporting oversight role** includes positions such as CFO, controller, chief accounting officer, and anyone who has direct influence over the preparation of the financial statements.

## Disciplinary Actions

Violations of the Code can result in disciplinary proceedings through:

- **AICPA** — Can suspend or terminate membership
- **State Boards of Accountancy** — Can revoke or suspend CPA licenses
- **SEC and PCAOB** — Can impose sanctions for violations involving public companies

A CPA's license can be automatically revoked upon conviction of certain crimes (typically felonies or crimes involving moral turpitude) without a hearing.

## Key Terms

- **Independence in mind** — The actual objectivity and impartiality of the auditor's mental state
- **Independence in appearance** — The perception of independence by a reasonable and informed third party
- **Covered member** — An individual on the attest engagement team, someone who can influence the engagement, or a partner in the lead partner's office
- **Conceptual framework** — A principles-based approach to evaluating threats and applying safeguards when specific rules do not address a situation
- **Management responsibility** — A function that involves making decisions, directing activities, or taking actions that are properly the role of management
- **Safeguard** — A control or action that reduces a threat to independence to an acceptable level
