# Government Auditing Standards

## Learning Objectives

- Understand the purpose and scope of Government Auditing Standards (Yellow Book)
- Identify the key differences between GAGAS and GAAS
- Explain the Single Audit Act and Uniform Guidance requirements
- Describe the types of government audits and additional reporting requirements
- Recognize the independence requirements unique to government auditing

## Overview of Government Auditing Standards

**Government Auditing Standards** (GAS), commonly known as the **Yellow Book**, are issued by the U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO). These standards apply to audits of government organizations, entities receiving government awards, and other entities as required by law or regulation. The current revision, effective since 2018 (with a 2024 technical update), establishes the framework for conducting high-quality audits with competence, integrity, objectivity, and independence.

GAGAS build upon generally accepted auditing standards (GAAS) but impose **additional requirements** in several areas. When an audit is conducted under GAGAS, the auditor must comply with both GAAS and the supplementary GAGAS requirements. The Yellow Book does not replace GAAS — it layers on top of it.

There are three categories of GAGAS engagements:

1. **Financial audits** — audits of financial statements, which follow AICPA GAAS plus additional GAGAS requirements
2. **Attestation engagements** — examinations, reviews, or agreed-upon procedures on subject matter other than financial statements
3. **Performance audits** — evaluations of program effectiveness, economy, and efficiency (unique to GAGAS; no equivalent under GAAS)

> The CPA exam frequently tests the distinction between financial audits and performance audits under GAGAS. Financial audits follow GAAS plus GAGAS supplements. Performance audits are unique to the Yellow Book and evaluate whether programs are achieving intended objectives.

## GAGAS vs. GAAS: Key Differences

While GAGAS incorporates GAAS for financial audits, it imposes significant additional requirements:

### Independence

GAGAS independence requirements are **more restrictive** than AICPA standards. Key differences:

- **Conceptual framework approach** — Like AICPA standards, GAGAS uses a threats-and-safeguards approach, but certain nonaudit services that the AICPA permits are **prohibited** under GAGAS
- **Prohibited nonaudit services** — Under GAGAS, the auditor may not perform management functions for the audited entity. This includes preparing financial statements from a client's trial balance (which GAAS permits for non-issuers under certain conditions), maintaining the entity's basic accounting records, and performing management's internal control monitoring
- **Structural threats** — GAGAS specifically addresses threats arising from the organizational placement of auditors (e.g., an internal audit function that reports to the entity being audited)
- **Documentation** — GAGAS requires the auditor to document the consideration of independence, including any threats identified and safeguards applied, in the audit documentation

### Continuing Professional Education (CPE)

GAGAS imposes specific CPE requirements:

- Auditors performing work under GAGAS must complete **80 hours of CPE** every two years
- Of the 80 hours, **24 hours** must be directly related to the government environment, government auditing, or the specific environment in which the audited entity operates
- At least **20 hours** must be completed in each year of the two-year period

### Additional Reporting Requirements

GAGAS financial audits require additional reports beyond the standard GAAS audit report:

- A report on **internal control over financial reporting** — The auditor must report any significant deficiencies and material weaknesses identified during the audit, even if not performing an integrated audit
- A report on **compliance with provisions of laws, regulations, contracts, and grant agreements** — The auditor must test for and report on noncompliance that could have a material effect on the financial statements
- These reports may be combined with the opinion on the financial statements or issued separately

> Remember: Under GAAS alone, the auditor communicates internal control deficiencies in writing to management and those charged with governance but does not issue a separate report. Under GAGAS, a formal report on internal controls is required, and it accompanies the audit opinion.

## The Single Audit Act and Uniform Guidance

### Single Audit Overview

The **Single Audit Act** (originally 1984, amended 1996) requires non-federal entities that expend **$750,000 or more in federal awards** in a fiscal year to have a single audit or a program-specific audit. The **Uniform Guidance** (2 CFR Part 200) consolidates the requirements for federal awards, including audit requirements, into a single framework.

The single audit concept replaced the previous approach of multiple program-specific audits by different auditors. Instead, a single auditor performs a comprehensive audit that covers:

1. The entity's financial statements as a whole (the financial statement audit)
2. Federal award compliance (the compliance audit of major programs)

### Determining Major Programs

Not every federal program receives detailed compliance testing. The auditor identifies **major programs** using a risk-based approach:

- **Step 1** — Determine the **Type A/Type B threshold**. Type A programs are those exceeding the larger of $750,000 or 3% of total federal expenditures (the threshold scales up for larger recipients)
- **Step 2** — Assess risk for each Type A program (low risk or high risk based on prior audit results, oversight, and control effectiveness)
- **Step 3** — Identify high-risk Type B programs that should be audited as major programs
- **Step 4** — Select major programs: all high-risk Type A programs plus enough additional programs to meet the percentage-of-coverage requirement (generally 20% of total federal expenditures for low-risk auditees, 40% for others)

### Compliance Requirements

For each major program, the auditor tests compliance with the following categories from the **Compliance Supplement**:

| Category | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| A — Activities Allowed/Unallowed | Were costs charged to the program allowable? |
| B — Allowable Costs/Cost Principles | Were costs computed correctly per cost principles? |
| C — Cash Management | Was the entity in compliance with cash management requirements? |
| E — Eligibility | Did participants/beneficiaries meet eligibility criteria? |
| F — Equipment/Real Property | Were equipment purchases and management compliant? |
| G — Matching/Level of Effort/Earmarking | Were matching and earmarking requirements met? |
| H — Period of Performance | Were costs incurred within the authorized period? |
| I — Procurement/Suspension/Debarment | Did the entity follow procurement standards? |
| L — Reporting | Were required reports submitted accurately and on time? |
| N — Special Tests and Provisions | Were program-specific requirements met? |

> Not all compliance categories apply to every program. The Compliance Supplement identifies which categories are relevant for each specific federal program.

### Worked Example: Determining Single Audit Requirement

**Facts:** The City of Westfield received the following federal awards during fiscal year 2025:
- Community Development Block Grant (CDBG): $425,000
- FEMA Disaster Relief: $180,000
- EPA Clean Water State Revolving Fund: $200,000

**Analysis:**
- Total federal expenditures: $425,000 + $180,000 + $200,000 = **$805,000**
- Since $805,000 exceeds the **$750,000 threshold**, the City of Westfield is required to have a single audit
- The Type A threshold is the larger of $750,000 or 3% of $805,000 ($24,150). The threshold is $750,000
- The CDBG program is not a Type A program ($425,000 < $750,000), so all programs are Type B. However, the auditor must still identify major programs using the risk-based approach to meet the coverage requirement

## Types of Government Audits

### Financial Audits

These follow the same structure as GAAS audits but include the additional GAGAS reporting requirements. The auditor expresses an opinion on the financial statements and issues supplemental reports on internal controls and compliance.

### Performance Audits

**Performance audits** are unique to GAGAS and do not exist under AAS. They evaluate:

- **Effectiveness** — Is the program achieving its intended goals and objectives?
- **Economy and efficiency** — Are resources being used efficiently? Could the same results be achieved at lower cost?
- **Internal control** — Are controls adequate to provide reasonable assurance of achieving program objectives?
- **Compliance** — Is the entity complying with applicable laws and regulations?

Performance audit reports include **findings** with four elements:

1. **Condition** — What exists (the current state)
2. **Criteria** — What should exist (the standard or benchmark)
3. **Cause** — Why the condition differs from the criteria
4. **Effect** — The consequence or impact of the condition

### Attestation Engagements

GAGAS attestation engagements follow the AICPA SSAE standards with additional GAGAS requirements. Common government attestation engagements include:

- Examinations of compliance with specific program requirements
- Agreed-upon procedures on grant expenditures
- Examinations of internal controls at government entities

## Single Audit Reporting Package

The single audit report package submitted to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse includes:

1. The **financial statements** and auditor's opinion
2. **Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards (SEFA)** — A supplementary schedule listing all federal awards expended during the period
3. **Auditor's report on internal control and compliance** required by GAGAS
4. **Auditor's report on compliance and internal control over compliance for major programs** — This includes the auditor's opinion on whether the entity complied with requirements applicable to each major program
5. **Schedule of findings and questioned costs** — Includes a summary of the auditor's results, findings related to the financial statements, and findings related to federal awards
6. **Data collection form** (SF-SAC) — Summary of single audit results submitted to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse

### Audit Findings in the Single Audit

When the auditor identifies findings related to federal awards, each finding must include:

- The federal program and CFDA number
- The compliance requirement involved
- The condition, criteria, cause, and effect
- The auditor's recommendation
- The auditee's perspective (response)
- **Questioned costs** — dollar amounts that the auditor questions as potentially unallowable, undocumented, or unreasonable

The auditee must prepare a **corrective action plan** for each finding and submit it with the reporting package.

> The reporting package must be submitted to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse within the earlier of 30 days after receiving the auditor's report or 9 months after the end of the fiscal year. This deadline is a frequently tested detail.

## Key Terms

- **Yellow Book** — The common name for Government Auditing Standards issued by the GAO
- **GAGAS** — Generally Accepted Government Auditing Standards, the standards contained in the Yellow Book
- **Single Audit** — A comprehensive audit required for non-federal entities expending $750,000 or more in federal awards
- **Uniform Guidance** — 2 CFR Part 200, which consolidates federal requirements for grants and audit requirements
- **Performance audit** — A GAGAS-specific audit type evaluating program effectiveness, economy, and efficiency
- **Major program** — A federal program selected for detailed compliance testing based on a risk-based approach
- **Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards (SEFA)** — A supplementary schedule listing all federal awards expended during the audit period
- **Questioned costs** — Expenditures identified during the audit as potentially unallowable, undocumented, or unreasonable
- **Compliance Supplement** — A reference document identifying the compliance requirements applicable to each federal program

## Practice Problems

**1.** A non-federal entity expended $600,000 in federal awards during the fiscal year. Which of the following is correct regarding the single audit requirement?

a) The entity must have a single audit because it received federal funding
b) The entity is not required to have a single audit because expenditures are below the $750,000 threshold
c) The entity must have a program-specific audit for each federal award
d) The entity may elect to have a single audit even though it is not required

**Answer:** b) Under the Uniform Guidance, a single audit is required only when an entity expends $750,000 or more in federal awards in a fiscal year. At $600,000, the entity is below the threshold and is not required to have a single audit, though it may still be subject to other audit requirements.

---

**2.** Which of the following is an additional requirement under GAGAS that is NOT required under GAAS alone?

a) Obtaining an understanding of internal control
b) Issuing a separate report on compliance with laws and regulations
c) Using professional skepticism during the audit
d) Obtaining a management representation letter

**Answer:** b) GAGAS requires a separate report on the entity's compliance with laws, regulations, contracts, and grant agreements. Under GAAS alone, the auditor considers laws and regulations but does not issue a separate compliance report. The other items are standard GAAS requirements.

---

**3.** A performance audit finding should include all of the following elements EXCEPT:

a) Condition
b) Criteria
c) Materiality threshold
d) Cause and effect

**Answer:** c) Performance audit findings include condition (what exists), criteria (what should exist), cause (why the difference exists), and effect (the impact). Materiality threshold is a financial audit concept and is not one of the elements of a performance audit finding.
