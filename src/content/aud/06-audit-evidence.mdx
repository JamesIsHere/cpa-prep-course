# Audit Evidence

## Learning Objectives

- Understand the concepts of sufficiency and appropriateness of audit evidence
- Identify the types and sources of audit evidence
- Describe the audit procedures used to obtain evidence
- Evaluate the reliability of different forms of evidence

## Sufficiency and Appropriateness

All audit conclusions rest on **audit evidence** — the information the auditor obtains to draw reasonable conclusions and form an opinion. Two qualities determine whether the auditor has gathered enough of the right evidence:

**Sufficiency** is the measure of the **quantity** of audit evidence. The amount needed depends on the assessed risk of material misstatement and the quality of the evidence obtained. Higher risk requires more evidence. Higher-quality evidence may allow less of it.

**Appropriateness** is the measure of the **quality** of audit evidence — its relevance and reliability. Evidence must be relevant to the assertion being tested and reliable enough to support the auditor's conclusion.

These two qualities work together. A large quantity of low-quality evidence does not compensate for a lack of relevance, and a single highly reliable piece of evidence may not be sufficient on its own.

> The exam frequently tests the interplay between sufficiency and appropriateness. Remember: more evidence is needed when risk is higher or when the evidence obtained is of lower quality.

## Reliability of Audit Evidence

Not all evidence is equally reliable. Several factors affect reliability:

| Factor | More Reliable | Less Reliable |
|--------|--------------|---------------|
| Source | External (third-party confirmations) | Internal (documents prepared by the client) |
| Nature | Documentary | Oral |
| Directness | Obtained directly by the auditor | Obtained indirectly or provided by the client |
| Controls | Generated under effective internal controls | Generated under weak internal controls |
| Original vs. copy | Original documents | Photocopies or faxes |

**External evidence** (such as a bank confirmation) is generally more reliable than **internal evidence** (such as a client-prepared bank reconciliation). However, internal evidence generated under effective controls can be reasonably reliable.

**Direct evidence** obtained by the auditor (such as personally observing inventory) is more reliable than information provided by others.

### The Hierarchy in Practice

Consider testing the existence of cash. The auditor could:

1. **Inspect a bank statement** — Internal evidence, moderate reliability
2. **Obtain a bank confirmation** — External evidence obtained directly by the auditor, higher reliability
3. **Accept management's representation** — Internal, oral evidence, lowest reliability

A well-planned audit uses a combination of evidence types that, taken together, provide sufficient appropriate evidence.

## Types of Audit Procedures

Auditors use specific procedures to obtain evidence. Each procedure serves a different purpose and has different strengths:

### Inspection

**Inspection** involves examining records, documents, or tangible assets. Inspecting documents provides evidence about the information contained in them. Inspecting physical assets provides evidence about existence but not necessarily about ownership or valuation.

- Inspecting an invoice supports occurrence and accuracy
- Inspecting inventory supports existence
- Inspecting a signed contract supports rights and obligations

### Observation

**Observation** involves watching a process or procedure being performed by others. It provides evidence about the performance of the activity at the point in time it is observed, but does not provide evidence that the activity is performed consistently at other times.

- Observing a physical inventory count
- Observing the segregation of duties in the cash receipts process

### Inquiry

**Inquiry** involves seeking information from knowledgeable persons, both financial and nonfinancial, inside or outside the entity. Inquiries can be formal written requests or informal oral questions.

Inquiry alone generally does not provide sufficient evidence to support an assertion or to evaluate operating effectiveness of controls. It must be corroborated with other procedures.

### Confirmation

**Confirmation** is a specific type of inquiry — it involves obtaining a direct written response from a third party. Confirmations are external evidence and are among the most reliable forms.

Common confirmations include:

- **Bank confirmations** — Verify cash balances and loan details
- **Accounts receivable confirmations** — Verify amounts owed by customers
- **Legal confirmations** — Obtain information about pending litigation from the client's attorneys

Two types of confirmation requests exist:

- **Positive confirmations** — Ask the respondent to reply whether they agree or disagree with the stated amount. Used when individual balances are large or when the auditor expects disagreements.
- **Negative confirmations** — Ask the respondent to reply only if they disagree. Used when the assessed risk is low, there are many small balances, and the auditor expects few exceptions.

> Positive confirmations provide more reliable evidence than negative confirmations because the auditor receives an explicit response. With negative confirmations, the absence of a reply could mean agreement or simply that the respondent ignored the request.

### External Confirmation Process

The auditor must maintain control over the confirmation process:

1. Determine the information to be confirmed
2. Select the appropriate parties to confirm with
3. Design the confirmation request
4. **Send the request directly** to the confirming party (not through the client)
5. Evaluate the responses and follow up on exceptions and non-responses

If management refuses to allow the auditor to send confirmations, the auditor should consider the reasons, evaluate the impact on risk assessment, and perform alternative procedures. If the refusal is unreasonable, it may constitute a scope limitation.

### Recalculation

**Recalculation** involves checking the mathematical accuracy of documents or records. This is a highly reliable procedure because the auditor independently verifies the computation.

- Recalculating depreciation expense
- Footing a trial balance
- Verifying the mathematical accuracy of an inventory listing

### Reperformance

**Reperformance** involves the auditor independently executing procedures or controls that were originally performed by the entity. It is particularly useful for testing operating effectiveness of controls.

### Analytical Procedures

**Analytical procedures** involve evaluating financial information through analysis of plausible relationships among financial and nonfinancial data. They are used at three stages of the audit:

1. **Planning** — Required. Used to identify areas of potential risk.
2. **Substantive testing** — Optional. Used to obtain evidence about specific assertions.
3. **Overall review** — Required. Used near the end of the audit to assess whether the financial statements are consistent with the auditor's understanding.

## Management Representations

Near the end of every audit, the auditor obtains a **management representation letter** — a written statement from management confirming certain matters and supporting other audit evidence. The letter typically covers:

- Management's responsibility for the financial statements
- Completeness of information provided to the auditor
- The effects of uncorrected misstatements
- Information about fraud or suspected fraud
- Subsequent events
- Related-party transactions

The representation letter is dated as of the date of the auditor's report and is signed by the CEO and CFO (or equivalents). It is required — refusal to provide it constitutes a scope limitation that results in a disclaimer of opinion.

## Key Terms

- **Sufficiency** — The quantity of audit evidence, influenced by risk assessment and evidence quality
- **Appropriateness** — The quality (relevance and reliability) of audit evidence
- **Positive confirmation** — A request for the third party to respond whether they agree or disagree with stated information
- **Negative confirmation** — A request for the third party to respond only if they disagree with stated information
- **Analytical procedures** — Evaluations of financial data through analysis of plausible relationships among financial and nonfinancial information
- **Management representation letter** — A written statement from management required on every audit, confirming key matters and supporting audit evidence

## Practice Problems

**1.** Which of the following types of audit evidence is generally considered the most reliable?

a) A client-prepared accounts receivable aging schedule
b) Oral inquiry of the controller regarding revenue recognition policies
c) A positive confirmation reply received directly from a customer
d) An internal memo documenting management's estimate methodology

**Answer:** c) External evidence obtained directly by the auditor is the most reliable. A positive confirmation from a third party provides direct, independent verification of the account balance.

---

**2.** Analytical procedures are required at which stage(s) of the audit?

a) Planning only
b) Substantive testing only
c) Planning and overall review
d) All three stages: planning, substantive testing, and overall review

**Answer:** c) Analytical procedures are required during planning and during the overall review near the end of the audit. They are optional during substantive testing, where they may be used to obtain evidence about specific assertions.

---

**3.** If management refuses to provide a representation letter, the auditor should:

a) Issue a qualified opinion
b) Issue a disclaimer of opinion
c) Issue an adverse opinion
d) Withdraw from the engagement without issuing a report

**Answer:** b) The management representation letter is required on every audit. Refusal to provide it is a scope limitation serious enough to warrant a disclaimer of opinion, because the auditor cannot obtain sufficient appropriate evidence.
