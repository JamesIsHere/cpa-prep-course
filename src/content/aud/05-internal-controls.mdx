# Internal Controls

## Learning Objectives

- Explain the components of internal control under the COSO framework
- Describe the auditor's responsibility to understand and evaluate internal controls
- Distinguish between testing the design, implementation, and operating effectiveness of controls
- Understand how control deficiencies are evaluated and communicated

## What Are Internal Controls?

**Internal controls** are the processes designed and implemented by management to provide reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives in three categories:

1. **Reliability of financial reporting** — Financial statements are prepared in accordance with the applicable framework
2. **Effectiveness and efficiency of operations** — The entity's resources are used as intended
3. **Compliance with laws and regulations** — The entity adheres to applicable legal requirements

For auditing purposes, the auditor is primarily concerned with controls relevant to the preparation of financial statements — those that affect the risk of material misstatement.

## The COSO Framework

The **Committee of Sponsoring Organizations (COSO)** Internal Control — Integrated Framework is the most widely recognized framework for internal controls and is the model used in auditing standards. It consists of five interrelated components:

### 1. Control Environment

The **control environment** sets the tone of the organization. It is the foundation for all other components and reflects management's commitment to integrity and ethical values. Key elements include:

- Management's philosophy and operating style
- The entity's commitment to competence
- Board of directors and audit committee participation
- Organizational structure and assignment of authority
- Human resource policies and practices

> The control environment is sometimes called "tone at the top." If the control environment is weak, other controls — no matter how well designed — are unlikely to function effectively. The exam frequently tests this concept.

### 2. Risk Assessment

The entity's own **risk assessment** process involves identifying and analyzing risks that could prevent the achievement of objectives. This includes:

- Identifying risks to reliable financial reporting
- Estimating the significance of risks
- Assessing the likelihood of occurrence
- Deciding on actions to address the risks

Changes in the business environment, new personnel, new systems, rapid growth, and new accounting standards can all introduce new risks.

### 3. Control Activities

**Control activities** are the policies and procedures that help ensure management directives are carried out. Common examples include:

- **Authorization** — Proper approval of transactions
- **Segregation of duties** — No single person controls all aspects of a transaction (authorization, recording, custody)
- **Physical controls** — Safeguarding assets through locks, restricted access, and periodic counts
- **Information processing controls** — Application controls (edit checks, matching) and general IT controls (access security, change management)
- **Performance reviews** — Management review of actual performance against budgets and expectations

### 4. Information and Communication

The **information and communication** component addresses how the entity captures and exchanges information needed to conduct, manage, and control its operations. The accounting information system is the primary focus, including:

- How transactions are initiated, recorded, processed, and reported
- How the entity communicates roles and responsibilities
- Communication channels between management, the board, and external parties

### 5. Monitoring Activities

**Monitoring** is the process that assesses the quality of internal control performance over time. It includes:

- Ongoing monitoring built into routine operations (supervisory review, reconciliations)
- Separate evaluations (internal audit assessments, self-assessments)
- Reporting and correcting deficiencies

## The Auditor's Responsibility

On every audit, the auditor is required to obtain an understanding of internal controls relevant to the audit. This understanding helps the auditor:

- Identify types of potential misstatements
- Consider factors that affect the risks of material misstatement
- Design the nature, timing, and extent of further audit procedures

### Understanding vs. Testing

It is important to distinguish between three levels of engagement with controls:

**Understanding the design** — Does the control, if operating as designed, effectively address the identified risk? The auditor evaluates this for all relevant controls.

**Evaluating implementation** — Has the control actually been put into operation? A well-designed control that exists only on paper provides no benefit. The auditor evaluates this for all relevant controls.

**Testing operating effectiveness** — Is the control working consistently over the period? The auditor only tests operating effectiveness when the audit strategy includes **reliance on controls** to reduce substantive testing.

### Deciding Whether to Test Controls

The auditor is not required to test the operating effectiveness of controls. However, there are two situations where testing is necessary:

1. **The auditor plans to rely on controls** — When the audit strategy assumes that controls are operating effectively, the auditor must test them to support that assumption.
2. **Substantive procedures alone are insufficient** — In some cases, transactions are processed with little or no manual intervention (highly automated systems), making it impractical to obtain sufficient evidence from substantive procedures alone. Control testing becomes essential.

### Methods for Testing Controls

Common procedures for testing the operating effectiveness of controls include:

- **Inquiry** — Asking personnel who perform the control about how it works (least persuasive on its own)
- **Observation** — Watching the control being performed
- **Inspection** — Examining documents or reports that provide evidence the control operated
- **Reperformance** — Independently executing the control procedure to verify it works

Inquiry alone is not sufficient to test operating effectiveness. The auditor must combine inquiry with at least one other procedure.

## Evaluating Control Deficiencies

When the auditor identifies a weakness in internal controls, the deficiency must be evaluated and classified:

**Control deficiency** — A control is designed, implemented, or operating in a way that does not allow management or employees to prevent or detect misstatements on a timely basis.

**Significant deficiency** — A deficiency, or combination of deficiencies, that is important enough to merit the attention of those charged with governance.

**Material weakness** — A deficiency, or combination of deficiencies, such that there is a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement will not be prevented or detected on a timely basis.

### Communication Requirements

The auditor must communicate the following in writing:

| Deficiency Level | Communicate To | Timing |
|------------------|----------------|--------|
| Material weakness | Management and those charged with governance | In writing, within 60 days of the report release date |
| Significant deficiency | Those charged with governance | In writing, within 60 days of the report release date |
| Other deficiencies | Management (optional) | Orally or in writing |

> The auditor is prohibited from issuing a written communication stating that no material weaknesses were identified during the audit. This is because the audit was not specifically designed to identify all control deficiencies.

## Key Terms

- **COSO framework** — The Committee of Sponsoring Organizations framework for evaluating internal controls, consisting of five components
- **Control environment** — The organizational culture, ethics, and governance structure that forms the foundation for all other internal control components
- **Segregation of duties** — The separation of authorization, recording, and custody functions to reduce the risk of error or fraud
- **Material weakness** — A control deficiency creating a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement will not be prevented or detected timely
- **Significant deficiency** — A control deficiency important enough to merit the attention of those charged with governance

## Practice Problems

**1.** Which component of the COSO framework is considered the foundation for all other components of internal control?

a) Control activities
b) Risk assessment
c) Control environment
d) Monitoring activities

**Answer:** c) The control environment, often referred to as "tone at the top," is the foundation. It encompasses management's integrity, ethical values, and governance structure, and influences the effectiveness of all other components.

---

**2.** An auditor identifies a control deficiency that creates a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement would not be prevented or detected on a timely basis. This deficiency is classified as:

a) A significant deficiency
b) A material weakness
c) An inherent limitation
d) A scope limitation

**Answer:** b) A material weakness is defined as a deficiency (or combination) where there is a reasonable possibility of a material misstatement not being prevented or detected timely. This must be communicated in writing to management and those charged with governance.

---

**3.** When testing the operating effectiveness of a control, the auditor should:

a) Rely solely on inquiry of personnel who perform the control
b) Combine inquiry with observation, inspection, or reperformance
c) Only use reperformance, as it is the most reliable technique
d) Test the control at a single point in time to confirm it operates

**Answer:** b) Inquiry alone is never sufficient to test operating effectiveness. The auditor must use inquiry combined with at least one other procedure — such as observation, inspection of documentation, or reperformance of the control.
