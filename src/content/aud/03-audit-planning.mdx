# Audit Planning

## Learning Objectives

- Describe the process of engagement acceptance and continuance
- Explain how auditors develop an understanding of the entity and its environment
- Understand how audit strategy and the audit plan are developed
- Apply the concept of materiality in planning an audit

## Engagement Acceptance and Continuance

Before any audit work begins, the firm must decide whether to accept a new client or continue with an existing one. This decision involves evaluating both the **client** and the **firm's own capabilities**.

### Predecessor and Successor Auditor Communications

When a new client is being considered, the **successor auditor** is required to communicate with the **predecessor auditor** before accepting the engagement. This communication should cover:

- The reasons for the change in auditors
- Any disagreements between the predecessor and management regarding accounting principles or audit procedures
- Matters related to management's integrity
- Whether the predecessor is aware of any fraud

> The predecessor auditor is required to respond to the successor's inquiries promptly and completely, but the client must authorize the predecessor to share confidential information. If the client refuses to authorize communication, the successor should consider this a red flag.

### Engagement Letters

Once the firm decides to accept, an **engagement letter** formalizes the arrangement. The engagement letter should include:

- The objective and scope of the audit
- Management's responsibilities (preparing financial statements, maintaining internal controls, providing access to information)
- The auditor's responsibilities (conducting the audit in accordance with GAAS)
- Limitations of the audit (reasonable, not absolute, assurance)
- The expected form of the auditor's report
- Fees and billing arrangements

The engagement letter is required for every audit engagement and should be updated when terms change significantly.

## Understanding the Entity and Its Environment

Effective audit planning depends on the auditor's thorough understanding of the client's business. This is not a checkbox exercise — it directly drives every risk assessment and procedure selection decision.

The auditor should understand:

**Industry and regulatory factors** — What industry does the client operate in? What regulations apply? A healthcare company faces different risks than a manufacturer.

**The entity's nature** — Ownership structure, governance, business model, how the entity is financed, and what accounting policies it selects.

**Objectives, strategies, and business risks** — What is the entity trying to achieve? What risks threaten those objectives? Business risks often lead to risks of material misstatement.

**Measurement and review of financial performance** — What metrics does management use? What internal reporting exists? Key performance indicators (KPIs) can help the auditor identify anomalies.

**Internal control** — The design and implementation of controls relevant to the audit. This is explored in depth in later lessons.

### Sources of Information

Auditors gather this understanding from multiple sources:

- Prior audit experience with the client
- Inquiries of management and those charged with governance
- Industry publications and analyst reports
- Tours of the client's facilities
- Inspection of documents such as board minutes, contracts, and internal reports

## Developing the Audit Strategy and Plan

The **overall audit strategy** sets the scope, timing, and direction of the audit. It guides the development of the detailed **audit plan**.

### Audit Strategy Considerations

When developing the strategy, the auditor considers:

- **Nature and extent of resources** — Which team members will work on which areas? Are specialists needed (IT auditors, valuations experts)?
- **Timing** — When will fieldwork occur? Will interim testing be performed before year-end?
- **Scope** — Which locations or business units will be covered? Are there components audited by other firms?
- **Risk profile** — Preliminary identification of higher-risk areas that require more attention

### The Audit Plan

The audit plan is more detailed than the strategy. It specifies the **nature, timing, and extent** of:

- Risk assessment procedures
- Tests of controls (if the auditor plans to rely on controls)
- Substantive procedures (tests of details and analytical procedures)

The plan is not fixed. As the audit progresses and the auditor obtains new information, the strategy and plan should be updated.

> The exam often tests whether a particular factor affects the nature, timing, or extent of procedures. "Nature" refers to the type of procedure. "Timing" refers to when it is performed. "Extent" refers to how much testing is done.

## Materiality

**Materiality** is one of the most important concepts in auditing. It is the threshold above which a misstatement could reasonably influence the economic decisions of financial statement users.

### Setting Materiality

Materiality is a matter of professional judgment. There is no single formula, but common benchmarks include:

| Benchmark | Typical Range |
|-----------|--------------|
| Pre-tax income | 5% to 10% |
| Total revenue | 0.5% to 1% |
| Total assets | 0.5% to 1% |
| Net assets (equity) | 1% to 2% |

The auditor selects the benchmark most appropriate for the entity. A not-for-profit organization might use total revenue or total expenses, while a for-profit company might use pre-tax income.

### Performance Materiality

**Performance materiality** is set at an amount lower than overall materiality. Its purpose is to reduce to an appropriately low level the probability that the aggregate of uncorrected and undetected misstatements exceeds overall materiality.

For example, if overall materiality is $500,000, performance materiality might be set at $375,000. This provides a buffer so that individually immaterial misstatements do not accumulate beyond the overall threshold.

### Clearly Trivial Threshold

Below performance materiality, the auditor also establishes a **clearly trivial** threshold — the amount below which misstatements are not accumulated because they are inconsequential individually and in the aggregate. This is typically set at 5% or less of overall materiality.

### Revising Materiality

Materiality is set during planning but may need to be revised as the audit progresses. If the auditor discovers that a lower materiality level is appropriate (for example, because actual results differ significantly from the planning estimates), previously completed audit work may need to be reassessed.

## Preliminary Analytical Procedures

During planning, auditors perform **preliminary analytical procedures** to identify areas that may warrant special attention. These procedures involve comparing current-period financial data with:

- Prior-period financial data
- Budgets and forecasts
- Industry data and benchmarks

Unexpected fluctuations or the absence of expected fluctuations may signal risks of material misstatement. For example, if a company's revenue grew 30% while the industry average was 3%, the auditor would want to understand why.

## Key Terms

- **Engagement letter** — A written agreement between the auditor and client that documents the terms of the audit engagement
- **Materiality** — The magnitude of a misstatement that could reasonably influence the decisions of financial statement users
- **Performance materiality** — An amount set lower than overall materiality to reduce the risk that aggregate uncorrected misstatements exceed materiality
- **Audit strategy** — The high-level plan that sets the scope, timing, and direction of the audit
- **Predecessor auditor** — The auditor who previously reported on the client's financial statements
