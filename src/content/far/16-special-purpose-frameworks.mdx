# Special Purpose Frameworks

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the types of special purpose frameworks (SPFs) and when they are used
- Distinguish cash basis, modified cash basis, and tax basis financial statements from GAAP
- Explain regulatory basis and contractual basis financial reporting
- Apply the correct financial statement titles and disclosure requirements for SPF presentations
- Recognize common differences between tax basis and GAAP financial statements

## What Are Special Purpose Frameworks?

A **special purpose framework (SPF)** — historically called an **other comprehensive basis of accounting (OCBOA)** — is a financial reporting framework other than GAAP. SPFs are commonly used by small and mid-sized entities that do not need full GAAP compliance and want to reduce the complexity and cost of financial reporting.

Under **AU-C Section 800** (for audits) and **SSARS** (for compilations and reviews), four types of special purpose frameworks are recognized:

1. **Cash basis**
2. **Modified cash basis**
3. **Tax basis**
4. **Regulatory basis**
5. **Contractual basis**

> The term "OCBOA" is no longer used in current professional standards, but you may still encounter it on the CPA exam. The current term is **special purpose framework**. Know both terms.

## Cash Basis

Under the **cash basis**, revenues are recognized when cash is **received** and expenses are recognized when cash is **paid**. No accruals, deferrals, or estimates are recorded. This is the simplest framework but produces the least informative financial statements.

### Characteristics

- **Revenue recognition** — Recorded only upon cash receipt, regardless of when earned
- **Expense recognition** — Recorded only upon cash disbursement, regardless of when incurred
- **No receivables or payables** — Since only cash transactions are recorded, there are no accounts receivable, accounts payable, or accrued liabilities
- **No depreciation** — Fixed assets are expensed when purchased (cash outflow), not depreciated over useful life
- **No inventory** — Purchases are expensed when paid

### Limitations

Cash basis statements can be misleading. A company could delay vendor payments to inflate cash-basis income, or accelerate collections to shift income between periods. These statements do not reflect economic reality the way accrual-based statements do.

**Example:** A consulting firm completes a $50,000 project in December but does not receive payment until January. Under the cash basis, no revenue is recognized in December — the full $50,000 appears in January, even though the work was performed in the prior year.

## Modified Cash Basis

The **modified cash basis** starts with the cash basis but adds selected accrual-basis modifications. The key requirement is that the modifications must have **substantial support** — they must relate to transactions that are commonly adjusted to accrual under accepted practice.

### Acceptable Modifications

The following accrual adjustments are generally considered to have substantial support:

- **Depreciation and amortization** of long-lived assets
- **Income taxes** (accrual of current and deferred income taxes)
- **Capitalization of fixed assets** (rather than expensing on purchase)
- **Accrual of debt** and related interest

### Unacceptable Modifications

Modifications that would essentially convert the statements to full GAAP defeat the purpose of the modified cash basis. A framework with too many accrual modifications is no longer a modified cash basis — it is either GAAP or an undefined hybrid that cannot be described as an SPF.

> The exam frequently tests the distinction between acceptable and unacceptable modifications. The general rule: modifications for items with **long-term effects** (depreciation, debt, income taxes) are acceptable. Modifications for routine operating items (receivables, payables, prepaid expenses) would push the framework too close to GAAP.

### Modified Cash Basis Example

A small law firm uses the modified cash basis. It records:
- Revenue when cash is collected (cash basis)
- Expenses when cash is disbursed (cash basis)
- Depreciation on its office building and furniture (accrual modification)
- Income tax accruals (accrual modification)
- The building as a fixed asset on the balance sheet (accrual modification)

This is a valid modified cash basis presentation because the modifications have substantial support and the framework is not indistinguishable from GAAP.

## Tax Basis

Under the **tax basis**, financial statements are prepared using the recognition and measurement rules of the **Internal Revenue Code (IRC)**. Revenue and expenses are recognized according to the entity's tax accounting methods (cash or accrual as elected for tax purposes), and assets are measured at tax basis rather than book basis.

### Common Tax-to-GAAP Differences

| Item | Tax Basis | GAAP |
|------|-----------|------|
| Depreciation | MACRS (accelerated, shorter lives) | Straight-line or systematic method over economic life |
| Revenue recognition | Per IRC Section 451 (generally when received, constructive receipt, or accrued per method) | ASC 606 five-step model |
| Inventory | LIFO allowed; UNICAP required (Sec. 263A) | LIFO, FIFO, weighted average; lower of cost or NRV |
| Lease accounting | Generally no ROU asset/lease liability | ASC 842 requires ROU asset and lease liability |
| Goodwill | Amortized over 15 years (Sec. 197) | Not amortized; tested for impairment annually |
| Meals and entertainment | 50% deduction limit for meals; entertainment nondeductible | Full expense recognized |
| Municipal bond interest | Excluded from income | Recognized as revenue |
| Bad debts | Direct write-off method (Sec. 166) | Allowance method (CECL under ASC 326) |

### When Tax Basis Is Used

Tax basis statements are common for:
- Small businesses whose owners focus on taxable income rather than GAAP income
- Partnerships and S corporations where financial results flow through to owners' tax returns
- Entities where preparing two sets of books (GAAP and tax) would be cost-prohibitive

> Tax basis financial statements are particularly common in compilation and review engagements under SSARS. The CPA exam tests your ability to identify which items cause tax and GAAP differences and to understand why an entity might choose tax basis reporting.

## Regulatory Basis

**Regulatory basis** financial statements are prepared in accordance with requirements imposed by a **governmental regulatory agency**. The entity follows the rules prescribed by the regulator rather than GAAP.

### Examples

- **Insurance companies** preparing statutory financial statements under state insurance department rules (SAP — Statutory Accounting Principles)
- **Banks and credit unions** filing call reports under Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council (FFIEC) requirements
- **Utilities** following rate-making rules prescribed by a public utility commission

### General-Use vs. Special-Use

Regulatory basis financial statements are classified as either:

- **General-use** — The regulatory basis is substantially equivalent to GAAP and the statements are intended for general distribution. An unmodified opinion may be issued using standard report language.
- **Special-use** — The basis differs significantly from GAAP and the statements are intended only for the regulatory agency. The auditor's report must include an emphasis-of-matter paragraph restricting use or must include an other-matter paragraph describing the purpose and intended users.

## Contractual Basis

**Contractual basis** financial statements are prepared in accordance with the specific financial reporting provisions of a **contract or agreement**. The terms of the contract dictate the recognition, measurement, and presentation rules.

### Examples

- Loan covenants that require specific calculations for debt-to-equity ratios or EBITDA
- Joint venture agreements specifying how profits, costs, or assets are measured
- Lease agreements requiring tenant financial statements using specific methods
- Franchise agreements requiring financial reporting on prescribed bases

Contractual basis statements are almost always **special-use** — they are prepared specifically for the parties to the contract and are not appropriate for general distribution.

## Financial Statement Titles

A critical requirement for SPF financial statements is that the **titles must differ from GAAP financial statement titles** to alert users that the statements are not prepared under GAAP.

| GAAP Title | SPF Alternative Title |
|------------|----------------------|
| Balance Sheet | Statement of Assets and Liabilities — Tax Basis |
| Income Statement | Statement of Revenue and Expenses — Cash Basis |
| Statement of Cash Flows | Statement of Cash Receipts and Disbursements |
| Statement of Stockholders' Equity | Statement of Changes in Equity — Tax Basis |

> If an SPF statement uses GAAP titles (e.g., "Balance Sheet"), the auditor must consider whether this is misleading. The titles should clearly communicate the basis of accounting to prevent users from assuming GAAP compliance.

## Disclosure Requirements for SPFs

SPF financial statements require disclosures that include:

1. **A description of the special purpose framework** used and how it differs from GAAP
2. **Informative disclosures similar to GAAP** when the financial statements contain items that are the same as or similar to GAAP items (e.g., related party transactions, going concern, subsequent events, contingencies)
3. **The basis of accounting** and significant accounting policies applied

The level of disclosure is generally less extensive than full GAAP, but **material items that would make the statements misleading if omitted** must still be disclosed. The practitioner uses professional judgment to determine which disclosures are necessary.

### Compilation and Review Considerations

Under **SSARS**, a CPA may compile or review SPF financial statements. The compilation or review report must:

- Identify the applicable special purpose framework
- State that the framework is a basis other than GAAP
- Include a reference to the note describing the framework

## Auditor Reporting on SPF Financial Statements

When auditing SPF financial statements under **AU-C Section 800**:

- The auditor's report uses an **unmodified opinion** if the statements are fairly presented in accordance with the SPF
- The report must include an **emphasis-of-matter paragraph** that describes the SPF and refers to the note in the financial statements describing the basis
- For **special-use** statements, the report includes an **other-matter paragraph** restricting distribution to specified users

## Key Terms

- **Special purpose framework (SPF)** — A financial reporting framework other than GAAP, including cash, modified cash, tax, regulatory, and contractual bases
- **OCBOA** — Other comprehensive basis of accounting, the former term for SPFs; still tested on the CPA exam
- **Cash basis** — Revenue recognized when cash is received; expenses recognized when cash is paid
- **Modified cash basis** — Cash basis with selected accrual modifications that have substantial support (depreciation, income taxes, debt)
- **Tax basis** — Financial statements prepared using IRC recognition and measurement rules
- **Regulatory basis** — Financial statements prepared in accordance with rules prescribed by a governmental regulatory agency
- **Contractual basis** — Financial statements prepared per terms of a specific contract or agreement
- **Substantial support** — The requirement that modifications to cash basis accounting must relate to commonly accepted accrual adjustments
- **MACRS** — Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System, the tax depreciation method under the IRC
- **AU-C Section 800** — The auditing standard governing audits of financial statements prepared under special purpose frameworks
- **Statutory accounting principles (SAP)** — Insurance industry regulatory basis prescribed by state insurance departments
- **General-use vs. special-use** — Classification that determines whether SPF statements can be broadly distributed or are restricted to specified parties
