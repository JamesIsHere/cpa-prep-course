# Fixed Assets

## Learning Objectives

- Determine the cost of a fixed asset at acquisition
- Calculate depreciation under straight-line, declining balance, and units-of-production methods
- Apply impairment testing rules for long-lived assets under ASC 360
- Account for disposals and nonmonetary exchanges of assets

## Acquisition Costs

**Property, plant, and equipment (PP&E)** are tangible long-lived assets used in operations and not held for sale. Under U.S. GAAP, a fixed asset is initially recorded at its **historical cost**, which includes all expenditures necessary to acquire the asset and prepare it for its intended use:

- Purchase price (less any trade discounts)
- Sales tax
- Freight and delivery charges
- Installation and setup costs
- Testing costs incurred before the asset is ready for use
- Legal fees for title transfer

Costs incurred **after** the asset is placed in service are capitalized only if they extend the useful life, increase capacity, or improve efficiency. Routine repairs and maintenance are expensed as incurred.

**Interest capitalization** applies when an entity constructs an asset for its own use. Under ASC 835, interest is capitalized during the construction period based on the weighted-average accumulated expenditures, up to the amount of actual interest incurred.

> On the exam, remember that land is never depreciated. Also, the cost of land includes grading, clearing, and razing existing structures — but land improvements (parking lots, fences) are separately depreciated.

## Depreciation Methods

Depreciation is the systematic allocation of an asset's cost (less salvage value) over its useful life. The three primary methods are:

### Straight-Line

The most common method. Equal expense each period.

**Annual depreciation = (Cost - Salvage value) / Useful life**

### Declining Balance

An accelerated method that applies a constant rate to the declining book value. The most common variant is **double-declining balance (DDB)**, which uses twice the straight-line rate. Salvage value is not subtracted in the calculation, but the asset is never depreciated below salvage value.

**Annual depreciation = Book value at beginning of year x (2 / Useful life)**

### Units of Production

Depreciation varies with actual usage, making it appropriate for assets whose wear is driven by output rather than time.

**Depreciation per unit = (Cost - Salvage value) / Total estimated units**
**Annual depreciation = Depreciation per unit x Units produced in the period**

### Worked Example

A machine is purchased for $100,000, with a $10,000 salvage value and a 5-year useful life.

**Straight-line:**
- Annual depreciation = ($100,000 - $10,000) / 5 = **$18,000 per year**

**Double-declining balance (Year 1 and Year 2):**
- DDB rate = 2 / 5 = 40%
- Year 1: $100,000 x 40% = **$40,000** (book value drops to $60,000)
- Year 2: $60,000 x 40% = **$24,000** (book value drops to $36,000)
- Continue until book value reaches salvage value of $10,000

> Under DDB, entities often switch to straight-line in the year when straight-line depreciation on the remaining book value exceeds the DDB amount. This maximizes the deduction and ensures the asset is fully depreciated to salvage value.

## Impairment of Long-Lived Assets

Under **ASC 360**, long-lived assets held for use are tested for impairment when events or changes in circumstances indicate the carrying amount may not be recoverable. The test is a two-step process:

**Step 1 — Recoverability test:** Compare the asset's carrying amount to the sum of its estimated **undiscounted** future cash flows. If the carrying amount exceeds undiscounted cash flows, the asset is impaired.

**Step 2 — Measurement:** The impairment loss equals the carrying amount minus the asset's **fair value**.

Once recognized, an impairment loss on an asset held for use **cannot be reversed** under U.S. GAAP.

For assets **held for sale**, the asset is written down to fair value less costs to sell. No further depreciation is recorded. Subsequent recoveries are allowed, but only up to the cumulative loss previously recognized.

## Disposals and Exchanges

When an asset is disposed of, the entity removes the asset's cost and accumulated depreciation from the books. The difference between the net proceeds and the carrying amount is recognized as a gain or loss.

**Nonmonetary exchanges** are governed by ASC 845. The general rule is that nonmonetary exchanges are recorded at **fair value**, with gains and losses recognized. However, if the exchange **lacks commercial substance** (the entity's future cash flows are not expected to change significantly), the exchange is recorded at the **carrying amount** of the asset given up, and no gain is recognized.

An exchange has **commercial substance** if the configuration (timing, risk, or amount) of future cash flows changes as a result of the exchange.

## Key Terms

- **Historical cost** — The total expenditure to acquire an asset and prepare it for its intended use
- **Salvage value** — The estimated amount the entity expects to receive when the asset is retired
- **Accumulated depreciation** — The total depreciation recognized on an asset since it was placed in service
- **Impairment** — A condition in which the carrying amount of an asset exceeds its fair value and is not recoverable through future cash flows
- **Commercial substance** — An exchange has commercial substance if the entity's future cash flows change significantly as a result

## Practice Problems

**1.** Equipment is purchased for $80,000 with a $5,000 salvage value and a 10-year useful life. Using the straight-line method, what is the annual depreciation expense?

a) $8,000
b) $7,500
c) $7,000
d) $5,000

**Answer:** b) $7,500 — ($80,000 - $5,000) / 10 = $7,500 per year.

---

**2.** A long-lived asset has a carrying amount of $200,000. The expected undiscounted future cash flows are $180,000 and the fair value is $150,000. What is the impairment loss?

a) $0
b) $20,000
c) $50,000
d) $30,000

**Answer:** c) $50,000 — Step 1: The carrying amount ($200,000) exceeds undiscounted cash flows ($180,000), so the asset is impaired. Step 2: Impairment loss = $200,000 - $150,000 fair value = $50,000.

---

**3.** In a nonmonetary exchange that lacks commercial substance, the asset received is recorded at:

a) Fair value of the asset received
b) Fair value of the asset given up
c) Carrying amount of the asset given up
d) The lower of fair value or carrying amount

**Answer:** c) Carrying amount of the asset given up — when an exchange lacks commercial substance, no gain or loss is recognized and the new asset is recorded at the book value of the old asset (plus any cash paid or minus any cash received).
