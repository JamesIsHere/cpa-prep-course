# Financial Statement Presentation

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the four primary financial statements and their purposes
- Understand the classification and presentation requirements for the balance sheet and income statement
- Distinguish between single-step and multi-step income statement formats
- Explain the relationships between the financial statements
- Recognize key IFRS vs. U.S. GAAP differences tested on the CPA exam

## The Balance Sheet (Statement of Financial Position)

The **balance sheet** presents a snapshot of an entity's financial position at a specific point in time. It reports three elements: assets, liabilities, and equity — linked by the fundamental accounting equation:

**Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

Assets and liabilities are classified as either **current** or **noncurrent**. Current assets are expected to be converted to cash or consumed within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer. Current liabilities are obligations due within that same period.

Common balance sheet classifications include:

- **Current assets** — Cash, accounts receivable, inventory, prepaid expenses
- **Noncurrent assets** — Property, plant, and equipment (PP&E), intangible assets, long-term investments
- **Current liabilities** — Accounts payable, accrued expenses, current portion of long-term debt
- **Noncurrent liabilities** — Bonds payable, long-term lease obligations, deferred tax liabilities
- **Equity** — Common stock, additional paid-in capital, retained earnings, accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI)

> On the CPA exam, watch for questions about proper classification. A debt maturing within 12 months stays noncurrent only if the entity has both the intent and ability to refinance it on a long-term basis before the financial statements are issued.

### Offsetting Assets and Liabilities

As a general rule, assets and liabilities should **not** be offset against each other on the balance sheet. A right of offset exists only when the entity has a legally enforceable right to set off the amounts and intends to settle on a net basis. This is most commonly tested in the context of deferred tax assets and liabilities, which may be offset if they relate to the same taxing jurisdiction.

## The Income Statement

The **income statement** reports an entity's financial performance over a period of time. It can be presented in two formats:

**Single-step format** — Groups all revenues together and all expenses together, arriving at net income in one step.

**Multi-step format** — Separates operating and nonoperating activities, highlighting key subtotals:

1. **Gross profit** = Net sales - Cost of goods sold
2. **Operating income** = Gross profit - Operating expenses
3. **Income before tax** = Operating income + Other revenues/gains - Other expenses/losses
4. **Net income** = Income before tax - Income tax expense

Certain items receive special presentation:

- **Discontinued operations** — Reported net of tax as a separate line item below income from continuing operations. A discontinued operation is a component of an entity that has been disposed of or is classified as held for sale, and represents a strategic shift that has (or will have) a major effect on the entity's operations and financial results.
- **Other comprehensive income (OCI)** — Items like unrealized gains on available-for-sale securities, foreign currency translation adjustments, and pension adjustments that bypass net income. OCI may be presented in a combined statement of comprehensive income or in a separate statement.

> Extraordinary items are no longer separately reported under U.S. GAAP. This change was made by ASU 2015-01. If you see an exam question referencing extraordinary items, the correct answer is that they are no longer a valid classification.

## The Statement of Changes in Equity

The **statement of stockholders' equity** reconciles the beginning and ending balances of each equity component. Common line items include:

- Net income (increases retained earnings)
- Dividends declared (decreases retained earnings)
- Issuance of common stock (increases common stock and APIC)
- Treasury stock transactions
- Other comprehensive income items

This statement ties together the balance sheet and income statement by showing how net income flows into retained earnings and how equity transactions affect the overall equity balance.

## Relationships Between the Statements

The four primary financial statements are interconnected:

1. Net income from the income statement flows to retained earnings on the statement of equity
2. Ending retained earnings from the equity statement appears on the balance sheet
3. The statement of cash flows reconciles the beginning and ending cash balances shown on the balance sheet
4. OCI from the income statement (or separate OCI statement) flows to AOCI on the balance sheet

Understanding these linkages is essential for analyzing how a single transaction ripples across multiple statements. For example, recording depreciation expense reduces net income (income statement), reduces retained earnings (equity statement), increases accumulated depreciation (balance sheet), and appears as a noncash add-back on the statement of cash flows.

## GAAP Hierarchy and Authoritative Guidance

The **FASB Accounting Standards Codification (ASC)** is the single source of authoritative U.S. GAAP for nongovernmental entities. The codification organized thousands of prior pronouncements into a structured, topical system. When researching a GAAP question, the hierarchy is:

1. **FASB ASC** — The sole authoritative source (includes ASUs that update the codification)
2. **Nonauthoritative sources** — FASB Concepts Statements, AICPA Issues Papers, textbooks, articles — may be consulted when the codification does not address a topic, but they are not binding

> The SEC has additional authoritative guidance for public companies (SABs, EITF interpretations, SEC rules). For CPA exam purposes, know that SEC registrants follow both the ASC and SEC-specific requirements.

## Key IFRS vs. GAAP Differences

The CPA exam tests select differences between U.S. GAAP and IFRS. The most frequently tested include:

| Topic | U.S. GAAP | IFRS |
|-------|-----------|------|
| Inventory methods | FIFO, LIFO, weighted average permitted | LIFO prohibited |
| Inventory write-down | No reversal (FIFO/WA); lower of cost or market (LIFO) | Reversal allowed up to original cost |
| Development costs | Expensed as incurred (R&D) | Capitalized if criteria met (IAS 38) |
| Component depreciation | Permitted but not required | Required (each significant component separately) |
| Revaluation of PP&E | Not permitted (historical cost only) | Permitted under revaluation model (IAS 16) |
| Investment property | No separate category | Separate category with fair value option (IAS 40) |
| Impairment reversal (long-lived) | Not permitted | Permitted (except goodwill) |
| Extraordinary items | Eliminated (ASU 2015-01) | Never existed under IFRS |
| Contingent liabilities | Accrue if probable (>75%) and estimable | Accrue if probable (>50%) and estimable |

## Key Terms

- **Current asset** — An asset expected to be converted to cash or consumed within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer
- **Discontinued operations** — A component of an entity that has been disposed of or is held for sale, reported net of tax below continuing operations
- **Multi-step income statement** — A format that separates operating and nonoperating activities to highlight gross profit, operating income, and net income as distinct subtotals
- **Other comprehensive income (OCI)** — Gains and losses that bypass net income and are reported in equity as accumulated other comprehensive income
- **Working capital** — Current assets minus current liabilities, a measure of short-term liquidity
- **FASB ASC** — The sole source of authoritative U.S. GAAP for nongovernmental entities, organized as a topical codification
