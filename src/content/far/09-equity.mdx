# Equity

## Learning Objectives

- Account for the issuance of common stock, preferred stock, and treasury stock
- Distinguish between stock dividends and stock splits and their effects on equity
- Calculate basic and diluted earnings per share
- Understand the components of stockholders' equity and how transactions affect each component

## Components of Stockholders' Equity

Stockholders' equity represents the residual interest in an entity's assets after deducting liabilities. The major components are:

- **Common stock** — Par value of issued shares
- **Additional paid-in capital (APIC)** — The excess of the issue price over par value
- **Retained earnings** — Cumulative net income less cumulative dividends declared
- **Treasury stock** — The cost of shares repurchased by the entity (a contra-equity account)
- **Accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI)** — Cumulative OCI items such as unrealized gains on available-for-sale securities, foreign currency translation adjustments, and pension adjustments

## Issuance of Stock

When stock is issued for cash, the entry credits Common Stock for the par value of shares issued and credits APIC for the excess.

**Example:** A company issues 10,000 shares of $1 par common stock for $15 per share.

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---------|-------|--------|
| Cash | $150,000 | |
| Common stock ($1 par) | | $10,000 |
| APIC — common stock | | $140,000 |

When stock is issued for **noncash consideration** (such as land or services), the transaction is recorded at the **fair value of the consideration received** or the fair value of the stock issued, whichever is more readily determinable.

## Preferred Stock

**Preferred stock** carries preferential rights, most commonly:

- **Dividend preference** — Preferred shareholders receive dividends before common shareholders
- **Liquidation preference** — Preferred shareholders receive their stated liquidation value before common shareholders in a wind-down

Key characteristics:

- **Cumulative** preferred stock — Unpaid dividends accumulate as **dividends in arrears**. All arrears must be paid before common dividends can be distributed. Dividends in arrears are not liabilities until declared, but they are disclosed in the notes.
- **Participating** preferred stock — After receiving its stated dividend, it shares in additional dividends with common shareholders
- **Convertible** preferred stock — Can be converted into common shares at a predetermined ratio

## Treasury Stock

**Treasury stock** is a company's own stock that has been issued and subsequently reacquired. It is not an asset — it is a **contra-equity** account that reduces total stockholders' equity. Treasury shares have no voting rights and receive no dividends.

Two methods are commonly used:

**Cost method** (most common) — Treasury stock is recorded at the reacquisition cost. When resold above cost, the excess is credited to APIC — Treasury Stock. When resold below cost, the deficit is debited first against APIC — Treasury Stock (to the extent of prior credits), then against Retained Earnings.

**Par value method** — Treasury stock is recorded at par value. The original APIC associated with the shares is removed at reacquisition.

> Treasury stock transactions never produce gains or losses on the income statement. Any differences flow through APIC or retained earnings — never through net income.

## Stock Dividends and Stock Splits

**Stock dividends** distribute additional shares to existing shareholders proportionally. They do not change total equity — they reclassify amounts within equity.

- **Small stock dividend** (less than 20-25% of outstanding shares) — Recorded at **fair market value** of the shares distributed. Retained earnings is debited, and Common Stock and APIC are credited.
- **Large stock dividend** (25% or more of outstanding shares) — Recorded at **par value**. Retained earnings is debited and Common Stock is credited. No APIC adjustment.

**Stock splits** increase the number of shares outstanding while proportionally reducing the par value per share. No journal entry is required — only a memo entry noting the new share count and par value.

**Example — Small stock dividend:** A company with 100,000 shares outstanding ($2 par, market price $30) declares a 10% stock dividend.

- New shares issued: 100,000 x 10% = 10,000 shares
- Fair value: 10,000 x $30 = $300,000

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---------|-------|--------|
| Retained earnings | $300,000 | |
| Common stock ($2 par) | | $20,000 |
| APIC — common stock | | $280,000 |

## Earnings Per Share (EPS)

Public entities are required to report **basic EPS** and **diluted EPS** on the face of the income statement.

### Basic EPS

**Basic EPS = (Net income - Preferred dividends) / Weighted-average common shares outstanding**

Preferred dividends are subtracted because they represent income not available to common shareholders. For cumulative preferred stock, the annual dividend is subtracted whether or not it is declared. For noncumulative preferred stock, only declared dividends are subtracted.

### Diluted EPS

Diluted EPS accounts for all potentially dilutive securities — stock options, warrants, convertible bonds, and convertible preferred stock. It shows the worst-case EPS scenario.

- **Stock options and warrants** — Use the **treasury stock method**. Assume options are exercised, cash proceeds are used to buy back shares at the average market price, and the net new shares are added to the denominator.
- **Convertible bonds** — Use the **if-converted method**. Add back the after-tax interest expense to the numerator and add the shares that would be issued upon conversion to the denominator.
- **Convertible preferred stock** — Use the **if-converted method**. Add back the preferred dividends to the numerator and add the shares that would be issued upon conversion to the denominator.

A potentially dilutive security is included only if it **reduces** EPS. If including it would increase EPS, it is **antidilutive** and excluded.

> When computing diluted EPS, test each potentially dilutive security individually. Start with the most dilutive and stop adding securities when the next one would be antidilutive.

## Key Terms

- **Par value** — An arbitrary per-share amount assigned in the corporate charter, representing the legal capital of the corporation
- **Additional paid-in capital (APIC)** — The amount received from shareholders in excess of par value
- **Treasury stock** — Shares of a company's own stock that have been reacquired; a contra-equity account
- **Dividends in arrears** — Cumulative preferred dividends that have not been declared; disclosed in the notes but not recorded as a liability
- **Diluted EPS** — Earnings per share calculated assuming all potentially dilutive securities are converted or exercised

## Practice Problems

**1.** A company has 200,000 weighted-average common shares outstanding, net income of $500,000, and cumulative preferred dividends of $50,000 (not declared this year). What is basic EPS?

a) $2.50
b) $2.25
c) $2.75
d) $2.00

**Answer:** b) $2.25 — Basic EPS = ($500,000 - $50,000) / 200,000 = $2.25. For cumulative preferred stock, the dividend is subtracted whether or not it is declared.

---

**2.** A company declares a 5% stock dividend when 500,000 shares are outstanding at $1 par value and the market price is $20 per share. What is the debit to retained earnings?

a) $25,000
b) $100,000
c) $475,000
d) $500,000

**Answer:** d) $500,000 — This is a small stock dividend (5% < 20-25%), so it is recorded at fair market value: 25,000 shares x $20 = $500,000.

---

**3.** Treasury stock transactions can result in:

a) A gain reported on the income statement
b) A loss reported on the income statement
c) An increase to additional paid-in capital
d) Both a gain and a loss on the income statement

**Answer:** c) An increase to additional paid-in capital — when treasury stock is reissued above cost, the excess is credited to APIC — Treasury Stock. Treasury stock transactions never affect the income statement.
