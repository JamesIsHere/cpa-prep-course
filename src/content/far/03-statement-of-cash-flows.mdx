# Statement of Cash Flows

## Learning Objectives

- Classify transactions into operating, investing, and financing activities
- Prepare operating cash flows using the indirect method and the direct method
- Handle common classification edge cases (interest, dividends, taxes)
- Account for noncash investing and financing activities in supplemental disclosures

## Purpose of the Statement of Cash Flows

The **statement of cash flows** explains the change in cash and cash equivalents during a reporting period. It answers a question the income statement cannot: where did the cash come from and where did it go?

Cash equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible to known amounts of cash and are so near maturity that they present insignificant risk of changes in value (generally original maturities of three months or less). Examples include Treasury bills, commercial paper, and money market funds.

The statement is organized into three sections:

- **Operating activities** — Cash flows from the entity's core revenue-producing activities
- **Investing activities** — Cash flows from the purchase and sale of long-term assets and investments
- **Financing activities** — Cash flows from transactions with owners and creditors (issuing stock, borrowing, paying dividends)

## Operating Activities — Indirect Method

The **indirect method** starts with net income and adjusts for items that affected net income but did not involve cash:

**Step 1:** Start with net income

**Step 2:** Add back noncash expenses (depreciation, amortization, impairment losses)

**Step 3:** Remove gains and add back losses on investing/financing activities (these are reclassified to the appropriate section)

**Step 4:** Adjust for changes in operating assets and liabilities (working capital changes)

### Working Capital Adjustments

| Change | Effect on Cash |
|--------|---------------|
| Increase in current assets (AR, inventory, prepaids) | **Subtract** — cash was used |
| Decrease in current assets | **Add** — cash was freed up |
| Increase in current liabilities (AP, accrued expenses) | **Add** — cash was conserved |
| Decrease in current liabilities | **Subtract** — cash was paid out |

### Worked Example — Indirect Method

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Net income | $120,000 |
| Depreciation expense | 25,000 |
| Amortization of patent | 5,000 |
| Gain on sale of equipment | (8,000) |
| Loss on debt extinguishment | 3,000 |
| Increase in accounts receivable | (15,000) |
| Decrease in inventory | 10,000 |
| Increase in accounts payable | 7,000 |
| Decrease in accrued liabilities | (4,000) |
| **Cash from operating activities** | **$143,000** |

## Operating Activities — Direct Method

The **direct method** reports gross cash receipts and payments from operating activities. It shows the actual cash flows rather than reconciling from net income:

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Cash received from customers | $850,000 |
| Cash paid to suppliers | (520,000) |
| Cash paid to employees | (180,000) |
| Cash paid for interest | (25,000) |
| Cash paid for income taxes | (40,000) |
| **Cash from operating activities** | **$85,000** |

### Converting Accrual Amounts to Cash (Direct Method)

To prepare the direct method, convert each income statement line item to its cash equivalent:

**Cash received from customers** = Revenue + Decrease in AR (or − Increase in AR)

**Cash paid to suppliers** = COGS + Increase in inventory (or − Decrease) − Increase in AP (or + Decrease)

**Cash paid for interest** = Interest expense − Increase in interest payable (or + Decrease) + Amortization of bond premium (or − Discount amortization)

Although the FASB encourages the direct method, the indirect method is far more commonly used in practice. If an entity uses the direct method, a reconciliation of net income to operating cash flows (essentially the indirect method) must be provided in a supplemental schedule.

## Investing Activities

Investing activities involve changes in long-term assets:

| Transaction | Classification |
|-------------|---------------|
| Purchase of PP&E or intangible assets | Cash outflow |
| Proceeds from sale of PP&E | Cash inflow |
| Purchase of investments (stocks, bonds held to maturity) | Cash outflow |
| Proceeds from sale/maturity of investments | Cash inflow |
| Loans made to other entities | Cash outflow |
| Collection of loan principal | Cash inflow |

> The gain or loss on sale of equipment is removed from operating activities (indirect method) because the total cash proceeds are reported as an investing inflow. Including both would double-count the cash effect.

## Financing Activities

Financing activities involve changes in long-term liabilities and equity:

| Transaction | Classification |
|-------------|---------------|
| Proceeds from issuing bonds or notes | Cash inflow |
| Repayment of debt principal | Cash outflow |
| Proceeds from issuing stock | Cash inflow |
| Repurchase of treasury stock | Cash outflow |
| Dividends paid to shareholders | Cash outflow |
| Debt issuance costs paid | Cash outflow |

## Classification Edge Cases

These frequently tested rules distinguish U.S. GAAP from IFRS:

| Item | U.S. GAAP | IFRS |
|------|-----------|------|
| Interest paid | Operating | Operating or Financing (policy choice) |
| Interest received | Operating | Operating or Investing (policy choice) |
| Dividends paid | Financing | Operating or Financing (policy choice) |
| Dividends received | Operating | Operating or Investing (policy choice) |
| Income taxes paid | Operating (unless identifiable with investing/financing) | Operating (unless identifiable with investing/financing) |

> Under U.S. GAAP, interest paid and received are always operating. This is one of the most commonly tested classification rules on the CPA exam.

## Noncash Investing and Financing Activities

Transactions that do not involve cash but are significant investing or financing activities are disclosed in a **supplemental schedule** — not in the body of the statement of cash flows. Examples include:

- Converting debt to equity
- Acquiring assets by assuming liabilities (e.g., purchasing a building by signing a mortgage)
- Obtaining an asset through a capital lease
- Exchanging noncash assets

These disclosures ensure that users understand the full scope of investing and financing activities, even when no cash changed hands.

## The T-Account Approach

A common technique for preparing the statement of cash flows is to set up T-accounts for every balance sheet account and "explain" each change. Since the balance sheet must balance, every change in a non-cash account must be offset by a change in cash or another non-cash account. By systematically working through each account, you can identify every cash flow and ensure the statement is complete.

Steps:
1. Set up a T-account for cash and one for every other balance sheet account
2. Enter the beginning and ending balances
3. For each non-cash account, identify the transactions that caused the change
4. Record each transaction's cash effect in the cash T-account, classified as operating, investing, or financing

## Key Terms

- **Cash equivalents** — Short-term, highly liquid investments with original maturities of three months or less
- **Indirect method** — A method of presenting operating cash flows that begins with net income and adjusts for noncash items and working capital changes
- **Direct method** — A method of presenting operating cash flows that reports gross cash receipts and payments from operations
- **Noncash investing and financing activities** — Significant transactions that do not involve cash, disclosed in a supplemental schedule to the statement of cash flows
- **Operating activities** — Cash flows from the entity's core revenue-producing business operations
- **Investing activities** — Cash flows from the purchase and sale of long-term assets and investments
- **Financing activities** — Cash flows from transactions with the entity's owners and creditors
