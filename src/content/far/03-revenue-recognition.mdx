# Revenue Recognition

## Learning Objectives

- Apply the five-step revenue recognition model under ASC 606
- Identify and account for distinct performance obligations within a contract
- Determine the transaction price, including variable consideration and the constraint
- Recognize revenue at the appropriate point in time or over time

## Overview of ASC 606

**ASC 606, Revenue from Contracts with Customers**, replaced virtually all prior industry-specific revenue guidance with a single, principles-based framework. The core principle is straightforward: an entity recognizes revenue to depict the transfer of promised goods or services to customers in an amount that reflects the consideration the entity expects to be entitled to in exchange.

The standard applies to all contracts with customers except leases, insurance contracts, financial instruments, and certain nonmonetary exchanges.

## The Five-Step Model

### Step 1 — Identify the Contract with a Customer

A **contract** exists when all of the following criteria are met:

- The parties have approved the contract and are committed to their obligations
- Each party's rights regarding goods or services can be identified
- Payment terms can be identified
- The contract has **commercial substance** (future cash flows are expected to change)
- It is **probable** the entity will collect the consideration it is entitled to

If these criteria are not met at inception, the entity continues to reassess. Revenue is not recognized until a valid contract exists.

### Step 2 — Identify the Performance Obligations

A **performance obligation** is a promise to transfer a distinct good or service (or a series of distinct goods or services) to the customer. A good or service is distinct if:

1. The customer can **benefit** from it on its own or with readily available resources, and
2. The promise is **separately identifiable** from other promises in the contract

For example, a software company sells a license bundled with two years of customer support. The license and support are distinct performance obligations because the customer can benefit from each independently and they are not highly interrelated.

### Step 3 — Determine the Transaction Price

The **transaction price** is the amount of consideration the entity expects to be entitled to. It may include:

- **Fixed consideration** — A stated price in the contract
- **Variable consideration** — Discounts, rebates, refunds, performance bonuses, penalties, or rights of return
- **Noncash consideration** — Measured at fair value
- **Consideration payable to the customer** — Generally reduces the transaction price

**Variable consideration** is estimated using either the **expected value** method (probability-weighted amounts) or the **most likely amount** method, whichever better predicts the outcome. It is included in the transaction price only to the extent it is probable that a significant revenue reversal will not occur — this is the **constraint on variable consideration**.

> The constraint is a frequent exam topic. Remember: include variable consideration in the transaction price only when it is probable that a significant reversal will NOT occur when the uncertainty is resolved.

### Step 4 — Allocate the Transaction Price

When a contract has multiple performance obligations, the transaction price is allocated to each based on **relative standalone selling prices**. If a standalone selling price is not directly observable, the entity estimates it using:

- **Adjusted market assessment approach** — What would the market pay?
- **Expected cost plus a margin approach** — Forecast costs plus a reasonable margin
- **Residual approach** — Allowed only when the selling price is highly variable or uncertain

**Worked example:**

A company sells Product A and Service B in a bundle for $900. Standalone selling prices are $600 for Product A and $400 for Service B.

| Obligation | Standalone Price | Ratio | Allocated Price |
|------------|-----------------|-------|-----------------|
| Product A | $600 | 600/1,000 = 60% | $900 x 60% = **$540** |
| Service B | $400 | 400/1,000 = 40% | $900 x 40% = **$360** |
| **Total** | **$1,000** | **100%** | **$900** |

### Step 5 — Recognize Revenue

Revenue is recognized when (or as) a performance obligation is satisfied by transferring control of the good or service to the customer.

**Over time** recognition applies if any one of these criteria is met:

1. The customer simultaneously receives and consumes the benefits (e.g., routine cleaning services)
2. The entity's performance creates or enhances an asset the customer controls (e.g., building on customer land)
3. The entity's performance does not create an asset with alternative use, and the entity has an enforceable right to payment for work completed to date

If none of these criteria are met, revenue is recognized **at a point in time**, using indicators of control transfer such as physical possession, legal title, risks and rewards, and customer acceptance.

## Contract Costs

Under ASC 606, **incremental costs of obtaining a contract** (such as sales commissions) are capitalized as an asset if the entity expects to recover them. These costs are amortized on a systematic basis consistent with the transfer of goods or services. A practical expedient allows expensing these costs immediately if the amortization period is one year or less.

**Costs to fulfill a contract** are capitalized only if they relate directly to a specific contract, generate or enhance resources used to satisfy obligations, and are expected to be recovered.

## Key Terms

- **Performance obligation** — A promise in a contract to transfer a distinct good or service to the customer
- **Transaction price** — The amount of consideration an entity expects to be entitled to in exchange for transferring goods or services
- **Variable consideration** — Components of the transaction price that are contingent on future events, such as discounts, bonuses, or penalties
- **Standalone selling price** — The price at which an entity would sell a promised good or service separately to a customer
- **Constraint** — The requirement that variable consideration be included in the transaction price only when a significant reversal is not probable

## Practice Problems

**1.** A company enters a contract to deliver a product and provide one year of maintenance. The bundle price is $1,200. Standalone selling prices are $1,000 for the product and $500 for the maintenance. How much revenue is allocated to the product?

a) $1,000
b) $800
c) $600
d) $1,200

**Answer:** b) $800 — The total standalone selling price is $1,500 ($1,000 + $500). The product's ratio is $1,000 / $1,500 = 66.7%. Allocated amount: $1,200 x 66.7% = $800.

---

**2.** A construction company builds custom homes on land owned by the customer. Under ASC 606, when should the company recognize revenue?

a) At a point in time when the home is completed
b) Over time because the customer controls the asset as it is created
c) At a point in time when the customer takes physical possession
d) Over time because the contract term exceeds one year

**Answer:** b) Over time because the customer controls the asset as it is created — when the entity's performance creates or enhances an asset that the customer controls, revenue is recognized over time.

---

**3.** Under the constraint on variable consideration, an entity should include variable consideration in the transaction price when:

a) It is possible that the amount will be collected
b) The variable consideration is estimable
c) It is probable that a significant reversal of revenue will not occur
d) The customer has agreed to the variable terms

**Answer:** c) It is probable that a significant reversal of revenue will not occur — this is the core requirement of the constraint. The entity must be confident that including the estimate will not lead to a material reversal when uncertainty resolves.
