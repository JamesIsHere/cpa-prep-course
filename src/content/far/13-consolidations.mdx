# Consolidations

## Learning Objectives

- Understand when and why consolidated financial statements are required
- Apply the acquisition method for business combinations under ASC 805
- Account for noncontrolling interests in consolidated financial statements
- Distinguish between the equity method and full consolidation

## Business Combinations Under ASC 805

A **business combination** occurs when an acquirer obtains control of one or more businesses. Under **ASC 805**, all business combinations are accounted for using the **acquisition method**. The key steps are:

1. **Identify the acquirer** — The entity that obtains control of the acquiree
2. **Determine the acquisition date** — The date the acquirer obtains control
3. **Recognize and measure identifiable assets acquired, liabilities assumed, and any noncontrolling interest**
4. **Recognize and measure goodwill or a gain from a bargain purchase**

On the acquisition date, the acquirer measures all identifiable assets acquired and liabilities assumed at their **acquisition-date fair values**. This is true regardless of the percentage acquired — even a 60% acquisition requires measuring 100% of the acquiree's assets and liabilities at fair value.

### Goodwill Calculation

**Goodwill = Consideration transferred + Fair value of NCI + Fair value of previously held equity interest - Fair value of net identifiable assets**

If the fair value of net identifiable assets exceeds the total of consideration and NCI, the result is a **bargain purchase gain**, which is recognized immediately in earnings. Before recording a bargain purchase gain, the acquirer must reassess whether all assets and liabilities have been properly identified and measured.

### Worked Example

Parent Company acquires 80% of Subsidiary for $800,000 in cash. The fair value of Subsidiary's identifiable net assets is $900,000. The fair value of the 20% noncontrolling interest is $180,000.

- Goodwill = $800,000 + $180,000 - $900,000 = **$80,000**

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---------|-------|--------|
| Identifiable assets (at fair value) | $900,000 | |
| Goodwill | $80,000 | |
| Liabilities assumed (at fair value) | | (included in net assets) |
| Cash | | $800,000 |
| Noncontrolling interest | | $180,000 |

## Consolidation Procedures

Consolidated financial statements present the parent and its subsidiaries as a single economic entity. The consolidation process requires several adjustments:

### Elimination of Investment

The parent's investment account and the subsidiary's equity accounts are eliminated against each other. Any difference between the investment balance and the subsidiary's book value equity is allocated to fair value adjustments and goodwill.

### Elimination of Intercompany Transactions

All transactions between the parent and subsidiary must be eliminated to avoid double-counting:

- **Intercompany sales and purchases** — Eliminate the revenue and cost of goods sold
- **Intercompany receivables and payables** — Eliminate balances owed between entities
- **Intercompany profit in inventory** — If goods sold intercompany are still in the buyer's ending inventory, the unrealized profit must be eliminated
- **Intercompany profit in fixed assets** — If one entity sells a fixed asset to another at a gain, the gain and any excess depreciation must be eliminated
- **Intercompany dividends** — The parent's share of subsidiary dividends is eliminated

### Intercompany Inventory Profit Elimination

**Downstream sale** (parent sells to subsidiary) — 100% of the unrealized profit is eliminated against the parent's income.

**Upstream sale** (subsidiary sells to parent) — 100% of the unrealized profit is eliminated, but the effect is split between the parent's share and the noncontrolling interest's share.

**Example:** Parent sells inventory to Subsidiary for $50,000 (cost to Parent: $30,000). At year-end, 40% of the goods remain in Subsidiary's inventory.

- Unrealized profit = ($50,000 - $30,000) x 40% = **$8,000**
- Eliminate $8,000 of intercompany profit from consolidated income and reduce ending inventory by $8,000

## Noncontrolling Interest (NCI)

When a parent owns less than 100% of a subsidiary, the portion owned by outside shareholders is the **noncontrolling interest**. Under U.S. GAAP:

- NCI is measured at **fair value** on the acquisition date (the full goodwill method)
- NCI is presented as a separate component of **equity** on the consolidated balance sheet (not as a liability or mezzanine item)
- The NCI's share of the subsidiary's net income is reported as a separate line item on the consolidated income statement, below consolidated net income

**NCI's share of income = Subsidiary's adjusted net income x NCI ownership percentage**

Adjusted net income reflects any fair value amortization (e.g., amortization of identifiable intangible assets that were stepped up to fair value at acquisition).

> On the CPA exam, NCI questions frequently test whether you know to present NCI in equity (not liabilities) and whether you allocate the NCI's share of subsidiary income correctly after fair value adjustments.

## The Equity Method

The **equity method** applies when an investor has **significant influence** over an investee but does not have control. Significant influence is presumed when the investor holds **20% to 50%** of the voting stock.

Under the equity method:

- The investment is initially recorded at **cost**
- The investor recognizes its share of the investee's **net income** as an increase to the investment and as equity income
- **Dividends received** reduce the investment balance (they are a return of investment, not revenue)
- The investor amortizes any excess of cost over the investor's share of the investee's book value (typically attributable to identifiable intangible assets or goodwill)

**Example:** Investor acquires 30% of Investee for $300,000. Investee reports net income of $100,000 and pays dividends of $40,000.

| Transaction | Investment Balance |
|-------------|-------------------|
| Initial investment | $300,000 |
| Share of net income: $100,000 x 30% = $30,000 | $330,000 |
| Share of dividends: $40,000 x 30% = ($12,000) | $318,000 |

**Journal entries:**

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---------|-------|--------|
| Investment in Investee | $30,000 | |
| Equity in earnings of Investee | | $30,000 |
| Cash | $12,000 | |
| Investment in Investee | | $12,000 |

### Equity Method vs. Consolidation

| Feature | Equity Method | Full Consolidation |
|---------|--------------|-------------------|
| Ownership | 20-50% (significant influence) | >50% (control) |
| Financial statements | Single line on balance sheet and income statement | Line-by-line combination of all accounts |
| Intercompany eliminations | Investor's share of unrealized profits eliminated | All intercompany transactions eliminated |
| NCI presentation | Not applicable | Reported in equity and on the income statement |

## Variable Interest Entities (VIEs)

A **variable interest entity** is an entity in which the equity investors do not have sufficient equity at risk, or do not have the characteristics of a controlling financial interest through voting rights. ASC 810 requires that the **primary beneficiary** consolidate a VIE, even without majority voting ownership.

### Identifying a VIE

An entity is a VIE if any of these apply:
- Total equity at risk is **insufficient** to finance activities without additional subordinated financial support
- The equity investors as a group lack **decision-making power** (the power to direct activities that most significantly impact economic performance)
- The equity investors lack the obligation to **absorb losses** or the right to **receive returns**

### Identifying the Primary Beneficiary

The primary beneficiary is the entity that has **both**:
1. **Power** — The ability to direct the activities of the VIE that most significantly impact its economic performance
2. **Economics** — The obligation to absorb losses or the right to receive benefits that could potentially be significant

If one entity has power and another has significant economics, no single entity meets both criteria — a judgment call is required, often resulting in neither entity consolidating.

> VIE consolidation does NOT depend on voting ownership percentages. An entity can be required to consolidate a VIE it owns 0% of, or not consolidate an entity it owns 49% of. The test is power + economics, not votes.

### Common VIE Structures
- Special purpose entities (SPEs) used for securitization
- Joint ventures structured with insufficient equity
- Research and development arrangements
- Leasing entities with limited equity

## Step Acquisitions

A **step acquisition** occurs when an investor achieves control over an investee through multiple transactions over time (e.g., first acquires 25%, later acquires an additional 30% for majority control).

Under ASC 805:
- On the date control is obtained, the acquirer **remeasures** its previously held equity interest at **acquisition-date fair value**
- Any difference between fair value and the carrying amount of the previously held interest is recognized as a **gain or loss** in earnings
- The full acquisition is then accounted for using the acquisition method (as if 100% were acquired on that date, with NCI for the unowned portion)

**Example:** An investor holds 25% of an investee (equity method, carrying amount $200,000). It acquires an additional 35% for $400,000, gaining control. The fair value of the previously held 25% is $250,000.

- Gain on remeasurement = $250,000 - $200,000 = **$50,000** (recognized in earnings)
- Total consideration for goodwill calculation = $400,000 (new purchase) + $250,000 (remeasured old interest) = $650,000
- Proceed with standard acquisition method using $650,000 as total consideration for the 60% held

## Equity Method — Additional Topics

### Basis Differences

When an investor pays more than its proportionate share of the investee's book value, the excess is attributed to specific assets (fair value adjustments) and goodwill, similar to a full acquisition:

- **Excess allocated to depreciable/amortizable assets** — The investor amortizes its share of the excess against equity income over the asset's remaining life
- **Excess allocated to goodwill** — Not amortized, but tested for impairment
- **Excess allocated to inventory** — Recognized in the period the inventory is sold

**Example:** Investor pays $500,000 for 30% of Investee (book value $1,200,000, so investor's share of book = $360,000). The $140,000 excess is attributed: $100,000 to a building with 20 years remaining life, $40,000 to goodwill.

- Annual amortization of basis difference: $100,000 / 20 = **$5,000**
- Each year, equity income is reduced by $5,000 for the building amortization

### Equity Method Impairment

If the fair value of an equity method investment drops below its carrying amount, the investor must assess whether the decline is **other than temporary (OTTI)**. If the decline is deemed other than temporary, the investment is written down to fair value and the loss is recognized in earnings. Unlike goodwill for consolidated entities, equity method goodwill impairment follows the OTTI model.

### Equity Method Intercompany Transactions

- **Upstream** (investee sells to investor) — The investor eliminates its proportionate share of unrealized profit from equity income
- **Downstream** (investor sells to investee) — The investor eliminates its proportionate share of unrealized profit from equity income
- In both cases, profit is recognized when the goods are sold to an outside party

## Key Terms

- **Acquisition method** — The required method for accounting for business combinations, in which identifiable assets and liabilities are measured at fair value on the acquisition date
- **Noncontrolling interest** — The portion of a subsidiary's equity not attributable to the parent, presented as a separate component of consolidated equity
- **Goodwill** — The excess of the consideration transferred plus NCI fair value over the fair value of net identifiable assets acquired
- **Equity method** — A method of accounting for investments where the investor has significant influence; the investment is adjusted for the investor's share of income and dividends
- **Intercompany elimination** — The removal of transactions between a parent and subsidiary to prevent double-counting in consolidated financial statements
- **Bargain purchase** — A business combination in which the fair value of net identifiable assets exceeds the total consideration and NCI, resulting in a gain recognized in earnings
