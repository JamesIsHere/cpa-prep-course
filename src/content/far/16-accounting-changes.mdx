# Accounting Changes and Error Corrections

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish between changes in accounting principle, changes in estimate, and changes in reporting entity
- Apply the retrospective approach for changes in accounting principle under ASC 250
- Apply the prospective approach for changes in accounting estimate
- Account for prior period error corrections through restatement

## Overview of ASC 250

**ASC 250, Accounting Changes and Error Corrections**, establishes the framework for how entities report changes in their accounting and correct errors from prior periods. The standard identifies four categories, each with its own accounting treatment:

| Category | Treatment | Financial Statement Effect |
|----------|-----------|---------------------------|
| Change in accounting principle | Retrospective | Restate prior periods as if the new method had always been used |
| Change in accounting estimate | Prospective | Apply the new estimate in the current and future periods only |
| Change in reporting entity | Retrospective | Restate all prior periods presented |
| Error correction | Restatement | Adjust the beginning balance of retained earnings in the earliest period presented |

## Change in Accounting Principle

A **change in accounting principle** occurs when an entity adopts a different acceptable accounting method from the one previously used. Examples include:

- Switching from FIFO to weighted-average for inventory
- Changing from the completed-contract method to percentage-of-completion (prior to ASC 606)
- Adopting a new revenue recognition model

### Retrospective Application

The entity must apply the new principle **retrospectively** — as if the new method had always been used:

1. Adjust the **cumulative effect** on prior periods by restating beginning retained earnings of the earliest period presented
2. Restate all **comparative financial statements** presented as if the new principle had always been applied
3. Disclose the nature and reason for the change, the method of applying the change, and the effect on each financial statement line item

### Impracticability Exception

If it is **impracticable** to determine the cumulative effect for all prior periods, the entity applies the new principle from the earliest date practicable. A change is impracticable if the entity cannot determine the effects after making every reasonable effort.

### Worked Example — Inventory Method Change

A company switches from FIFO to weighted-average at the beginning of Year 3. The cumulative pre-tax effect on prior periods is a $60,000 reduction in inventory (and retained earnings). The tax rate is 25%.

After-tax cumulative effect = $60,000 × (1 − 0.25) = **$45,000**

Adjustment to opening retained earnings of the earliest period presented:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---------|-------|--------|
| Retained Earnings | $45,000 | |
| Deferred Tax Asset | $15,000 | |
| Inventory | | $60,000 |

All comparative income statements and balance sheets are restated to reflect the weighted-average method.

> On the CPA exam, look for the phrase "cumulative effect" — a change in principle restates retained earnings, while a change in estimate does not. This is a critical distinction.

## Change in Accounting Estimate

A **change in accounting estimate** reflects new information or developments that cause a revision of a previously used estimate. Common examples include:

- Revising the useful life of a depreciable asset
- Changing the estimated uncollectible percentage for accounts receivable
- Revising the estimated warranty liability
- Adjusting the salvage value of a fixed asset

### Prospective Application

Changes in estimate are accounted for **prospectively** — in the period of the change and future periods. Prior periods are **not** restated.

If a change in estimate is caused by a change in principle (e.g., switching depreciation methods, which is both a change in method and a revision of how the asset's benefits are consumed), it is treated as a **change in estimate** under ASC 250. The prospective approach applies.

### Worked Example — Useful Life Revision

A machine was purchased for $200,000 with a $20,000 salvage value and a 10-year useful life. After 4 years, the remaining useful life is revised from 6 years to 3 years.

Book value at end of Year 4 = $200,000 − [($200,000 − $20,000) / 10 × 4] = $200,000 − $72,000 = **$128,000**

New annual depreciation = ($128,000 − $20,000) / 3 = **$36,000** per year

No restatement of Years 1-4. The new depreciation applies to Years 5-7 only.

## Change in Reporting Entity

A **change in reporting entity** occurs when the financial statements are, in effect, the statements of a different entity. Examples include:

- Presenting consolidated statements instead of individual entity statements
- Changing the subsidiaries included in consolidated statements
- Changing the entities included in combined financial statements

The treatment is **retrospective** — all prior period financial statements are restated to reflect the new reporting entity, and the nature of and reason for the change must be disclosed.

## Error Corrections

An **error** is the result of a mistake in recognition, measurement, presentation, or disclosure in financial statements of a prior period. Errors include:

- Mathematical mistakes
- Mistakes in applying GAAP
- Oversights or misuse of facts that existed at the time the financial statements were prepared
- Changes from a non-GAAP method to a GAAP method (treated as error corrections, not changes in principle)

### Restatement Approach

Errors are corrected through **restatement** (also called a prior period adjustment):

1. Adjust the **beginning balance of retained earnings** in the earliest period presented for the cumulative effect of the error
2. Restate all **comparative financial statements** affected by the error
3. Disclose the nature of the error, the effect on each financial statement line item, and the effect on EPS

### Worked Example — Understated Depreciation

In Year 3, a company discovers that depreciation expense in Year 1 was understated by $30,000 (pre-tax). The tax rate is 25%.

After-tax effect = $30,000 × (1 − 0.25) = **$22,500**

Correcting entry in Year 3:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---------|-------|--------|
| Retained Earnings (prior period adjustment) | $22,500 | |
| Deferred Tax Asset | $7,500 | |
| Accumulated Depreciation | | $30,000 |

The Year 1 and Year 2 comparative statements are restated to reflect the correct depreciation.

## Distinguishing Between Changes and Errors

The distinction matters because the accounting treatment differs significantly:

| Situation | Classification | Treatment |
|-----------|---------------|-----------|
| Adopt a new FASB standard | Change in principle | Retrospective (unless the standard specifies otherwise) |
| Revise useful life of equipment | Change in estimate | Prospective |
| Switch depreciation methods | Change in estimate (effected by a change in principle) | Prospective |
| Discover prior year revenue was not recorded | Error correction | Restatement |
| Change from cash basis to accrual basis | Error correction | Restatement |
| Change subsidiaries included in consolidation | Change in reporting entity | Retrospective |

> A common exam trap: switching depreciation methods (e.g., double-declining balance to straight-line) is treated as a change in estimate, not a change in principle. It is accounted for prospectively.

## Key Terms

- **Retrospective application** — Applying a new accounting principle to prior periods as if the new method had always been used, including restating comparative financial statements
- **Prospective application** — Applying a change in estimate to the current and future periods only, with no restatement of prior periods
- **Cumulative effect** — The difference between retained earnings at the beginning of the period of change and what retained earnings would have been if the new principle had been applied to all prior periods
- **Prior period adjustment** — A correction of an error in previously issued financial statements, reported as an adjustment to beginning retained earnings
- **Restatement** — Revising previously issued financial statements to correct an error
- **Impracticability exception** — An exemption from full retrospective application when the entity cannot determine the effects despite every reasonable effort
