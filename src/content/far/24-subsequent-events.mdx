# Subsequent Events

## Learning Objectives

- Identify subsequent events and distinguish between recognized and nonrecognized events
- Apply the appropriate accounting treatment for each type of subsequent event
- Calculate required adjustments to financial statements based on subsequent events
- Determine required note disclosures for nonrecognized subsequent events
- Understand the evaluation period for subsequent events

## What Are Subsequent Events?

Subsequent events are events or transactions that occur **after the balance sheet date but before the financial statements are issued** (or available to be issued). ASC 855 governs the recognition and disclosure requirements.

The key question is always: **Does this event provide additional evidence about conditions that existed at the balance sheet date?**

## The Evaluation Period

### SEC Filers

Evaluate subsequent events through the date the financial statements are **issued** (filed with the SEC).

### Non-SEC Filers

Evaluate subsequent events through the date the financial statements are **available to be issued** — the date the statements are complete in a form and format that complies with GAAP and all approvals necessary for issuance have been obtained.

An entity must disclose the date through which subsequent events have been evaluated.

## Two Types of Subsequent Events

### Type I: Recognized Subsequent Events

These events provide **additional evidence about conditions that existed at the balance sheet date**. The financial statements must be **adjusted** to reflect the new information.

**Examples:**
- Settlement of litigation that was pending at the balance sheet date — confirms the loss existed and provides evidence of the amount
- Bankruptcy of a customer with an outstanding receivable at year-end — indicates the receivable was impaired at the balance sheet date
- Receipt of information indicating an asset was already impaired at the balance sheet date (e.g., inventory was obsolete)
- Discovery of fraud or errors that occurred in periods covered by the financial statements
- Resolution of a tax dispute for a period ending on or before the balance sheet date

**Accounting treatment:** Adjust the financial statements. Update the affected account balances, disclosures, and any related estimates.

**Journal entry example — Litigation settlement confirming year-end liability:**

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---------|-------|--------|
| Loss from Litigation | XXX | |
| Accrued Litigation Liability | | XXX |

If a contingent liability was already accrued at an estimated amount and the settlement reveals a different amount, adjust the accrual.

### Type II: Nonrecognized Subsequent Events

These events provide evidence about conditions that **did not exist at the balance sheet date** but arose subsequently. The financial statements are **not adjusted**, but disclosure may be required.

**Examples:**
- Fire or natural disaster occurring after year-end that destroys company assets
- Decline in market value of investments after year-end
- Issuance of debt or equity after the balance sheet date
- Business combination completed after year-end
- Loss of a major customer or supplier after year-end
- Government action (new regulation, trade restriction) occurring after year-end

**Accounting treatment:** Do not adjust the financial statements. If the event is significant enough that it would cause the statements to be misleading without disclosure, provide note disclosure including:
- Nature of the event
- Estimated financial effect (or a statement that an estimate cannot be made)

## Decision Framework

```
Event occurs after balance sheet date but before statements are issued/available to be issued?
│
├── YES → Did the condition exist at the balance sheet date?
│         │
│         ├── YES → Type I: ADJUST the financial statements
│         │
│         └── NO  → Type II: DISCLOSE if material (do not adjust)
│
└── NO  → Not a subsequent event (no action required)
```

## Calculating Adjustments

When a Type I event requires adjustment, the calculation depends on the nature of the underlying account:

### Receivable Impairment

If a customer declares bankruptcy after year-end and had an outstanding balance at year-end:

1. Assess the expected recovery amount
2. Adjust the allowance for credit losses to reflect the uncollectible portion
3. If the receivable was not previously reserved, record the full credit loss expense

### Litigation Accrual

If a lawsuit was pending at year-end and settled after year-end:

1. If no accrual existed: record the loss and liability
2. If an accrual existed at a different amount: adjust to the settlement amount
3. Consider whether the settlement also affects related insurance recoveries

### Inventory Write-Down

If information received after year-end reveals inventory was already obsolete at the balance sheet date:

1. Write down inventory to net realizable value as of the balance sheet date
2. The subsequent event confirms a condition that existed — it does not create a new condition

## Special Considerations

### Going Concern

If subsequent events raise substantial doubt about the entity's ability to continue as a going concern, management must evaluate whether mitigating plans are sufficient. This may require:
- Note disclosure of the conditions and management's plans
- In severe cases, adjustment of asset carrying amounts to liquidation values

### Dividends Declared After Year-End

Dividends declared after the balance sheet date are **not** adjusted — they are a Type II event. However, note disclosure is required. The liability is not recorded until the declaration date.

### Stock Splits and Stock Dividends

Stock splits or stock dividends occurring after year-end but before issuance require **retroactive adjustment** of earnings per share calculations — but this is a separate requirement under ASC 260, not an ASC 855 adjustment.

### Reissuance of Financial Statements

If financial statements are reissued (not revised for SEC filers), the entity must evaluate subsequent events through the **new issuance date**. However, for non-SEC filers, the entity is not required to evaluate events after the original available-to-be-issued date.

## Key Exam Tips

1. **The condition-at-balance-sheet-date test is everything** — if the condition existed at year-end, adjust; if it arose after, disclose only
2. **Know the evaluation period** — SEC filers evaluate through issuance date; others through available-to-be-issued date
3. **Litigation settlements are the classic Type I example** — the lawsuit existed at year-end, the settlement confirms the amount
4. **Fires and disasters after year-end are Type II** — the asset existed, but the destruction did not
5. **Dividends declared after year-end are never adjusted** — the board action creates the obligation, not the passage of time
6. **Disclose the evaluation date** — required for all entities under ASC 855
