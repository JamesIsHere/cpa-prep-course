# SOC Testing Controls

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish between testing the design effectiveness and the operating effectiveness of controls in a SOC engagement
- Identify the four primary nature-of-testing procedures: inquiry, observation, inspection, and reperformance
- Explain how sampling is applied in SOC engagements, including population definition and sample selection methods
- Evaluate test results and determine whether deviations constitute exceptions
- Apply a risk-based approach to selecting which controls to test and the extent of testing
- Describe the documentation requirements for SOC testing procedures and results

## Design Effectiveness vs. Operating Effectiveness

SOC engagements evaluate controls at two levels, and the distinction is fundamental to understanding what each SOC report type communicates.

### Design Effectiveness

**Design effectiveness** asks: "Is the control properly designed to achieve its objective if it operates as described?" This evaluation determines whether the control, as designed, would prevent or detect the risks it is intended to address.

A control has effective design when:
- It addresses the **specific risk** or trust services criterion it is mapped to
- The control logic is **sound** — if performed as described, it would actually prevent or detect the issue
- The control is **placed** at the right point in the process to be effective
- The control is supported by adequate **documentation** (policies, procedures, configurations)

| Test Focus | Question Answered | Report Type |
|------------|-------------------|-------------|
| Design only | Is the control suitably designed? | SOC Type I |
| Design + operating effectiveness | Is the control suitably designed AND operating effectively? | SOC Type II |

> **Exam tip:** A SOC 1 or SOC 2 Type I report evaluates ONLY design effectiveness at a point in time. A Type II report evaluates design AND operating effectiveness over a period (typically 6-12 months). The exam may present a scenario where a user auditor is evaluating a service organization's controls and ask which report provides more assurance. Type II always provides more assurance because it tests both design and operation.

### Operating Effectiveness

**Operating effectiveness** asks: "Did the control actually operate as designed, consistently, throughout the examination period?" This requires testing whether the control was applied by the right people, at the right time, with the right frequency, and produced the intended results.

A control operates effectively when:
- It was performed **consistently** throughout the period
- It was performed by **authorized personnel** with appropriate competence
- It was performed at the **prescribed frequency** (daily, weekly, monthly, per-transaction)
- **Deviations** were identified, investigated, and corrected in a timely manner
- The control produced the **intended outcome** (prevented unauthorized access, detected errors, etc.)

**Worked Example — Design vs. Operating Effectiveness:**

Control description: "The IT Security Manager reviews user access rights quarterly and removes accounts for terminated employees within 24 hours of notification."

Design effectiveness assessment: The control is suitably designed because quarterly reviews would detect inappropriate access, and 24-hour removal prevents terminated employees from accessing systems. The control maps to the Security trust services criterion.

Operating effectiveness assessment: The auditor selects a sample of quarters during the examination period and tests whether:
- Quarterly reviews were actually performed (evidence of review documentation)
- Terminated employee accounts were removed within 24 hours (compare HR termination dates to system access removal dates)
- Any exceptions were identified and how they were resolved

## Nature of Tests

SOC practitioners use four primary types of testing procedures, ordered from least to most persuasive:

### 1. Inquiry

**Inquiry** involves asking the service organization's personnel about how controls operate. It is the **least persuasive** form of evidence and is almost never sufficient alone for operating effectiveness.

- **Use:** Gain understanding of control design and operation
- **Limitation:** Management's statements are self-serving; must be corroborated
- **When sufficient alone:** Rarely — typically only for understanding context, not for reaching conclusions on effectiveness

### 2. Observation

**Observation** involves watching a control being performed in real time. It provides evidence that the control operated at the specific moment observed.

- **Use:** Controls that leave no documentary evidence (physical security checks, verbal approvals)
- **Limitation:** Only proves the control operated at one point in time; the control may not operate consistently
- **Example:** Observing a security guard checking employee badges at the data center entrance

### 3. Inspection

**Inspection** involves examining documents, records, system configurations, or other tangible evidence that a control operated. This is the most common testing procedure.

- **Use:** Controls that produce documentary evidence (approval signatures, system logs, review checklists)
- **Limitation:** Documents can be falsified; must consider authenticity
- **Examples:** Reviewing change management tickets for evidence of approval, examining system access reports for evidence of quarterly reviews, inspecting firewall configuration settings

### 4. Reperformance

**Reperformance** involves the auditor independently executing the control procedure to verify it produces the expected results. It is the **most persuasive** form of evidence.

- **Use:** Complex controls, automated controls, high-risk areas, or when other procedures are insufficient
- **Limitation:** Time-consuming and may require technical expertise
- **Examples:** Re-running an automated access provisioning workflow to verify it enforces role-based restrictions, recalculating a reconciliation to verify mathematical accuracy

| Procedure | Persuasiveness | Evidence Type | Best For |
|-----------|---------------|---------------|----------|
| Inquiry | Lowest | Testimonial | Understanding design and context |
| Observation | Low-Medium | Direct | Controls without documentation trail |
| Inspection | Medium-High | Documentary | Most manual and IT controls |
| Reperformance | Highest | Direct + Documentary | Complex, automated, or high-risk controls |

> **Exam tip:** The exam often tests the hierarchy of evidence persuasiveness. Inquiry alone is NEVER sufficient for testing operating effectiveness. At minimum, inquiry must be combined with one or more of the other procedures. Reperformance provides the strongest evidence. If the exam asks which single procedure provides the MOST reliable evidence, the answer is reperformance.

## Extent of Testing and Sampling

The extent of testing — how many items are tested — is a critical judgment in SOC engagements. The practitioner must obtain sufficient evidence that controls operated effectively throughout the entire examination period.

### Population Definition

The **population** is the complete set of items over which the control operated during the examination period. Proper population definition is essential for valid sampling.

| Control Type | Population | Example |
|-------------|------------|---------|
| Per-transaction | All transactions processed | All change requests submitted (e.g., 2,400 changes over 12 months) |
| Daily | All business days | 260 business days (52 weeks x 5 days) |
| Weekly | All weeks | 52 weeks |
| Monthly | All months | 12 months |
| Quarterly | All quarters | 4 quarters |
| Annual | All occurrences | 1 (test the single occurrence) |

### Sample Selection Methods

| Method | Description | When Used |
|--------|-------------|-----------|
| **Random selection** | Each item has an equal chance of being selected | Default method for large populations |
| **Systematic selection** | Every nth item after a random start | Alternative to random when ordered populations exist |
| **Haphazard selection** | Auditor selects without bias but without mathematical randomness | Acceptable when population is small or unstructured |
| **Block selection** | All items from a contiguous time block | NOT recommended as the primary method (does not test the full period) |

### Sample Size Guidance

While there is no single mandated sample size for SOC engagements, common practice follows these general guidelines:

| Control Frequency | Typical Sample Size | Rationale |
|-------------------|--------------------|-----------|
| Multiple times per day | 25-60 items | Large population; need representative coverage |
| Daily | 20-40 items | Moderate population; ensure period coverage |
| Weekly | 10-20 items | Smaller population; most or all weeks tested |
| Monthly | 2-5 items (often all 12) | Small population; test all if practical |
| Quarterly | All 4 occurrences | Very small population; test 100% |
| Annual | 1 (the single occurrence) | Test the one time it was performed |

> **Exam tip:** The exam may describe a control that operates daily and ask about an appropriate sample size. Key principle: the sample must be large enough to provide reasonable assurance that the control operated effectively throughout the entire period. Testing 5 out of 260 daily occurrences is insufficient. Testing 25-40 is typical practice.

**Worked Example — Sample Size Determination:**

A SOC 2 Type II engagement covers a 12-month period. Control C-4.1 requires the IT Manager to review the system access report weekly and investigate anomalies. Population = 52 weekly reviews.

Approach: Select a sample of 15 weeks spread across the full 12-month period. For each selected week, inspect the access report and evidence of the IT Manager's review and investigation of anomalies (signatures, email confirmations, ticketing system entries).

## Evaluating Test Results and Deviations

A **deviation** occurs when a control did not operate as described for a specific item in the sample. Not every deviation is an **exception** that impacts the SOC report opinion.

### Evaluating Deviations

When a deviation is identified, the practitioner must evaluate:

1. **Nature of the deviation** — Was the control completely absent, partially performed, or performed by the wrong person?
2. **Cause of the deviation** — Was it a one-time error, a systemic issue, or a change in process?
3. **Compensating controls** — Does another control mitigate the risk created by the deviation?
4. **Impact on the trust services criterion** — Does the deviation mean the criterion was not met?
5. **Frequency of deviations** — Is this an isolated instance or a pattern?

### Deviation vs. Exception

| Term | Definition | Report Impact |
|------|-----------|---------------|
| **Deviation** | A control did not operate as described for a specific test item | May or may not result in an exception |
| **Exception** | A deviation that the practitioner concludes represents a failure of the control to meet its objective | Disclosed in the SOC report with description of test, deviation, and management's response |

**Worked Example — Evaluating a Deviation:**

During testing of weekly access reviews (15-item sample), the auditor finds that for 2 of the 15 weeks, the access report was reviewed 3 days late instead of within the prescribed 1-day window.

Evaluation: The reviews were still performed (not missed entirely). The delay was 3 days, not weeks or months. The IT Manager documented the reason (vacation during those weeks, with review performed immediately upon return). No unauthorized access occurred during the gap.

Conclusion: The practitioner may determine this is a deviation that does not rise to the level of an exception, particularly if compensating controls (automated access restrictions, IDS monitoring) were operating. However, the deviation should still be documented in the working papers.

> **Exam tip:** One or two minor deviations in a sample do not automatically result in a qualified opinion. The practitioner evaluates the nature, cause, and impact of deviations. However, if deviations are pervasive or indicate a systemic failure, the control is reported as an exception.

## Risk-Based Approach to Test Selection

Not all controls receive the same level of testing. A **risk-based approach** allocates more testing effort to controls that address higher risks and are more critical to the trust services criteria.

### Factors Increasing Testing Rigor

| Factor | Effect on Testing |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Higher inherent risk** | Larger sample sizes, more persuasive procedures |
| **Manual control** (vs. automated) | Must test across the period; more prone to human error |
| **New or changed control** | Additional testing to verify the new control is operating |
| **Prior period exceptions** | Increased scrutiny and larger samples |
| **Complex control logic** | May require reperformance rather than inspection |
| **Key control** (no compensating controls) | Must test thoroughly; no fallback if this control fails |

### Automated vs. Manual Controls

| Feature | Automated Controls | Manual Controls |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Consistency | Inherently consistent if properly configured | Subject to human error and judgment |
| Testing approach | Test configuration + limited reperformance; ITGCs must be effective | Test sample across the full period |
| Sample size | Smaller (if ITGCs effective, one successful test may suffice) | Larger (must demonstrate operation throughout the period) |
| Dependency | Relies on IT general controls (change management, access controls) | Relies on competence and diligence of personnel |

> **Exam tip:** If the exam describes an automated control (such as system-enforced password complexity), the key question is whether IT general controls are effective. If ITGCs are effective, the automated control is expected to operate consistently throughout the period, reducing the extent of testing needed. If ITGCs are NOT effective, the auditor cannot rely on the consistency of automated controls.

## Documentation Requirements

SOC engagement documentation must be sufficient for an experienced practitioner to understand the tests performed, the evidence obtained, and the conclusions reached.

### Required Documentation Elements

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Control objective/criterion** | The trust services criterion or control objective being addressed |
| **Control description** | How management describes the control's operation |
| **Test procedure** | The specific nature, timing, and extent of the test |
| **Population and sample** | The defined population, sample selection method, and sample size rationale |
| **Evidence obtained** | The specific documents, configurations, or observations reviewed |
| **Results** | Outcome of each test item (pass or deviation) |
| **Deviations** | Description of any deviations, their nature, cause, and evaluation |
| **Conclusion** | Whether the control was operating effectively, with any exceptions noted |

### Working Paper Best Practices

- **Tie each test** to a specific control listed in management's description
- **Document the population** completely (source, size, period covered)
- **Retain evidence** (screenshots, reports, configuration exports) to support conclusions
- **Cross-reference** to the trust services criteria matrix
- **Date and sign** all working papers

## Key Terms

- **Design effectiveness** — Whether a control is suitably designed to achieve its stated objective if it operates as described
- **Operating effectiveness** — Whether a control actually operated as designed, consistently and by authorized personnel, throughout the examination period
- **Inquiry** — A testing procedure involving questioning personnel about how controls operate; least persuasive evidence
- **Observation** — A testing procedure involving watching a control being performed in real time; provides point-in-time evidence
- **Inspection** — A testing procedure involving examining documents, records, or system configurations for evidence that a control operated
- **Reperformance** — A testing procedure involving the auditor independently executing the control to verify it produces expected results; most persuasive evidence
- **Population** — The complete set of items over which a control operated during the examination period
- **Sample** — A subset of the population selected for testing to draw conclusions about the entire population
- **Deviation** — An instance where a control did not operate as described for a specific test item
- **Exception** — A deviation that the practitioner concludes represents a failure of the control to meet its objective, disclosed in the SOC report
- **Compensating control** — An alternative control that mitigates the risk when the primary control fails or has a deviation
- **ITGC dependency** — The reliance of automated application controls on the effectiveness of underlying IT general controls

## Practice Problems

**1.** A SOC 2 Type II engagement is testing a control that requires the Security Operations Center to review system alerts daily. The examination period is 12 months. Which approach provides the MOST appropriate evidence of operating effectiveness?

a) Inquire of the SOC manager whether daily reviews were performed throughout the year
b) Observe the SOC team performing the daily review on the day of the auditor's site visit
c) Inspect documentation of daily alert reviews for a sample of 25 days spread across the 12-month period
d) Reperform the daily review for one day and extrapolate the results to the full year

**Answer:** c) Inspection of documentation for a sample spread across the full period provides appropriate evidence of operating effectiveness. Inquiry alone (a) is never sufficient for operating effectiveness. Observation (b) only proves the control operated on one day. Reperformance of a single day (d) does not demonstrate consistent operation throughout the period.

---

**2.** During testing of a monthly reconciliation control (12 occurrences in the examination period), the auditor finds that one month's reconciliation was prepared but not reviewed by the supervisor as required. Which of the following BEST describes how this should be evaluated?

a) The single deviation automatically results in a qualified opinion because the control failed
b) The deviation should be evaluated for its nature, cause, and whether compensating controls mitigate the risk before determining if it is an exception
c) The deviation should be ignored because one out of twelve is an acceptable error rate
d) The entire population must be tested because sampling has revealed an error

**Answer:** b) The practitioner must evaluate the nature and cause of the deviation, whether it represents an isolated event or systemic issue, and whether compensating controls exist. A single deviation does not automatically result in a qualified opinion (a) nor should it be ignored (c). Retesting the full population (d) is not required for a single deviation in a small population that was already fully tested.

---

**3.** An automated system control enforces password complexity requirements (minimum 12 characters, uppercase, lowercase, number, special character). IT general controls over program changes are effective. What is the MOST appropriate approach for testing operating effectiveness of this control?

a) Select a sample of 25 user accounts created during the period and verify each password met the complexity requirements
b) Inspect the system configuration settings once and perform one reperformance test to confirm the control operates as configured
c) Inquire of the IT administrator whether the password policy has been in effect throughout the period
d) Observe a new user creating an account to verify the system enforces the password requirements

**Answer:** b) For automated controls where ITGCs are effective, the control operates consistently once properly configured. Testing involves inspecting the configuration and performing limited reperformance (e.g., attempting to create a non-compliant password to verify rejection). There is no need for extensive sampling (a) because the control's consistency depends on its configuration, not on human execution. Inquiry alone (c) and single observation (d) are insufficient.
