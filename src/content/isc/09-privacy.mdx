# Privacy Requirements and Data Protection

## Learning Objectives

- Identify foundational privacy principles that underpin major privacy regulations
- Understand GDPR scope, key requirements, data subject rights, and penalties
- Compare CCPA/CPRA consumer rights and opt-out requirements
- Recognize HIPAA requirements for protected health information
- Apply privacy by design concepts and understand cross-border data transfer mechanisms

## Privacy Principles

Privacy regulations worldwide share a common foundation of **fair information practice principles (FIPPs)**. Understanding these principles provides a framework for analyzing any privacy law.

| Principle | Description |
|---|---|
| Notice | Organizations must inform individuals about their data collection and use practices |
| Choice/Consent | Individuals must have the ability to agree to or decline the collection and use of their data |
| Collection Limitation | Only data that is necessary for the stated purpose should be collected |
| Use Limitation | Data should only be used for the purpose for which it was collected |
| Access | Individuals should be able to view, correct, or delete their personal data |
| Security | Organizations must protect personal data with reasonable security measures |
| Accountability | Organizations are responsible for complying with privacy principles and must be able to demonstrate compliance |

> **Exam tip:** These principles appear across all major privacy frameworks. When evaluating a privacy scenario on the exam, identify which principle is being violated — this often points directly to the correct answer.

## GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation)

The **GDPR** is the European Union's comprehensive data protection law, effective May 2018. It is widely considered the most stringent privacy regulation globally and has influenced privacy laws worldwide.

### Scope

GDPR applies to any organization that:
- Is established in the EU, **or**
- Offers goods or services to individuals in the EU, **or**
- Monitors the behavior of individuals in the EU

This means GDPR can apply to U.S. companies that have EU customers, even if the company has no physical presence in Europe.

### Key Roles

| Role | Definition |
|---|---|
| Data subject | The individual whose personal data is being processed |
| Data controller | The entity that determines the purposes and means of processing personal data |
| Data processor | The entity that processes personal data on behalf of the controller |
| Data Protection Officer (DPO) | Required for organizations that process personal data at scale or handle sensitive data; oversees compliance |

### Data Subject Rights

GDPR grants individuals eight fundamental rights:

1. **Right to be informed** — Know how data is collected and used
2. **Right of access** — Obtain a copy of personal data being processed
3. **Right to rectification** — Correct inaccurate personal data
4. **Right to erasure** ("right to be forgotten") — Request deletion of personal data under certain conditions
5. **Right to restrict processing** — Limit how data is used
6. **Right to data portability** — Receive data in a structured, machine-readable format
7. **Right to object** — Object to processing for direct marketing or legitimate interests
8. **Rights related to automated decision-making** — Not be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing

### Penalties

GDPR violations can result in fines up to the **greater of 20 million euros or 4% of annual global turnover**. Organizations must report data breaches to the supervisory authority within **72 hours** of becoming aware.

> **Exam tip:** The 72-hour breach notification requirement and the two-tier penalty structure (up to 2% for administrative violations, up to 4% for core violations) are heavily tested. Remember that GDPR has extraterritorial reach — it applies based on where the data subjects are, not where the company is located.

## CCPA/CPRA (California Consumer Privacy Act / California Privacy Rights Act)

The CCPA (effective 2020) and its amendment, the CPRA (effective 2023), establish privacy rights for California residents and impose obligations on businesses that collect their personal information.

### Scope

CCPA/CPRA applies to for-profit businesses that collect California residents' personal information **and** meet any of these thresholds:
- Annual gross revenue exceeding $25 million
- Buy, sell, or share the personal information of 100,000+ consumers or households
- Derive 50% or more of annual revenue from selling or sharing personal information

### Consumer Rights

| Right | Description |
|---|---|
| Right to know | What personal information is collected, used, shared, or sold |
| Right to delete | Request deletion of personal information |
| Right to opt-out | Direct a business to stop selling or sharing personal information |
| Right to non-discrimination | Cannot be penalized for exercising privacy rights |
| Right to correct | Correct inaccurate personal information (added by CPRA) |
| Right to limit use of sensitive PI | Restrict use of sensitive categories like SSN, financial data, geolocation (added by CPRA) |

Businesses must provide a clear **"Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information"** link on their website.

## HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act)

HIPAA establishes national standards for protecting **protected health information (PHI)** — any individually identifiable health information held or transmitted by a covered entity or business associate.

### Key Entities

| Entity | Examples |
|---|---|
| Covered entities | Health plans, healthcare providers, healthcare clearinghouses |
| Business associates | Third parties that handle PHI on behalf of covered entities (cloud providers, billing companies, IT consultants) |

### HIPAA Rules

| Rule | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Privacy Rule | Establishes standards for who can access PHI and under what circumstances |
| Security Rule | Requires administrative, physical, and technical safeguards to protect electronic PHI (ePHI) |
| Breach Notification Rule | Requires notification to affected individuals, HHS, and potentially media within 60 days of discovering a breach |

### HIPAA Key Terms

Understanding HIPAA requires familiarity with its core terminology:

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Protected Health Information (PHI)** | Individually identifiable health information held or transmitted by a covered entity or business associate, in any form (paper, electronic, oral) |
| **Electronic PHI (ePHI)** | PHI that is created, stored, transmitted, or received in electronic form — the specific focus of the HIPAA Security Rule |
| **Covered Entity** | Health plans, healthcare providers who transmit health information electronically, and healthcare clearinghouses |
| **Business Associate** | Any person or entity that performs functions involving the use or disclosure of PHI on behalf of a covered entity |
| **Minimum Necessary Standard** | Covered entities must limit PHI access, use, and disclosure to the minimum amount necessary to accomplish the intended purpose |

The **minimum necessary standard** is a key operational requirement: employees should only access the PHI needed for their specific job function. This principle drives role-based access controls, segmented data views, and audit logging in healthcare IT systems.

### Required Safeguards

- **Administrative** — Risk assessments, workforce training, access management policies, business associate agreements
- **Physical** — Facility access controls, workstation security, device and media controls
- **Technical** — Access controls, audit controls, integrity controls, transmission security

> **Exam tip:** HIPAA requires a **Business Associate Agreement (BAA)** before any third party can handle PHI. The absence of a BAA is itself a HIPAA violation. The exam tests this concept frequently in scenarios involving cloud service providers or outsourced billing.

## Privacy by Design and Privacy Impact Assessments

**Privacy by design** embeds privacy protections into systems and processes from the outset, rather than adding them as an afterthought. Its seven foundational principles include:

1. Proactive, not reactive — anticipate and prevent privacy issues
2. Privacy as the default setting — no action required from the individual
3. Privacy embedded into design — integral to the system architecture
4. Full functionality — positive-sum, not zero-sum (privacy and functionality coexist)
5. End-to-end security — protect data throughout its entire lifecycle
6. Visibility and transparency — keep practices open and verifiable
7. Respect for user privacy — keep the user's interests central

A **Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA)** is a systematic evaluation conducted before launching a new system, product, or process that involves personal data. The PIA identifies privacy risks, evaluates their likelihood and impact, and recommends mitigating controls.

## Cross-Border Data Transfers

When personal data crosses international borders, additional legal mechanisms are required to ensure adequate protection. This is particularly relevant under GDPR.

| Mechanism | Description |
|---|---|
| Adequacy decisions | The European Commission determines that a country provides adequate data protection (e.g., Japan, UK, South Korea) |
| Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) | Pre-approved contractual terms between data exporters and importers that ensure data protection obligations |
| Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs) | Internal policies approved by supervisory authorities for multinational companies transferring data within their corporate group |
| Derogations | Specific exceptions such as explicit consent or contractual necessity (used only in limited circumstances) |

> **Exam tip:** After the Schrems II decision invalidated the EU-U.S. Privacy Shield, SCCs became the primary mechanism for EU-to-U.S. data transfers. The EU-U.S. Data Privacy Framework was adopted in 2023 as a replacement. Know that cross-border transfers require a valid legal mechanism — companies cannot simply transfer data internationally without one.
