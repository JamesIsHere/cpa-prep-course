# International Tax

## Learning Objectives

- Identify Controlled Foreign Corporation (CFC) rules and Subpart F income categories
- Compute Global Intangible Low-Taxed Income (GILTI) and Foreign-Derived Intangible Income (FDII)
- Apply the foreign tax credit limitation formula and basket system
- Understand transfer pricing principles, tax treaty provisions, and the BEAT

## Controlled Foreign Corporations (CFCs)

A **Controlled Foreign Corporation** is any foreign corporation in which **U.S. shareholders** own more than 50% of the total combined voting power or value of stock. A U.S. shareholder is any U.S. person owning at least **10%** of the voting power or value of the foreign corporation's stock.

CFC shareholders must include their pro rata share of **Subpart F income** in gross income currently, regardless of whether any distribution is made.

### Subpart F Income Categories

| Category | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| Foreign base company sales income | Income from buying/selling property involving a related party where the CFC is in a different country than the property's origin or destination |
| Foreign base company services income | Services performed by the CFC for or on behalf of a related person outside the CFC's country of incorporation |
| Foreign personal holding company income | Dividends, interest, rents, royalties, and net gains from property transactions |
| Insurance income | Income from insuring risks outside the CFC's country of organization |

> **Exam tip:** Subpart F income does NOT include income earned by a CFC from operations within its own country of incorporation. The "base company" concept targets income artificially shifted to low-tax jurisdictions.

## Global Intangible Low-Taxed Income (GILTI)

GILTI is a minimum tax on the earnings of CFCs that exceed a routine return on tangible assets. U.S. shareholders of CFCs must include GILTI annually.

### GILTI Computation

| Step | Item | Description |
|------|------|-------------|
| 1 | Tested income | CFC's gross income minus allocable deductions, excluding Subpart F income, high-taxed income, and U.S. effectively connected income |
| 2 | Net CFC tested income | Aggregate tested income minus aggregate tested losses across all CFCs |
| 3 | Net deemed tangible income return (DTIR) | 10% of qualified business asset investment (QBAI) minus interest expense |
| 4 | GILTI inclusion | Net CFC tested income minus Net DTIR |

**Worked Example:**

A U.S. shareholder's CFC has tested income of $2,000,000 and QBAI of $8,000,000 with $50,000 of specified interest expense.

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| Net CFC tested income | | $2,000,000 |
| DTIR (10% of QBAI) | $8,000,000 x 10% | $800,000 |
| Net DTIR | $800,000 - $50,000 interest | $750,000 |
| **GILTI inclusion** | $2,000,000 - $750,000 | **$1,250,000** |

### Section 250 Deduction

Corporate U.S. shareholders may deduct **37.5%** of GILTI for tax years beginning after 2025 (down from the original 50% deduction for 2018–2025). This effectively taxes GILTI at **13.125%** before foreign tax credits.

> **Exam tip:** The Section 250 deduction is available only to C corporations. Individual shareholders of CFCs receive no Section 250 deduction unless they make a Section 962 election to be taxed as if they were a corporation.

## Foreign-Derived Intangible Income (FDII)

FDII encourages U.S. corporations to keep intangible-related income in the United States. It applies to income earned domestically from serving foreign markets.

### FDII Computation

| Step | Calculation |
|------|------------|
| 1 | Deduction-eligible income (DEI) = Gross income minus excluded items and allocable deductions |
| 2 | Deemed intangible income (DII) = DEI minus (10% x QBAI) |
| 3 | Foreign-derived ratio = Foreign-derived DEI / Total DEI |
| 4 | FDII = DII x Foreign-derived ratio |
| 5 | Section 250 deduction = 37.5% of FDII (effective rate: 13.125%) |

FDII and GILTI are designed as two sides of one framework: FDII rewards keeping operations onshore, while GILTI penalizes shifting profits offshore.

## Foreign Tax Credit

U.S. persons may claim a **credit** against U.S. tax for foreign income taxes paid or accrued, preventing double taxation of the same income.

### Limitation Formula

The credit cannot exceed the U.S. tax attributable to foreign-source income:

**FTC Limitation = U.S. tax liability x (Foreign-source taxable income / Worldwide taxable income)**

**Worked Example:**

A U.S. corporation has worldwide taxable income of $1,000,000, of which $400,000 is foreign-source. U.S. tax liability before credits is $210,000 (21%). Foreign taxes paid: $100,000.

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| FTC limitation | $210,000 x ($400,000 / $1,000,000) | $84,000 |
| Foreign taxes paid | | $100,000 |
| **Credit allowed** | Lesser of $100,000 or $84,000 | **$84,000** |
| Excess credit | $100,000 - $84,000 | $16,000 (carryforward) |

### Baskets and Carryovers

Credits are computed separately for two **baskets**:

| Basket | Income Types |
|--------|-------------|
| General category | Active business income, branch profits |
| Passive category | Dividends, interest, rents, royalties, capital gains from passive investments |

Excess foreign tax credits carry back **one year** and forward **ten years** within the same basket.

> **Exam tip:** Cross-crediting between baskets is not allowed. Excess credits in the passive basket cannot offset a limitation in the general basket. The exam tests whether income is classified into the correct basket.

## Transfer Pricing — Section 482

The IRS may reallocate income among related entities to clearly reflect income using the **arm's length standard**: related-party transactions must produce results consistent with those between unrelated parties. Common methods include comparable uncontrolled price (CUP), resale price, cost plus, comparable profits method (CPM), and profit split. Taxpayers must maintain **contemporaneous documentation** to avoid a **20% penalty** for substantial misstatements and a **40% penalty** for gross misstatements.

> Transfer pricing is covered at a high level on the TCP section. The key concept is the arm's length standard — related-party transactions must produce results consistent with those between unrelated parties.

## Tax Treaties and BEAT

### Tax Treaties

Bilateral treaties reduce withholding rates and prevent double taxation. Key concepts:

- **Permanent establishment (PE):** A fixed place of business that creates taxing jurisdiction; treaty protection prevents taxation without a PE
- **Reduced withholding rates:** Treaties commonly reduce withholding on dividends (often to 5-15%), interest (often to 0-10%), and royalties
- **Treaty override:** A later-enacted statute may override a treaty provision under the "last in time" rule

### Base Erosion and Anti-Abuse Tax (BEAT)

BEAT is a minimum tax targeting large corporations making deductible payments to foreign related parties.

| Element | Requirement |
|---------|------------|
| Applicable taxpayer | Average annual gross receipts of $500 million+ over 3 years, and base erosion percentage of 3%+ (2%+ for banks/dealers) |
| Base erosion payments | Deductible amounts paid to foreign related parties (interest, royalties, services, depreciation on acquired assets) |
| Tax computation | 10% of modified taxable income (taxable income computed without base erosion tax benefits and certain credits) minus regular tax liability |

The BEAT applies only when the BEAT amount exceeds the corporation's regular tax liability. It operates as an alternative minimum tax specifically for base erosion.
