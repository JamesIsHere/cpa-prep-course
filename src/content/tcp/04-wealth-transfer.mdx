# Wealth Transfer and Gifting Strategies

## Learning Objectives

- Calculate the gift tax using the annual exclusion, lifetime exemption, and unified credit
- Compute the estate tax including the gross estate, deductions, and applicable credits
- Identify generation-skipping transfer tax triggers and exemption amounts
- Apply valuation discount strategies for closely held business interests
- Evaluate grantor trust structures for wealth transfer planning

## Gift Tax Framework

The federal gift tax applies to transfers of property for less than full and adequate consideration. The tax is imposed on the **donor**, not the donee, and is computed on a cumulative lifetime basis.

### Annual Exclusion

Each donor may give up to **$18,000 per donee per year** (2024) without any gift tax consequences. This exclusion applies only to gifts of a **present interest** — the donee must have an immediate, unrestricted right to use or enjoy the property.

**Split gifts:** A married couple may elect under Section 2513 to treat gifts as made one-half by each spouse. This effectively doubles the annual exclusion to **$36,000 per donee** from a married couple.

Gifts that are **not subject** to the annual exclusion:
- Future interests (remainder interests, reversions)
- Gifts to trusts without a **Crummey withdrawal power** (the power converts a future interest into a present interest)

### Lifetime Exemption and Unified Credit

Beyond the annual exclusion, each taxpayer has a **lifetime exemption** of **$13.61 million** (2024). Taxable gifts exceeding the annual exclusion reduce this exemption. The unified credit is the tax on the exemption amount — effectively shielding the first $13.61 million of cumulative taxable gifts from tax.

> **Exam tip:** The annual exclusion applies per donee and does not reduce the lifetime exemption. Only gifts exceeding the annual exclusion are "taxable gifts" that consume the lifetime exemption. A donor giving $18,000 to 100 people ($1.8M total) uses zero lifetime exemption.

### Gift Tax Computation

The gift tax is calculated cumulatively:

| Step | Calculation |
|------|------------|
| 1 | Total gifts for the year |
| 2 | Less: Annual exclusions ($18,000 per donee) |
| 3 | Less: Marital deduction (unlimited, for gifts to U.S. citizen spouse) |
| 4 | Less: Charitable deduction (unlimited) |
| 5 | = **Taxable gifts for the year** |
| 6 | Add: Taxable gifts from all prior years |
| 7 | = **Cumulative taxable gifts** |
| 8 | Compute tentative tax on cumulative total (unified rate schedule) |
| 9 | Less: Tax computed on prior-year cumulative gifts |
| 10 | Less: Unified credit remaining |
| 11 | = **Gift tax due** |

**Worked Example:**

In 2024, David gives $100,000 to his daughter and $50,000 to his son. He made $500,000 in taxable gifts in prior years. His wife consents to split gifts.

| Item | Daughter | Son | Total |
|------|----------|-----|-------|
| Gift amount | $100,000 | $50,000 | $150,000 |
| Split (each spouse's share) | $50,000 | $25,000 | $75,000 |
| Less: Annual exclusion | ($18,000) | ($18,000) | ($36,000) |
| David's taxable gifts | $32,000 | $7,000 | $39,000 |

David's cumulative taxable gifts: $500,000 + $39,000 = $539,000. Since this is well below the $13.61 million exemption, no gift tax is due. The unified credit absorbs the entire amount.

## Estate Tax Framework

The estate tax applies to the transfer of a decedent's property at death. The computation follows a structured path:

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Gross estate** | FMV of all property the decedent owned or had an interest in at death |
| Less: **Funeral/admin expenses** | Executor fees, attorney fees, court costs |
| Less: **Debts and mortgages** | Decedent's liabilities at date of death |
| Less: **Casualty losses** during estate administration | Deductible if not claimed on the estate income tax return |
| = **Adjusted gross estate** | |
| Less: **Marital deduction** | Unlimited for property passing to surviving U.S. citizen spouse |
| Less: **Charitable deduction** | Unlimited for property passing to qualified charities |
| = **Taxable estate** | |
| Add: **Adjusted taxable gifts** | Post-1976 taxable gifts (at gift-date values) |
| = **Tax base** | |
| Compute tentative tax | Unified rate schedule (top rate 40%) |
| Less: Gift taxes payable on post-1976 gifts | Avoid double taxation |
| Less: Unified credit ($5,389,800 for 2024) | Shields first $13.61M |
| = **Estate tax due** | |

### Key Inclusions in the Gross Estate

- Property owned outright (Section 2033)
- Life insurance proceeds where decedent had incidents of ownership (Section 2042)
- Jointly held property (Section 2040) — included based on contribution by the decedent
- Revocable trust assets (Section 2038)
- Property over which decedent held a general power of appointment (Section 2041)
- Transfers with retained life estates (Section 2036)
- Transfers taking effect at death (Section 2037)

> **Exam tip:** Life insurance is included in the gross estate if the decedent held any incident of ownership (right to change beneficiaries, borrow against the policy, or cancel it) at death, even if someone else is the beneficiary. Transferring ownership to an irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT) at least 3 years before death removes the policy from the estate.

## Generation-Skipping Transfer Tax (GSTT)

The GSTT is a flat **40% tax** imposed in addition to gift or estate tax when transfers skip a generation. It prevents wealthy families from avoiding a layer of transfer tax by gifting directly to grandchildren.

### Taxable Events

| Event | Description | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| **Direct skip** | Transfer directly to a skip person (2+ generations below) | Grandmother gives $1M to granddaughter |
| **Taxable distribution** | Distribution from a trust to a skip person | Trust distributes income to grandchild |
| **Taxable termination** | Interest in a trust terminates and only skip persons remain | Parent-beneficiary dies, grandchildren are remaining beneficiaries |

### GST Exemption

Each taxpayer has a GST exemption equal to the estate tax exemption ($13.61 million for 2024). The exemption can be allocated to specific transfers, creating an **inclusion ratio** that determines the portion of a transfer subject to GSTT.

- **Inclusion ratio = 0**: Fully exempt from GSTT
- **Inclusion ratio = 1**: Fully subject to GSTT at 40%

## Valuation Discounts

When transferring interests in closely held businesses or family entities, the FMV may be reduced by valuation discounts:

| Discount | Rationale | Typical Range |
|----------|-----------|---------------|
| **Minority (lack of control)** | A minority interest cannot direct the entity's operations, distributions, or liquidation | 15-40% |
| **Lack of marketability** | No public market exists for the interest; finding a buyer is difficult | 15-35% |
| **Blockage discount** | Large blocks of publicly traded stock depress the price if sold at once | Varies |

**Worked Example:**

A father transfers a 30% interest in a family LLC valued at $10 million total.

- Pro rata value of 30% interest: $3,000,000
- Minority discount (25%): ($750,000)
- Marketability discount (20% of remaining): ($450,000)
- Discounted gift value: **$1,800,000**

The combined discount reduces the gift value by 40%, allowing more wealth to transfer within the lifetime exemption.

> **Exam tip:** Valuation discounts are applied sequentially, not additively. The marketability discount applies to the already-discounted minority interest value, not the original pro rata value. The IRS frequently challenges aggressive discount percentages.

## Grantor Trust Strategies

**Grantor trusts** are trusts where the grantor retains certain powers causing the trust income to be taxed to the grantor rather than the trust. Paradoxically, this is a powerful planning tool:

### Intentionally Defective Grantor Trusts (IDGTs)

An IDGT is designed to be a completed gift for transfer tax purposes but an incomplete transfer for income tax purposes:

- Grantor pays income tax on trust earnings — this effectively makes tax-free additional gifts to the trust beneficiaries
- Sales between the grantor and an IDGT are **ignored for income tax purposes** (no gain recognition)
- Assets in the trust appreciate outside the grantor's estate

### Grantor Retained Annuity Trusts (GRATs)

A GRAT allows the grantor to transfer appreciating assets to a trust while retaining a fixed annuity for a term of years:

- If the assets appreciate faster than the Section 7520 rate, the excess passes to beneficiaries transfer-tax free
- A **zeroed-out GRAT** sets the annuity to equal the full value of the transferred assets, resulting in a taxable gift of approximately $0
- Risk: If grantor dies during the trust term, the assets are included in the gross estate

### Qualified Personal Residence Trusts (QPRTs)

A QPRT removes a personal residence from the estate at a discounted gift tax value:

- Grantor transfers the home to a trust, retaining the right to live there for a term of years
- Gift value equals the FMV minus the retained interest (valued using IRS actuarial tables)
- After the term, the home passes to beneficiaries. Grantor must pay fair market rent to continue living there

## Family Limited Partnerships (FLPs)

FLPs are a common wealth transfer vehicle:

1. Senior generation contributes assets to a limited partnership, taking both general partner (1-2%) and limited partner (98-99%) interests
2. Limited partnership interests are gifted to the next generation at a discount (minority + marketability)
3. General partner retains control over management and distributions

**Tax benefits:** Valuation discounts, removal of future appreciation from the estate, and centralized management. The IRS scrutinizes FLPs under Section 2036 if the senior generation retains too much control or the entity lacks a legitimate business purpose.

## Practice Problems

**1.** In 2024, a married couple elects to split gifts. They give $80,000 to their daughter. What are the total taxable gifts reported by the couple?

a) $0
b) $22,000
c) $44,000
d) $62,000

**Answer:** c) $44,000 — Each spouse is treated as giving $40,000. Each applies the $18,000 annual exclusion: $40,000 - $18,000 = $22,000 taxable per spouse. Total: $22,000 x 2 = $44,000.

---

**2.** A decedent dies owning a $2 million life insurance policy payable to her adult children. The decedent transferred the policy to an irrevocable trust 2 years before death. Is the policy included in the gross estate?

a) No, because the trust is irrevocable
b) No, because the beneficiaries are the children
c) Yes, because the transfer occurred within 3 years of death
d) Yes, because life insurance is always included in the gross estate

**Answer:** c) Yes, because the transfer occurred within 3 years of death — Under Section 2035, transfers of life insurance within 3 years of death are pulled back into the gross estate. The decedent needed to survive at least 3 years after the transfer to remove the policy from the estate.

---

**3.** A grandfather places $1 million in a trust for the benefit of his grandchildren. He allocates his GST exemption to the transfer. What is the inclusion ratio?

a) 0 — fully exempt from GSTT
b) 0.5 — partially exempt
c) 1.0 — fully subject to GSTT
d) Cannot be determined without knowing the trust terms

**Answer:** a) 0 — fully exempt from GSTT — When the GST exemption is allocated to a transfer, the inclusion ratio is 0 (exemption allocated / value of transfer = $1M / $1M = 1; inclusion ratio = 1 - 1 = 0). A zero inclusion ratio means no GSTT applies to current or future distributions from the trust.
