## Debt vs. Equity Classification

The tax treatment of returns on capital depends entirely on whether the instrument is classified as **debt** or **equity**. Interest payments on debt are generally deductible by the payor, while dividend distributions on equity are not. This fundamental distinction creates a strong incentive to structure financing as debt.

### Multi-Factor Test

Courts and the IRS use a facts-and-circumstances analysis to classify instruments. No single factor is determinative, but the most commonly cited factors include:

| Factor | Points Toward Debt | Points Toward Equity |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Fixed maturity date | Yes, reasonable maturity | No maturity or extremely long term |
| Unconditional obligation to pay | Mandatory principal repayment | Repayment contingent on profits |
| Fixed interest rate | Stated, reasonable rate | Returns vary with earnings |
| Creditor remedies | Rights to enforce payment, security interest | No enforcement rights on default |
| Debt-to-equity ratio | Reasonable leverage | Thin capitalization (excessive debt) |
| Subordination | Senior or pari passu with third-party debt | Subordinated to all other obligations |
| Source of repayment | Expected from operating cash flows | Dependent on speculative future profits |
| Participation in management | None | Holder has voting or governance rights |
| Proportionality | Debt held disproportionately to equity | Debt held in same ratio as stock ownership |
| Intent of parties | Treated as debt on books, in filings | No formal documentation |

> **Exam tip:** The CPA exam frequently tests the proportionality factor. When shareholders hold debt in the same proportion as their stock, courts are more likely to recharacterize the debt as equity. A 50/50 shareholder who also holds 50% of the corporate notes raises a red flag.

### Tax Consequences of Reclassification

If an instrument classified as debt is reclassified as equity:

- **Interest deductions** previously claimed by the corporation are **disallowed** and potentially reclassified as nondeductible dividends
- **Withholding obligations** may change (interest withholding vs. dividend withholding for foreign payees)
- The holder's **interest income** is reclassified as **dividend income** (potentially qualifying for the lower qualified dividend rate)
- **Repayment of principal** is reclassified as a **stock redemption** subject to Section 302 analysis

## Section 385 Regulations

Section 385 authorizes the Treasury to issue regulations distinguishing debt from equity. The current regulations target **related-party transactions** and impose documentation and recharacterization rules.

### Documentation Requirements (Reg. Section 1.385-2)

For related-party debt instruments to be respected as debt, the borrower must maintain:

1. **Written unconditional obligation** to pay a fixed or determinable sum on demand or at a specified date
2. **Creditor's rights** to enforce payment, including legal rights in the event of default
3. **Reasonable expectation of repayment** documented at the time of issuance based on the borrower's financial condition
4. **Evidence of payments** consistent with the terms of the instrument (timely interest payments, principal amortization)

Failure to maintain this documentation creates a rebuttable presumption that the instrument is equity.

### Recharacterization Rules (Reg. Section 1.385-3)

Certain related-party debt is automatically recharacterized as equity if it is issued in connection with:

| Transaction | Rule |
|------------|------|
| Distribution | Debt issued to a shareholder as a distribution is treated as stock |
| Acquisition of affiliate stock | Debt issued to fund a related-party stock acquisition is treated as stock |
| Funding rule | Debt issued within 72 months of a distribution or stock acquisition may be recharacterized if it effectively funded the transaction |

**Exception:** The regulations provide a $50 million threshold exemption. Expanded group members with total related-party debt below $50 million are exempt from the recharacterization rules.

> **Exam tip:** Section 385 regulations apply only to **related-party** debt. Third-party debt is not subject to these documentation or automatic recharacterization rules. The exam may present a scenario where the critical issue is whether the lender is a related party.

## Thin Capitalization

Thin capitalization occurs when a corporation has an excessive ratio of debt to equity, suggesting the debt is really disguised equity.

### Key Indicators

- **Debt-to-equity ratio** exceeding 3:1 or 4:1 raises scrutiny (no bright-line statutory ratio exists in the US)
- **Inside debt** (debt from shareholders) at the same proportion as equity ownership
- Corporation **unable to obtain similar financing** from third parties at arm's length
- **No sinking fund** or repayment schedule observed in practice

### International Context

Many countries impose statutory thin capitalization rules with fixed debt-to-equity ratios (e.g., 2:1 or 3:1). The US relies primarily on case law and Section 385 regulations rather than a fixed ratio, though Section 163(j) effectively limits the benefit of excessive leverage.

## Section 163(j) Business Interest Expense Limitation

### The Core Rule

For tax years beginning after December 31, 2017, Section 163(j) limits the deduction for **business interest expense** to the sum of:

1. **Business interest income** for the tax year
2. **30% of adjusted taxable income (ATI)** for the tax year
3. **Floor plan financing interest** (applicable to auto dealers)

**Formula:**

> Deductible Business Interest = Business Interest Income + (30% x ATI) + Floor Plan Financing Interest

Any disallowed interest is carried forward **indefinitely** to future tax years (subject to the same 30% limitation in each future year).

### Adjusted Taxable Income (ATI) Calculation

ATI starts with taxable income and makes the following adjustments:

| Adjustment | Treatment |
|-----------|-----------|
| Business interest expense | Add back |
| Business interest income | Subtract |
| Net operating loss deduction | Add back |
| Section 199A deduction | Add back |
| Depreciation, amortization, depletion | **Not** added back (post-2021 EBIT basis) |

### The EBITDA-to-EBIT Transition

For tax years beginning before January 1, 2022, ATI was calculated on an **EBITDA basis** (depreciation and amortization were added back). Starting in 2022, ATI switched to an **EBIT basis** (depreciation and amortization are **not** added back).

| Period | ATI Basis | Effect on Limitation |
|--------|-----------|---------------------|
| 2018-2021 | EBITDA (D&A added back) | More generous; higher ATI means more deductible interest |
| 2022+ | EBIT (D&A not added back) | More restrictive; capital-intensive businesses have lower ATI |

> **Exam tip:** The EBIT transition is a high-value testing point. A capital-intensive business that was comfortably within the 163(j) limit under EBITDA rules may find itself with significant disallowed interest under EBIT rules. Always check which period the question references.

### Worked Example

**Facts:** MidWest Manufacturing Corp has the following for 2025:
- Taxable income (before interest deduction): $2,000,000
- Business interest expense: $900,000
- Business interest income: $50,000
- Depreciation: $600,000
- No NOL, no Section 199A deduction

**Step 1: Calculate ATI (EBIT basis for 2025)**

ATI = Taxable income + Business interest expense + Business interest income adjustments

Since we are using the EBIT basis (2022+), depreciation is **not** added back:

ATI = $2,000,000 + $900,000 - $50,000 = $2,850,000

Wait -- let us recalculate. ATI begins with taxable income and then:
- Add back: business interest expense ($900,000)
- Subtract: business interest income ($50,000)
- Add back: NOL deduction ($0)
- Add back: Section 199A deduction ($0)
- Depreciation: NOT added back (post-2021)

ATI = $2,000,000 + $900,000 - $50,000 = $2,850,000

**Step 2: Calculate the limitation**

Deductible interest = Business interest income + 30% of ATI
= $50,000 + (30% x $2,850,000)
= $50,000 + $855,000
= **$905,000**

**Step 3: Compare to actual interest expense**

Business interest expense: $900,000
Limitation: $905,000

Since $900,000 < $905,000, **all business interest is deductible**. No carryforward is created.

### Small Business Exception

Section 163(j) does **not** apply to taxpayers that meet the **gross receipts test**: average annual gross receipts for the 3 prior tax years do not exceed **$30 million** (adjusted for inflation; $30 million for 2025).

| Requirement | Detail |
|-------------|--------|
| Threshold | Average annual gross receipts of $30 million or less (3-year average) |
| Aggregation | All members of a controlled group or affiliated group are treated as a single employer |
| Tax shelters | Tax shelter businesses cannot use this exception regardless of gross receipts |

### Electing Out (Real Property and Farming)

Certain businesses may elect out of Section 163(j):

| Election | Effect |
|----------|--------|
| Real property trade or business | Not subject to 163(j) but must use ADS depreciation for nonresidential real property (40 years), residential rental property (30 years), and qualified improvement property (20 years) |
| Farming business | Not subject to 163(j) but must use ADS depreciation for property with a recovery period of 10+ years |

The election is **irrevocable** once made.

> **Exam tip:** The trade-off for electing out of 163(j) is critical: full interest deductibility in exchange for slower depreciation under ADS. The exam may present a scenario requiring you to compare the net tax benefit of deducting all interest vs. claiming accelerated MACRS depreciation.

## Interest Expense Carryforward

Disallowed business interest under Section 163(j) carries forward **indefinitely** and is treated as business interest expense paid or accrued in the succeeding tax year. Key rules:

- Carryforwards are subject to the same 30% ATI limitation in future years
- For **partnerships**, disallowed interest is allocated to each partner and tracked at the partner level (the partner, not the partnership, carries forward the excess)
- For **S corporations**, disallowed interest is tracked at the corporate level
- Upon death of a taxpayer, any unused carryforward is lost (no step-up or transfer)
- In corporate acquisitions, Section 382 may limit the use of acquired corporations' interest carryforwards

## Planning Strategies

### Optimizing Capital Structure for Tax Efficiency

1. **Document all related-party debt thoroughly** to satisfy Section 385 requirements. Include written loan agreements, market-rate interest, fixed maturity dates, and evidence of actual payments.

2. **Monitor the 30% ATI threshold** proactively. Model the Section 163(j) limitation before issuing new debt and consider timing capital expenditures to manage ATI.

3. **Evaluate the real property election** for real estate businesses with significant interest expense. The election trades accelerated depreciation for unlimited interest deductions.

4. **Use third-party debt where possible** for leveraged acquisitions to avoid Section 385 recharacterization risk.

5. **Consider the entity type implications**: partnerships allocate disallowed interest to partners (tracked at partner level), while C corporations track at the entity level. Different entities may yield different planning opportunities.

6. **Time debt issuances strategically**: Avoid issuing related-party debt within 72 months of distributions or affiliate stock acquisitions that might trigger Section 1.385-3 recharacterization.

## Key Terms

- **Adjusted Taxable Income (ATI):** Modified taxable income used as the base for the Section 163(j) limitation, calculated on an EBIT basis starting in 2022
- **Business Interest Expense:** Interest paid or accrued on debt allocable to a trade or business, excluding investment interest
- **Floor Plan Financing Interest:** Interest on debt used to finance motor vehicle inventory held for sale
- **Recharacterization:** Reclassification of a debt instrument as equity, causing interest deductions to be treated as nondeductible dividend payments
- **Section 163(j):** IRC provision limiting the deduction for business interest expense to 30% of ATI plus business interest income
- **Section 385:** IRC provision authorizing regulations to distinguish debt from equity in corporate transactions
- **Thin Capitalization:** A capital structure with an excessive proportion of debt relative to equity, raising recharacterization risk

## Practice Problems

**1.** Corporation X issues a $1,000,000 note to its sole shareholder, payable in 20 years with 5% annual interest. The shareholder holds 100% of the stock and 100% of the notes. No payments are made for the first three years. Which factor most strongly suggests equity reclassification?

a) The 20-year maturity
b) The 5% interest rate
c) The proportionality of debt to equity holdings
d) The $1,000,000 face amount

**Answer:** c) The proportionality of debt to equity holdings -- When a shareholder holds debt in the exact same proportion as stock (100%/100%), courts view this as a strong indicator that the "debt" is really additional equity. The failure to make payments reinforces this conclusion, but proportionality is the most significant single factor.

**3.** A real property trade or business elects out of Section 163(j). Which of the following is a consequence of this election?

a) The business may use 200% declining balance MACRS depreciation
b) The business must use the Alternative Depreciation System for nonresidential real property, residential rental property, and QIP

**Answer:** b) The business must use the Alternative Depreciation System for nonresidential real property, residential rental property, and QIP -- Electing out of 163(j) allows unlimited interest deductions but requires ADS depreciation (40-year for nonresidential real property, 30-year for residential rental, 20-year for QIP).
