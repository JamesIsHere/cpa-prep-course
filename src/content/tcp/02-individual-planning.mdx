# Individual Tax Planning: Compensation

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish the tax treatment of incentive stock options (ISOs) and nonqualified stock options (NQSOs) at grant, exercise, and sale
- Evaluate the benefits and risks of a Section 83(b) election for restricted stock
- Apply Section 409A requirements to nonqualified deferred compensation arrangements
- Identify tax-free fringe benefits under Section 132 and their planning implications
- Develop timing strategies for income recognition and deferral in compensation planning

## Statutory vs. Nonstatutory Stock Options

Stock options are a cornerstone of executive compensation. The tax consequences depend entirely on whether the option qualifies as a **statutory** (incentive) stock option or a **nonstatutory** (nonqualified) stock option.

### Incentive Stock Options (ISOs)

ISOs receive favorable tax treatment under Sections 421-424 but must meet strict requirements:

- Granted under a shareholder-approved plan
- Exercise price must equal or exceed FMV at grant date
- Exercisable within 10 years of grant
- Employee must hold shares at least **2 years from grant** and **1 year from exercise**
- Only available to employees (not independent contractors or directors)

| Event | Regular Tax | AMT |
|-------|------------|-----|
| Grant | No taxable event | No AMT adjustment |
| Exercise | No taxable event | Bargain element is an AMT preference item |
| Qualifying sale | Entire gain taxed as LTCG | AMT basis includes bargain element |
| Disqualifying sale | Bargain element taxed as ordinary income; excess is capital gain | Recalculate using AMT basis |

**Worked Example:**

Emma receives ISOs to purchase 1,000 shares at $20 (FMV at grant). She exercises when FMV is $50 and sells two years later at $70.

| Step | Amount | Tax Treatment |
|------|--------|---------------|
| Grant | $20 exercise price | No tax |
| Exercise | $50 FMV - $20 price = $30 bargain element | No regular tax; $30,000 AMT preference |
| Sale | $70 - $20 = $50 gain | $50,000 LTCG (holding periods met) |

> **Exam tip:** The ISO bargain element at exercise is the single most tested AMT adjustment for individual taxpayers. Even though there is no regular tax at exercise, the spread creates an AMT preference item that can trigger AMT liability.

### Nonqualified Stock Options (NQSOs)

NQSOs have no statutory requirements and offer more flexibility but less favorable tax treatment:

| Event | Employee | Employer |
|-------|----------|----------|
| Grant (no readily ascertainable FMV) | No taxable event | No deduction |
| Grant (with readily ascertainable FMV) | Ordinary income = FMV of option | Deduction = FMV of option |
| Exercise | Ordinary income = FMV - exercise price | Deduction = same amount |
| Sale | Capital gain/loss from exercise-date FMV | No further deduction |

The key planning difference: **NQSOs generate an employer deduction** equal to the employee's ordinary income at exercise. ISOs provide no employer deduction for qualifying dispositions.

## Restricted Stock and Section 83(b) Elections

Under **Section 83**, property transferred in connection with services is taxed when it is **no longer subject to a substantial risk of forfeiture** — typically when the restrictions lapse (vesting).

Without a Section 83(b) election:
- No tax at grant
- Ordinary income at vesting = FMV at vesting minus amount paid
- Employer gets a deduction at vesting

With a **Section 83(b) election** (filed within 30 days of grant):
- Ordinary income at grant = FMV at grant minus amount paid
- No additional income at vesting
- All post-grant appreciation taxed as capital gain at sale

**Worked Example:**

Marcus receives 10,000 restricted shares worth $5 each ($50,000 total). Shares vest in 3 years when FMV is $20.

| Scenario | Income at Grant | Income at Vesting | Gain at Sale ($30/share) |
|----------|----------------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| No 83(b) | $0 | $200,000 ordinary | $100,000 capital gain |
| With 83(b) | $50,000 ordinary | $0 | $250,000 capital gain |

> **Exam tip:** The Section 83(b) election is a gamble. If the stock declines or the employee forfeits the shares, the tax paid at grant is not recoverable. The election must be filed within 30 days — there are no extensions and no exceptions.

## Deferred Compensation: Section 409A

**Nonqualified deferred compensation (NQDC)** plans allow employees to defer income beyond qualified plan limits. Section 409A imposes strict rules:

- **Deferral election** must be made before the year in which services are performed (prior calendar year)
- **Distribution** limited to six permissible events: separation from service, disability, death, specified time, change in control, unforeseeable emergency
- **Acceleration of payments** is generally prohibited

Violation of 409A rules results in:
- Immediate inclusion of all deferred amounts in income
- 20% additional tax penalty
- Interest at the underpayment rate plus 1%

**Constructive receipt doctrine:** Income is taxable when it is credited, set apart, or made available without substantial limitations — even if not actually received. A valid NQDC plan must impose genuine restrictions on access.

**Economic benefit doctrine:** If an employer's promise is funded through a trust or other arrangement where the employee has a current beneficial interest, the employee is taxed currently regardless of deferral elections.

## Section 132 Fringe Benefits

Certain employer-provided benefits are excluded from gross income under Section 132:

| Exclusion Type | Description | Example |
|---------------|-------------|---------|
| No-additional-cost service | Service the employer provides to customers at no substantial additional cost | Free flights for airline employees |
| Qualified employee discount | Discount on employer's goods/services (up to gross profit % for goods, 20% for services) | Retail store employee discount |
| Working condition fringe | Property/services that would be deductible if the employee paid for them | Company car for business use |
| De minimis fringe | Too small to account for | Occasional personal use of copier |
| Qualified transportation | Transit passes, parking, bicycle commuting | Up to $325/month parking (2025) |
| On-premises athletic facilities | Gym operated by employer on employer's premises | Company fitness center |

## Self-Employment Tax Planning

Self-employment (SE) tax applies to net earnings from self-employment at a combined rate of **15.3%** (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare). Key planning strategies:

- **Deduction for 50% of SE tax** — reduces AGI, not the SE tax base itself
- **S corporation election** — reasonable salary subject to employment tax; distributions are not (but must pay reasonable compensation)
- **Retirement plan contributions** — SEP-IRA or solo 401(k) reduces taxable income but not SE tax base
- **Additional Medicare tax** — 0.9% on SE income exceeding $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (MFJ)

The SE tax base is calculated as net SE earnings x **92.35%** (1 - 7.65%), reflecting the employer-equivalent portion.

## Timing Strategies for Income Recognition

Effective tax planning considers the timing of income and deductions:

- **Accelerate deductions** into high-income years; **defer income** into low-income years
- **Installment sales** (Section 453) spread gain recognition over the payment period
- **Cash method taxpayers** can control timing by accelerating or delaying billing and collections
- **Year-end bonuses** — accrual-method employers must pay within 2.5 months after year-end for a current deduction to related parties (Section 267)

> **Exam tip:** Timing strategies must consider the taxpayer's current and expected future marginal tax rates. Deferring income only saves tax when the taxpayer expects to be in a lower bracket in the future. If rates are expected to increase, acceleration may be better.
