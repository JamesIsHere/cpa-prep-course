# Trusts and Estates

## Learning Objectives

- Classify trust types and identify when grantor trust rules apply
- Compute Distributable Net Income (DNI) and the Income Distribution Deduction (IDD)
- Apply compressed trust and estate tax brackets and the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax
- Distinguish fiduciary accounting income from taxable income and handle estate termination

## Trust Classifications

### Simple vs. Complex Trusts

| Feature | Simple Trust | Complex Trust |
|---------|-------------|---------------|
| Required to distribute all income currently | Yes | No — may accumulate income |
| Distributes corpus (principal) | No | May distribute corpus |
| Charitable contributions | Not permitted from income | May make charitable contributions |
| Personal exemption | $300 | $100 |

A trust can be simple in one year and complex in another, depending on whether it distributes all income and whether it makes corpus distributions or charitable contributions that year.

### Revocable vs. Irrevocable Trusts

- **Revocable trust:** The grantor retains the power to alter, amend, or revoke the trust. All income is taxed to the grantor (grantor trust). Revocable trusts are ignored for income tax purposes during the grantor's lifetime.
- **Irrevocable trust:** The grantor permanently relinquishes control. The trust is a separate taxpayer unless grantor trust rules apply.

### Grantor Trust Rules (Sections 671-679)

A trust is treated as a **grantor trust** (income taxable to the grantor) if the grantor retains certain powers or interests:

| Power Retained | Code Section |
|---------------|-------------|
| Power to revoke | Section 676 |
| Power to control beneficial enjoyment | Section 674 |
| Administrative powers exercisable for grantor's benefit | Section 675 |
| Reversionary interest exceeding 5% of trust value | Section 673 |
| Income distributable to or accumulated for the grantor or grantor's spouse | Section 677 |

> **Exam tip:** If any grantor trust rule applies, the entire trust (or the affected portion) is disregarded and all items are reported on the grantor's individual return. The trust does not file a separate return for those items — it files an informational return only.

## Distributable Net Income (DNI)

DNI serves as the **ceiling** on the amount taxable to beneficiaries and the **floor** for the trust or estate's distribution deduction. It also carries out the character of income to beneficiaries.

### DNI Computation

| Start with | Taxable income of the trust/estate |
|-----------|-----------------------------------|
| Add back | Personal exemption ($300 or $100) |
| Add back | Net tax-exempt income (tax-exempt interest minus allocable expenses) |
| Subtract | Net capital gains allocable to corpus (not distributed to beneficiaries) |
| Add back | Capital losses deducted against ordinary income (trusts cannot claim capital loss against ordinary income — limited) |
| Equals | **Distributable Net Income (DNI)** |

**Worked Example:**

An estate has the following items: $80,000 rental income, $20,000 tax-exempt interest, $5,000 allocable expenses to tax-exempt income, $15,000 capital gain allocated to corpus, and a $600 personal exemption.

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Taxable income | $80,000 - $600 exemption + $15,000 capital gain = $94,400 |
| Add back: personal exemption | $600 |
| Add back: net tax-exempt income ($20,000 - $5,000) | $15,000 |
| Subtract: capital gain allocable to corpus | ($15,000) |
| **DNI** | **$95,000** |

### Character of Income (Tier System)

DNI carries the same character to beneficiaries as it had in the trust or estate. If the trust has $60,000 of rental income and $20,000 of dividend income in DNI ($80,000 total), a beneficiary receiving 50% of the distribution reports 50% of each type.

**First-tier beneficiaries** (those entitled to mandatory distributions of current income) absorb DNI first. **Second-tier beneficiaries** (discretionary distributions, corpus distributions) absorb the remaining DNI.

## Income Distribution Deduction (IDD)

The IDD is the trust or estate's deduction for amounts distributed or required to be distributed to beneficiaries.

**IDD = Lesser of:**
- Actual distributions to beneficiaries, or
- DNI (reduced by tax-exempt income)

The IDD ensures that income is taxed either to the entity or to the beneficiaries, but not both.

**Worked Example:**

A trust has DNI of $70,000 (including $10,000 of tax-exempt interest). The trust distributes $80,000 to beneficiaries.

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| DNI | | $70,000 |
| DNI minus tax-exempt portion | $70,000 - $10,000 | $60,000 |
| Actual distributions | | $80,000 |
| **IDD** | Lesser of $80,000 or $60,000 | **$60,000** |

The beneficiaries include $60,000 in taxable income and $10,000 in tax-exempt income. The excess distribution of $10,000 ($80,000 - $70,000) is a tax-free distribution of corpus.

> **Exam tip:** The IDD is always reduced by tax-exempt income included in DNI. Tax-exempt income retains its character when distributed to beneficiaries — it is not converted to taxable income.

## Trust and Estate Tax Brackets

Trusts and estates are subject to **compressed tax brackets** that reach the highest marginal rate at very low income levels compared to individuals:

| Taxable Income (2025) | Rate |
|-----------------------|------|
| $0 - $3,150 | 10% |
| $3,151 - $11,450 | 24% |
| $11,451 - $15,650 | 35% |
| Over $15,650 | 37% |

For comparison, individuals do not reach the 37% bracket until taxable income exceeds $626,350 (single). This compression creates a strong incentive to **distribute income** to beneficiaries in lower tax brackets.

### 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT)

Trusts and estates are subject to the 3.8% NIIT on the lesser of:
- Undistributed net investment income, or
- AGI exceeding the threshold of **$15,650** (2025) — the same as the top bracket threshold

Since the NIIT threshold for trusts is far lower than for individuals ($200,000 single / $250,000 MFJ), distributing investment income can significantly reduce total taxes.

## Charitable Contributions

Trusts and estates may deduct charitable contributions **without limitation** (unlike individuals, who are subject to AGI-based limits), provided the governing instrument authorizes charitable distributions.

| Entity | Charitable Deduction Limit |
|--------|---------------------------|
| Individuals | Generally 60% of AGI (cash to public charities), with lower limits for other contributions |
| C corporations | 10% of taxable income (before deduction) |
| Trusts and estates | **Unlimited** — full amount of qualifying contributions, if authorized by governing instrument |

## Fiduciary Accounting Income vs. Taxable Income

These are two different measurements that often diverge:

| Item | Fiduciary Accounting Income (FAI) | Taxable Income |
|------|-----------------------------------|----------------|
| Tax-exempt interest | Included | Excluded |
| Capital gains | Usually allocated to corpus (excluded from FAI) | Included in taxable income |
| Depreciation | Allocated per governing instrument or state law | Deducted under IRC rules |
| Stock dividends | Usually corpus | Excluded from income |
| Trustee fees | Allocated between income and corpus | Deductible (subject to 2% floor pre-TCJA, fully deductible for estate/trust administration) |

FAI determines the amount that must be distributed to income beneficiaries. Taxable income determines the trust's tax liability. The difference between these two concepts is a frequent exam topic.

> **Exam tip:** Capital gains are usually allocated to corpus under state law and the governing instrument, which means they are NOT part of FAI and are NOT distributed to income beneficiaries. They stay in the trust, taxed at the trust level. But if the trustee has discretion to allocate gains to income, or if gains are actually distributed, they can be included in DNI.

## Estate Termination

### Final Return

An estate must file its final Form 1041 for the taxable year in which it terminates. All remaining income, deductions, and credits are reported on this return.

### Excess Deductions on Termination

If the estate's deductions exceed its gross income in the final year, the **excess deductions** pass through to the beneficiaries succeeding to the estate's property. These excess deductions are:

- Allowed only in the estate's final year
- Reported on beneficiaries' individual returns as itemized deductions (miscellaneous deductions not subject to the 2% floor)
- Limited to deductions that exceed gross income — not including the personal exemption or charitable contributions

### Unused Loss Carryovers and Credits

Any unused **NOL carryovers** and **capital loss carryovers** of the estate pass to the beneficiaries in the final year, retaining their character (ordinary loss or capital loss).

## Practice Problems

**1.** A simple trust has $50,000 of rental income, $10,000 of tax-exempt interest (no allocable expenses), and a capital gain of $8,000 allocated to corpus. The trust distributes all income to one beneficiary. What is the trust's DNI?

a) $50,000
b) $60,000
c) $68,000
d) $52,000

**Answer:** b) $60,000 — DNI starts with taxable income ($50,000 rental + $8,000 capital gain - $300 exemption = $57,700), adds back the exemption ($300) and tax-exempt income ($10,000), and subtracts capital gains allocable to corpus ($8,000). DNI = $57,700 + $300 + $10,000 - $8,000 = $60,000.

---

**2.** A trust has AGI of $25,000 and undistributed net investment income of $18,000. What is the trust's NIIT liability?

a) $0
b) $372.40
c) $684.00
d) $950.00

**Answer:** b) $372.40 — The NIIT is 3.8% of the lesser of undistributed net investment income ($18,000) or AGI exceeding $15,200 ($25,000 - $15,200 = $9,800). The lesser amount is $9,800. NIIT = $9,800 x 3.8% = $372.40.

**3.** Which of the following is a key difference between fiduciary accounting income and taxable income for a trust?

a) Tax-exempt interest is included in both FAI and taxable income
b) Capital gains are typically included in FAI but excluded from taxable income
c) Capital gains are typically excluded from FAI but included in taxable income
d) Trustee fees are deductible for FAI purposes but not for taxable income

**Answer:** c) Capital gains are typically excluded from FAI but included in taxable income — Under most state laws and governing instruments, capital gains are allocated to corpus and excluded from fiduciary accounting income. However, they are included in the trust's taxable income. This means capital gains are generally taxed at the trust level (at compressed rates) rather than being distributed to beneficiaries.
