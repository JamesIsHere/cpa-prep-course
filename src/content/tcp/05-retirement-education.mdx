# Retirement Plans and Education Savings

## Learning Objectives

- Compare qualified plan types: defined benefit, defined contribution, 401(k), and Roth variants
- Calculate IRA contribution limits, deductibility rules, and Roth conversion strategies
- Apply SEP-IRA and SIMPLE IRA rules for self-employed and small business owners
- Determine Required Minimum Distribution amounts and applicable penalties
- Evaluate education savings vehicles including Section 529 plans and Coverdell ESAs

## Qualified Retirement Plans

Qualified plans meet IRS requirements under Sections 401-417 and receive favorable tax treatment: employer contributions are deductible when made, earnings grow tax-deferred, and employees are not taxed until distribution.

### Defined Benefit vs. Defined Contribution

| Feature | Defined Benefit | Defined Contribution |
|---------|----------------|---------------------|
| Promise | Specified retirement benefit (formula-based) | Specified contribution amount |
| Investment risk | Employer bears the risk | Employee bears the risk |
| Benefit formula | Based on salary, years of service | Based on contributions + investment returns |
| Annual limit (2025) | Lesser of $280,000 or 100% of avg. highest 3-year comp | Lesser of $70,000 or 100% of compensation |
| Funding | Actuarially determined; employer must fund shortfalls | Fixed contribution; no additional obligation |
| PBGC insurance | Yes | No |
| Typical employer | Large companies, government | Most private employers |

### 401(k) Plans

A 401(k) is the most common defined contribution plan. Key rules for 2025:

| Limit | Amount |
|-------|--------|
| Employee elective deferral | $23,500 |
| Catch-up contribution (age 50+) | $7,500 additional |
| Total annual addition (employer + employee) | $70,000 |
| Compensation limit for calculations | $350,000 |

**Employer matching:** Contributions are immediately deductible by the employer (up to 25% of eligible payroll). Common match formulas include 50% of the first 6% of salary or dollar-for-dollar up to 3%.

**Vesting schedules** for employer contributions:

| Type | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 | Year 4 | Year 5 | Year 6 |
|------|--------|--------|--------|--------|--------|--------|
| Cliff vesting | 0% | 0% | 100% | 100% | 100% | 100% |
| Graded vesting | 0% | 20% | 40% | 60% | 80% | 100% |

Employee contributions (deferrals) are **always 100% vested immediately**.

### Roth 401(k)

A Roth 401(k) option allows employees to make **after-tax contributions** within the same 401(k) plan:

- Contributions are not tax-deductible
- Qualified distributions are **completely tax-free** (contributions and earnings)
- Same contribution limits as traditional 401(k)
- No income limit for eligibility (unlike Roth IRAs)
- Subject to RMDs unless rolled to a Roth IRA (SECURE 2.0 eliminated Roth 401(k) RMDs starting 2024)

> **Exam tip:** The Roth 401(k) has no income limit, unlike the Roth IRA. High-income earners who cannot contribute directly to a Roth IRA can still make Roth contributions through their employer's 401(k) plan. This is a frequently tested distinction.

## Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs)

### Traditional IRA

**Contribution limits (2025):** $7,000 ($8,000 if age 50+). The contribution cannot exceed the taxpayer's earned income.

**Deductibility** depends on whether the taxpayer (or spouse) is an active participant in an employer plan:

| Filing Status | Active Participant? | Modified AGI Phase-Out | Deduction |
|--------------|--------------------|-----------------------|-----------|
| Single/HOH | Yes | $79,000 - $89,000 | Full to none |
| MFJ (contributor is active) | Yes | $126,000 - $146,000 | Full to none |
| MFJ (spouse is active, contributor is not) | No | $236,000 - $246,000 | Full to none |
| Single/HOH | No | No limit | Fully deductible |

If a contribution is **nondeductible**, the taxpayer files Form 8606 to track basis. Distributions are then partially taxable (pro-rata rule).

### Roth IRA

Roth IRA contributions are never deductible, but qualified distributions are **tax-free**:

**Income limits for direct contributions (2025):**

| Filing Status | Phase-Out Range | Effect |
|--------------|----------------|--------|
| Single/HOH | $150,000 - $165,000 | Reduced contribution, then $0 |
| MFJ | $236,000 - $246,000 | Reduced contribution, then $0 |

**Backdoor Roth conversion:** High-income taxpayers who exceed the Roth IRA income limits can contribute to a nondeductible Traditional IRA and then immediately convert to a Roth IRA. The conversion is taxable only on the earnings (if any) and is subject to the **pro-rata rule** — if the taxpayer has other pre-tax IRA balances, a portion of the conversion is taxable.

**Worked Example:**

A taxpayer has a $200,000 Traditional IRA (all pre-tax) and makes a $7,000 nondeductible contribution. She converts the $7,000 to a Roth IRA.

- Total IRA balance: $200,000 + $7,000 = $207,000
- Nondeductible (after-tax) basis: $7,000
- Tax-free portion of conversion: $7,000 / $207,000 = 3.38%
- Taxable portion: $7,000 x 96.62% = **$6,763**

> **Exam tip:** The pro-rata rule for Roth conversions considers ALL Traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances — not just the account being converted. A taxpayer cannot isolate after-tax contributions in a separate IRA to avoid this rule. This is heavily tested.

## SEP-IRA and SIMPLE IRA

### SEP-IRA (Simplified Employee Pension)

Designed for self-employed individuals and small businesses:

- **Employer-only contributions** (employees cannot defer salary)
- Contribution limit: Lesser of **25% of compensation** or $70,000 (2025)
- Self-employed individuals use net SE earnings minus 50% of SE tax, then multiply by 20% (effective rate due to circular calculation)
- Must cover all eligible employees (age 21+, worked 3 of last 5 years, earned $750+)
- **No filing requirements** — simplest plan to administer

### SIMPLE IRA (Savings Incentive Match Plan for Employees)

For employers with **100 or fewer employees**:

| Component | Limit (2025) |
|-----------|-------------|
| Employee salary deferral | $16,500 |
| Catch-up (age 50+) | $3,500 additional |
| Employer match | Dollar-for-dollar up to 3% of compensation |
| Alternative: Employer nonelective contribution | 2% of compensation for all eligible employees |

**Key rule:** Early withdrawal within the first **2 years** of participation triggers a **25% penalty** (instead of the normal 10%).

## Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)

RMDs ensure that tax-deferred retirement accounts are eventually subject to income tax.

### Age Triggers

Under SECURE 2.0:
- **Age 73** for individuals born 1951-1959
- **Age 75** for individuals born 1960 or later

**Roth IRAs** are exempt from RMDs during the owner's lifetime.

### Calculation

RMD = Account balance (December 31 of prior year) / Distribution period (from Uniform Lifetime Table)

**Worked Example:**

A 75-year-old taxpayer has a $500,000 Traditional IRA balance on December 31 of the prior year. The Uniform Lifetime Table divisor for age 75 is 24.6.

RMD = $500,000 / 24.6 = **$20,325**

### Penalties

Failure to take the full RMD results in a **25% excise tax** on the shortfall (reduced from 50% by SECURE 2.0). If corrected within 2 years, the penalty drops to **10%**.

### Inherited IRAs

Post-SECURE Act rules for non-spouse beneficiaries:

| Beneficiary Type | Distribution Rule |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Surviving spouse | Can treat as own IRA or remain as beneficiary; RMDs based on own life expectancy |
| Eligible designated beneficiary (minor child, disabled, chronically ill, within 10 years of age) | Stretch over life expectancy |
| Non-eligible designated beneficiary | **10-year rule** — must empty the account within 10 years of death |
| Non-designated beneficiary (estate, charity) | **5-year rule** (if death before RBD) or ghost life expectancy (if after) |

> **Exam tip:** The 10-year rule for inherited IRAs does not require annual distributions — the beneficiary simply must empty the account by December 31 of the 10th year following death. However, if the original owner had already begun RMDs, annual distributions within the 10-year period are required.

## Plan Distributions and Penalties

### Early Withdrawal Penalty

A **10% additional tax** applies to distributions before age 59 1/2, with exceptions:

- Death or disability
- Substantially equal periodic payments (Section 72(t) — SEPP)
- Medical expenses exceeding 7.5% of AGI
- Qualified higher education expenses (IRAs only)
- First-time home purchase up to $10,000 (IRAs only)
- Birth or adoption up to $5,000 per parent
- Separation from service at age 55+ (employer plans only, not IRAs)

### Section 72(t) — Substantially Equal Periodic Payments

A taxpayer under 59 1/2 can avoid the 10% penalty by taking **substantially equal periodic payments (SEPPs)** calculated using one of three IRS-approved methods:

1. **Required minimum distribution method** — recalculated annually; yields the smallest amount
2. **Fixed amortization method** — fixed annual amount based on life expectancy and reasonable interest rate
3. **Fixed annuitization method** — based on annuity factor from IRS mortality table

Payments must continue for 5 years or until age 59 1/2, **whichever is later**. Modifying the payments before that point triggers the 10% penalty retroactively on all prior distributions.

## Education Savings

### Section 529 Plans

State-sponsored plans with significant tax benefits:

- Contributions are **not federally deductible** (some states allow a state income tax deduction)
- Earnings grow **tax-free**
- Qualified distributions for education expenses are **tax-free**
- **Qualified expenses:** Tuition, fees, books, room and board, computers, K-12 tuition (up to $10,000/year), student loan repayment (up to $10,000 lifetime)
- No income limits for contributors
- Lifetime contribution limits set by each state (typically $300,000-$500,000+)

**SECURE 2.0 provision:** Starting 2024, unused 529 funds can be rolled over to a Roth IRA for the beneficiary, subject to:
- Account must have existed for 15+ years
- Annual Roth IRA contribution limits apply
- Lifetime rollover cap of $35,000
- Contributions (and earnings on them) from the last 5 years are ineligible

### Coverdell Education Savings Accounts (ESAs)

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Annual contribution limit | $2,000 per beneficiary |
| Contributor income limit | Phase-out at $95,000-$110,000 (single); $190,000-$220,000 (MFJ) |
| Qualified expenses | K-12 and higher education (broader than 529 for K-12) |
| Tax treatment | Tax-free growth and qualified distributions |
| Age limit | Contributions by age 18; must be distributed by age 30 |

> **Exam tip:** The 529 plan has no income limit for contributors and no annual contribution cap (beyond gift tax considerations), while the Coverdell ESA has a $2,000 annual limit and income phase-outs. On the exam, if a high-income taxpayer wants to save for education, the 529 plan is almost always the better vehicle.

## Practice Problems

**1.** A 52-year-old self-employed taxpayer has net self-employment income of $200,000 (after the deduction for 50% of SE tax). What is the maximum SEP-IRA contribution?

a) $40,000
b) $46,000
c) $50,000
d) $69,000

**Answer:** a) $40,000 — For self-employed individuals, the effective SEP contribution rate is 20% of net SE income (25% / 1.25 due to the circular calculation). $200,000 x 20% = $40,000. This is below the $69,000 maximum, so $40,000 is the limit.

---

**2.** A taxpayer born in 1955 has a Traditional IRA balance of $400,000 as of December 31, 2027. The Uniform Lifetime Table divisor for age 73 is 26.5. The taxpayer turns 73 in 2028. What is the penalty for failing to take any RMD for 2028?

a) No penalty — RMDs do not begin until age 75
b) $3,774 (25% of the required distribution)
c) $7,547 (50% of the required distribution)
d) $15,094 (100% of the required distribution)

**Answer:** b) $3,774 (25% of the required distribution) — The taxpayer born in 1955 falls in the 1951-1959 bracket and must begin RMDs at age 73. The RMD is $400,000 / 26.5 = $15,094. Under SECURE 2.0, the excise tax for failure to take an RMD was reduced from 50% to 25% of the shortfall: $15,094 x 25% = $3,774. If corrected within 2 years, the penalty further reduces to 10%.

---

**3.** A taxpayer wants to roll over unused Section 529 plan funds to a Roth IRA for the beneficiary. The account has existed for 20 years and has a balance of $50,000. What is the maximum amount that can be rolled over in a single year?

a) $7,000 (annual Roth IRA contribution limit)
b) $35,000 (lifetime cap)
c) $50,000 (full balance)
d) $0 — rollovers from 529 plans to Roth IRAs are not permitted

**Answer:** a) $7,000 (annual Roth IRA contribution limit) — While the lifetime cap for 529-to-Roth rollovers is $35,000, each year's rollover is limited to the annual Roth IRA contribution limit ($7,000 for 2025). The account meets the 15-year existence requirement. The beneficiary would need approximately 5 years to roll over the full $35,000 lifetime maximum.
