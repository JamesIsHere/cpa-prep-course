# Property Transactions: Gains and Losses

## Learning Objectives

- Calculate realized and recognized gain or loss on the disposition of property
- Distinguish between capital assets, Section 1231 assets, and ordinary income property
- Apply the depreciation recapture rules under Sections 1245 and 1250
- Determine the tax treatment of capital gains and losses for individuals

## Realized vs. Recognized Gain or Loss

When property is disposed of, two calculations are required:

**Realized gain or loss** = Amount realized - Adjusted basis

The **amount realized** includes everything received in the exchange: cash, FMV of property received, FMV of services received, and liabilities assumed by the buyer.

**Recognized gain or loss** is the portion of the realized gain or loss that must be reported on the tax return. Generally, all realized gains and losses are recognized unless a specific Code provision provides for deferral or exclusion (such as Section 1031 like-kind exchanges or Section 121 home sale exclusion).

**Worked Example:**

A taxpayer sells a rental building with an adjusted basis of $150,000. The buyer pays $100,000 cash and assumes the taxpayer's $120,000 mortgage.

| Component | Amount |
|-----------|--------|
| Cash received | $100,000 |
| Mortgage assumed by buyer | $120,000 |
| **Amount realized** | **$220,000** |
| Adjusted basis | ($150,000) |
| **Realized gain** | **$70,000** |

Unless a deferral provision applies, the entire $70,000 is recognized.

## Capital Assets vs. Ordinary Assets

The tax treatment of a gain or loss depends on the **classification of the asset**:

### Capital Assets (Section 1221)

A **capital asset** is any property held by the taxpayer **except**:

- Inventory or property held primarily for sale to customers
- Depreciable property and real property used in a trade or business
- Accounts and notes receivable from business operations
- Supplies consumed in the ordinary course of business
- Certain copyrights, literary, musical, or artistic compositions (held by the creator)
- U.S. government publications received at below-market cost

Common examples of capital assets: stocks, bonds, personal-use property (home, car, jewelry), and investment real estate not used in a trade or business.

> Exam tip: Capital assets are defined by **exclusion** — everything is a capital asset unless it falls into one of the specific exceptions. This reverse definition is a favorite exam question.

### Section 1231 Assets

**Section 1231 assets** are depreciable property and real property used in a trade or business and held for more than one year. These include:

- Business buildings and equipment
- Timber, coal, and domestic iron ore
- Unharvested crops on land sold together
- Certain livestock

Section 1231 provides the **best of both worlds**:
- Net Section 1231 **gains** are treated as **long-term capital gains** (lower tax rates)
- Net Section 1231 **losses** are treated as **ordinary losses** (fully deductible against ordinary income, no $3,000 annual limitation)

However, the **five-year lookback rule** requires that net Section 1231 gains must first be treated as ordinary income to the extent of any unrecaptured net Section 1231 losses from the prior five years.

## Depreciation Recapture

When depreciable business property is sold at a gain, a portion of the gain may be recharacterized as **ordinary income** to recapture the tax benefit of prior depreciation deductions. The two main recapture provisions are Sections 1245 and 1250.

### Section 1245 Recapture

**Section 1245** applies to depreciable **personal property** (equipment, machinery, vehicles, furniture) and certain other property.

**Rule:** The gain is treated as **ordinary income** to the extent of **all depreciation previously allowed or allowable**. Any gain exceeding total depreciation is treated as Section 1231 gain.

**Worked Example:**

A taxpayer sells equipment for $45,000. The equipment was purchased for $60,000 and $25,000 of depreciation has been claimed. Adjusted basis is $35,000.

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| Amount realized | | $45,000 |
| Adjusted basis | $60,000 - $25,000 | $35,000 |
| Realized gain | $45,000 - $35,000 | $10,000 |
| Section 1245 ordinary income | Lesser of gain ($10,000) or depreciation ($25,000) | **$10,000** |
| Section 1231 gain | $10,000 - $10,000 | **$0** |

The entire $10,000 gain is ordinary income because it does not exceed the $25,000 of depreciation claimed.

Now assume the equipment sells for $70,000 instead:

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| Realized gain | $70,000 - $35,000 | $35,000 |
| Section 1245 ordinary income | Limited to depreciation | **$25,000** |
| Section 1231 gain | $35,000 - $25,000 | **$10,000** |

### Section 1250 Recapture

**Section 1250** applies to depreciable **real property** (buildings, structural components). Under current law, most real property is depreciated using the straight-line method, which means there is typically no Section 1250 recapture because Section 1250 only recaptures depreciation **in excess of straight-line**.

However, **unrecaptured Section 1250 gain** is taxed at a maximum rate of **25%** rather than the standard long-term capital gains rate. This applies to the portion of gain attributable to straight-line depreciation taken on real property.

**Worked Example:**

A taxpayer sells a rental building for $400,000. Original cost was $300,000 and $50,000 of straight-line depreciation has been claimed. Adjusted basis is $250,000.

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| Realized gain | $400,000 - $250,000 | $150,000 |
| Section 1250 recapture (excess over SL) | None (straight-line used) | $0 |
| Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain (taxed at 25%) | Equal to depreciation | **$50,000** |
| Remaining gain (taxed at LTCG rates) | $150,000 - $50,000 | **$100,000** |

> Exam tip: Section 1245 recapture applies to personal property and recaptures ALL depreciation as ordinary income. Section 1250 recapture applies to real property and typically only creates the 25% unrecaptured gain category. Do not confuse the two.

## Section 1245 and 1250 Recapture — Detail

### Section 1245 — Full Recapture

Section 1245 applies broadly to depreciable **personal property** and certain real property that was or could have been depreciated using an accelerated method. Covered property includes:

- Machinery, equipment, vehicles, furniture, fixtures
- Tangible personal property used in a trade or business
- Amortizable Section 197 intangibles (goodwill, patents, covenants not to compete)
- Single-purpose agricultural and horticultural structures
- Storage facilities for petroleum products
- Railroad grading and tunnel bores

**Rule:** On disposition, gain is taxed as **ordinary income** to the full extent of depreciation (or amortization) previously allowed or allowable. Only gain exceeding total depreciation is treated as Section 1231 gain.

Section 1245 recapture overrides Section 1231 — you must first recapture all depreciation as ordinary income before any remaining gain gets Section 1231 (and potentially LTCG) treatment.

### Section 1250 — Excess Depreciation Recapture

Section 1250 applies to depreciable **real property** (buildings and structural components). It recaptures only the depreciation **in excess of straight-line** as ordinary income.

Since current law requires straight-line depreciation for real property (27.5-year residential, 39-year nonresidential), there is usually no excess depreciation and therefore no Section 1250 ordinary income recapture.

However, the concept is still tested in two scenarios:
1. **Pre-1987 real property** that was depreciated using accelerated methods (ACRS)
2. **Hypothetical exam questions** testing knowledge of the recapture rule

### Unrecaptured Section 1250 Gain

Even when there is no Section 1250 ordinary recapture, gain attributable to **straight-line depreciation** on real property is classified as **unrecaptured Section 1250 gain** and taxed at a maximum rate of **25%** (rather than the 0%/15%/20% LTCG rates).

The ordering of gain on the sale of depreciable real property is:
1. Section 1250 ordinary recapture (excess over straight-line) — ordinary rates
2. Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain (straight-line depreciation) — max 25%
3. Remaining gain — LTCG rates (0%/15%/20%)

### Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT)

The **Net Investment Income Tax** (Section 1411) imposes an additional **3.8% tax** on the lesser of:
- Net investment income, or
- Modified AGI exceeding the threshold ($200,000 single; $250,000 MFJ)

**Net investment income includes:**
- Interest, dividends, annuities, royalties, rents
- Capital gains (both short-term and long-term)
- Passive business income
- Gain from the sale of investment property

**Net investment income excludes:**
- Wages and self-employment income
- Active trade or business income (if the taxpayer materially participates)
- Distributions from qualified retirement plans and IRAs
- Tax-exempt interest

**Worked Example:**

A married couple filing jointly has $180,000 in wages, $40,000 in long-term capital gains, and $50,000 in rental income. Their MAGI is $270,000.

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| Net investment income | $40,000 + $50,000 | $90,000 |
| MAGI over threshold | $270,000 − $250,000 | $20,000 |
| NIIT | 3.8% × lesser of $90,000 or $20,000 | **$760** |

> Exam tip: The NIIT is tested as a separate calculation. Remember it applies to investment income (not wages), uses the $200K/$250K thresholds (not indexed for inflation), and is **in addition to** regular income tax and capital gains tax.

## Capital Gains and Losses for Individuals

### Tax Rates

Long-term capital gains (assets held more than one year) are taxed at preferential rates:

- **0%** — for taxpayers in the 10% and 12% ordinary income brackets
- **15%** — for most taxpayers (up to $533,800 married filing jointly in 2026)
- **20%** — for taxpayers in the highest ordinary income bracket
- **25%** — for unrecaptured Section 1250 gain
- **28%** — for collectibles (art, coins, stamps, antiques)

Short-term capital gains (assets held one year or less) are taxed at **ordinary income rates**.

### Capital Loss Limitations

Capital losses offset capital gains dollar for dollar. If capital losses exceed capital gains, individuals may deduct up to **$3,000** ($1,500 married filing separately) of net capital losses against ordinary income per year. Excess capital losses are **carried forward indefinitely** and retain their character (short-term or long-term).

### Netting Process

Capital gains and losses are netted in a specific order:

1. Net short-term gains against short-term losses
2. Net long-term gains against long-term losses
3. If one category has a net gain and the other a net loss, net them against each other
4. Apply the $3,000 limitation to any remaining net loss

**Worked Example:**

A taxpayer has the following capital transactions:

| Transaction | Amount | Character |
|------------|--------|-----------|
| Stock A sold | $8,000 gain | Short-term |
| Stock B sold | ($12,000) loss | Short-term |
| Stock C sold | $15,000 gain | Long-term |
| Stock D sold | ($5,000) loss | Long-term |

Step 1: Net short-term: $8,000 - $12,000 = **($4,000) net short-term loss**
Step 2: Net long-term: $15,000 - $5,000 = **$10,000 net long-term gain**
Step 3: Combine: ($4,000) + $10,000 = **$6,000 net long-term capital gain**

The $6,000 is taxed at the preferential long-term capital gains rate.

## Key Terms

- **Amount realized** — the total value received on the disposition of property, including cash, FMV of property received, and liabilities assumed by the buyer
- **Section 1231 asset** — depreciable property or real property used in a trade or business held for more than one year
- **Section 1245 recapture** — the recharacterization of gain as ordinary income to the extent of all depreciation previously claimed on personal property
- **Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain** — the portion of gain on real property attributable to straight-line depreciation, taxed at a maximum 25% rate
- **Capital loss carryforward** — excess capital losses that cannot be used in the current year, carried forward indefinitely to future years

## Practice Problems

**1.** A taxpayer sells business equipment for $30,000. The equipment was purchased for $50,000, and $28,000 of depreciation has been claimed. How much of the gain is ordinary income under Section 1245?

a) $2,000
b) $8,000
c) $22,000
d) $28,000

**Answer:** b) $8,000 — Adjusted basis = $50,000 - $28,000 = $22,000. Realized gain = $30,000 - $22,000 = $8,000. Section 1245 recaptures gain as ordinary income to the extent of depreciation claimed ($28,000), but cannot exceed the actual gain ($8,000). Therefore, the entire $8,000 gain is ordinary income.

---

**2.** A taxpayer has $5,000 in short-term capital gains and $15,000 in long-term capital losses. What amount can the taxpayer deduct against ordinary income in the current year?

a) $3,000
b) $5,000
c) $10,000
d) $15,000

**Answer:** a) $3,000 — Net the gains and losses: $5,000 - $15,000 = ($10,000) net capital loss. The maximum deduction against ordinary income is $3,000 per year. The remaining $7,000 is carried forward to future years.

---

**3.** Which of the following is a capital asset?

a) Equipment used in a taxpayer's business
b) Inventory held for sale to customers
c) Stock held as a personal investment
d) Accounts receivable from business services

**Answer:** c) Stock held as a personal investment — Capital assets are defined by exclusion. Business equipment is a Section 1231 asset, inventory and accounts receivable are ordinary assets. Stock held for investment is a capital asset.
