# Tax-Exempt Organizations

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the requirements for obtaining and maintaining Section 501(c)(3) tax-exempt status
- Distinguish between public charities and private foundations
- Apply the three-part test for unrelated business income tax (UBIT)
- Identify statutory exclusions from UBIT
- Understand private foundation excise taxes and operational restrictions
- Describe the Form 990 filing requirements and key schedules

## Section 501(c)(3) Overview

Section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code grants tax-exempt status to organizations that are organized and operated **exclusively** for one or more of the following purposes:

- Religious
- Charitable
- Scientific
- Testing for public safety
- Literary
- Educational
- Fostering national or international amateur sports competition
- Prevention of cruelty to children or animals

To qualify, an organization must meet two fundamental tests:

### Organizational Test

The organization's **governing documents** (articles of incorporation, trust instrument, or articles of association) must:

- **Limit the organization's purposes** to one or more exempt purposes under 501(c)(3)
- **Not expressly empower** the organization to engage in activities that are not in furtherance of exempt purposes (other than an insubstantial part)
- Provide that upon dissolution, assets will be distributed to another exempt organization or to the government — this is the **dissolution clause**

### Operational Test

The organization must be **operated primarily** for exempt purposes. This means:

- It engages primarily in activities that accomplish exempt purposes
- No part of net earnings **inures to the benefit** of any private shareholder or individual (the **private inurement prohibition**)
- It does not provide **excessive private benefit** to any individual, whether or not they are insiders
- It does not devote a **substantial part** of its activities to influencing legislation (**lobbying restriction**) — organizations may elect the Section 501(h) expenditure test as a safe harbor
- It does **not participate or intervene** in any political campaign for or against a candidate for public office (**absolute prohibition** on political campaign activity)

**Key distinction — lobbying vs. political activity:**

| Activity | Rule |
|----------|------|
| Lobbying (influencing legislation) | Permitted if not a "substantial part" of activities; 501(h) election provides specific dollar thresholds |
| Political campaign activity | **Absolutely prohibited** — any participation can result in loss of exempt status |

## Public Charity vs. Private Foundation

All 501(c)(3) organizations are presumed to be **private foundations** unless they demonstrate they qualify as a **public charity**. The distinction matters because private foundations face additional restrictions and excise taxes.

### Public Charity Qualifications

An organization qualifies as a public charity if it meets one of several tests:

- **Broadly supported:** Receives a substantial part of support from the general public or government (the one-third support test under Section 509(a)(1))
- **Gross receipts test:** Normally receives more than one-third of support from contributions, membership fees, and gross receipts from activities related to exempt functions (Section 509(a)(2))
- **Supporting organization:** Operated exclusively to support one or more public charities (Section 509(a)(3))
- **Public safety testing:** Tests for public safety under Section 509(a)(4))
- **Churches, schools, hospitals:** Automatically classified as public charities regardless of funding sources

### Private Foundation Restrictions

Private foundations are subject to a series of **excise taxes** under Sections 4940–4945 that penalize prohibited activities:

| Section | Prohibition | Excise Tax |
|---------|-------------|------------|
| 4940 | **Net investment income** | 1.39% tax on net investment income |
| 4941 | **Self-dealing** between foundation and disqualified persons | 10% on self-dealer; 5% on foundation manager who knowingly participates |
| 4942 | **Failure to distribute income** — must distribute at least 5% of non-charitable-use assets annually | 30% on undistributed amount |
| 4943 | **Excess business holdings** — foundation + disqualified persons cannot own more than 20% of voting stock | 10% on excess holdings |
| 4944 | **Jeopardizing investments** — investments that risk the foundation's ability to carry out exempt purposes | 10% on amount invested |
| 4945 | **Taxable expenditures** — lobbying, political activity, grants to individuals without IRS approval, grants to non-charities without expenditure responsibility | 20% on the expenditure |

**Worked Example — Self-Dealing:**

The Smith Family Foundation purchases office equipment from Smith Industries (owned by the founder's family) for $50,000 at fair market value.

- **Result:** This is a self-dealing transaction under Section 4941. Self-dealing is based on the **nature** of the transaction, not whether it was at arm's length. Any sale, exchange, or leasing of property between a private foundation and a disqualified person is prohibited regardless of the price.
- **Tax:** 10% initial tax on the self-dealer ($5,000) and 5% on any foundation manager who knowingly approved the transaction.

## Unrelated Business Income Tax (UBIT)

Tax-exempt organizations are subject to tax on income from activities that are **unrelated** to their exempt purpose. UBIT prevents tax-exempt organizations from having an unfair competitive advantage over taxable businesses.

### The Three-Part Test

Income is subject to UBIT if **all three** conditions are met:

1. **Trade or business:** The activity is carried on for the production of income from selling goods or performing services
2. **Regularly carried on:** The activity is conducted with a frequency and continuity comparable to commercial activities of taxable organizations
3. **Not substantially related:** The activity does not contribute importantly to the organization's exempt purposes (the fact that income is used for exempt purposes does not make the activity related)

### Statutory Exclusions from UBIT

Even if income meets the three-part test, the following are **excluded** from UBIT:

| Exclusion | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Volunteer labor** | Activity in which substantially all work is performed by unpaid volunteers |
| **Donated goods** | Sale of merchandise where substantially all items were donated (e.g., thrift store) |
| **Convenience** | Activity conducted for the convenience of members, students, patients, or employees |
| **Passive income** | Dividends, interest, annuities, royalties, and certain rents from real property |
| **Research** | Research performed for the U.S. government, a college/university, or research available to the public |
| **Bingo games** | Legal bingo where commercial bingo is not ordinarily carried out in the jurisdiction |

### Modifications to UBIT

- **Rents from real property** are generally excluded, but rents from **personal property** are included unless incidental (less than 10% of total rents under the lease)
- **Debt-financed property:** Income from property acquired with debt (e.g., rental real estate purchased with a mortgage) is subject to UBIT to the extent the property is debt-financed. The percentage of income subject to tax equals the **average acquisition indebtedness** divided by the **average adjusted basis** of the property
- **Controlled entity payments:** Rent, interest, or royalties received from a **controlled** subsidiary (80%+ ownership) are treated as UBIT to the extent they exceed fair market value
- **Net operating losses:** UBIT allows an NOL deduction, computed using only UBI items
- **Specific deduction:** A $1,000 specific deduction is allowed against UBIT (effectively exempting the first $1,000 of UBI)

**Worked Example — UBIT Analysis:**

A university hospital operates a gift shop on its premises that is open to the general public. The gift shop sells greeting cards, magazines, candy, and personal care items. Annual net income from the gift shop is $120,000.

- **Trade or business?** Yes — selling goods for income.
- **Regularly carried on?** Yes — the shop is open daily year-round.
- **Substantially related?** Likely **no** — selling general merchandise to the public does not contribute importantly to the hospital's exempt purpose of providing medical care. (A pharmacy selling prescriptions to patients might be related; a general gift shop is typically not.)
- **Convenience exclusion?** If the shop primarily serves patients, their families, and hospital employees, the convenience exclusion may apply. If primarily serving the general public, the exclusion does not apply.

## Form 990 Filing Requirements

Most tax-exempt organizations must file an annual information return with the IRS. The specific form depends on the organization's size and type:

| Form | Who Files | Requirements |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| Form 990 | Organizations with gross receipts ≥ $200,000 OR total assets ≥ $500,000 | Full return with financial statements, governance, and compensation disclosure |
| Form 990-EZ | Gross receipts < $200,000 AND total assets < $500,000 | Simplified version of Form 990 |
| Form 990-N (e-Postcard) | Gross receipts ≤ $50,000 | Electronic notice — name, address, EIN, confirmation of continued eligibility |
| Form 990-PF | All private foundations regardless of size | Detailed return including excise tax computations |
| Form 990-T | Any exempt organization with ≥ $1,000 gross UBI | Reports unrelated business income and computes UBIT |

**Key Form 990 schedules:**

- **Schedule A** — Public Charity Status and Public Support
- **Schedule B** — Schedule of Contributors (confidential — not publicly available)
- **Schedule D** — Supplemental Financial Statements
- **Schedule J** — Compensation Information (officers, directors, key employees)
- **Schedule O** — Supplemental Information (narrative explanations)

**Filing deadline:** The 15th day of the 5th month after the end of the organization's tax year (May 15 for calendar-year organizations). Extensions of up to 6 months are available using Form 8868.

**Penalty for failure to file:** $20 per day (up to $10,500 or 5% of gross receipts, whichever is less) for organizations with gross receipts under $1,186,000. For larger organizations, $100 per day up to $53,000.

**Automatic revocation:** If an organization fails to file Form 990 (or 990-EZ or 990-N) for **three consecutive years**, its tax-exempt status is **automatically revoked**.

## Intermediate Sanctions (Section 4958)

Rather than revoking exempt status for every instance of private benefit, the IRS may impose **intermediate sanctions** — excise taxes on **excess benefit transactions** between a 501(c)(3) or 501(c)(4) organization and a **disqualified person** (officers, directors, and those with substantial influence over the organization).

**Excise taxes on excess benefit transactions:**

- **Initial tax on disqualified person:** 25% of the excess benefit
- **Additional tax on disqualified person:** 200% of the excess benefit if not corrected within the taxable period
- **Tax on organization manager:** 10% of the excess benefit (up to $20,000 per transaction) if the manager knowingly participated

**Rebuttable presumption of reasonableness:** Compensation is presumed reasonable if the arrangement was approved by an independent board, the board relied on appropriate comparability data, and the board documented its decision.

## Key Exam Tips

- The **absolute prohibition** is on political campaign activity — not lobbying. Lobbying is permitted if not a substantial part of activities
- **Self-dealing** under Section 4941 is tested on the **nature of the transaction**, not whether the price was fair — even a sale at FMV is prohibited
- A private foundation must distribute at least **5%** of non-charitable-use assets annually
- **UBIT requires all three parts** of the test: trade or business + regularly carried on + not substantially related
- The **$1,000 specific deduction** means the first $1,000 of UBI is effectively tax-free
- **Three consecutive years** of failure to file Form 990 results in **automatic revocation** of tax-exempt status
- **Passive income** (dividends, interest, royalties, real property rents) is generally excluded from UBIT
- Debt-financed income is subject to UBIT in proportion to the **debt/basis ratio**
