# Like-Kind Exchanges

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the requirements for a tax-deferred exchange under Section 1031
- Determine which properties qualify as like-kind and which do not
- Calculate the basis of property received in a like-kind exchange, including the effect of boot
- Apply the timeline requirements for deferred and reverse exchanges

## Section 1031 Overview

A **like-kind exchange** under Section 1031 allows a taxpayer to defer recognition of gain or loss when exchanging qualifying property held for productive use in a trade or business or for investment. The economic rationale is that the taxpayer's investment continues in a similar form — only the specific property has changed, not the nature of the investment.

The key word is **deferral**, not exclusion. The gain is not eliminated; it is preserved in the basis of the replacement property and will be recognized when that property is eventually sold in a taxable transaction.

> Exam tip: Section 1031 is mandatory, not elective. If a transaction qualifies as a like-kind exchange, the taxpayer must defer the gain — they cannot choose to recognize it instead (unless boot is received).

## Qualifying Property

### What Qualifies

Section 1031 applies only to **real property** held for productive use in a trade or business or for investment. After the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, like-kind exchange treatment is no longer available for personal property (equipment, vehicles, machinery, artwork).

Examples of qualifying exchanges:

- Rental apartment building for an office building
- Farmland for a commercial lot
- A retail strip mall for an industrial warehouse
- Undeveloped investment land for a rental property

Real property located in the United States can only be exchanged for other U.S. real property. Foreign real property can only be exchanged for other foreign real property.

### What Does Not Qualify

- **Personal property** — equipment, vehicles, machinery, furniture (after 2017)
- **Inventory** — property held primarily for sale
- **Stocks, bonds, and other securities**
- **Partnership interests**
- **Personal-use property** — a primary residence or vacation home used exclusively for personal purposes
- **Property held primarily for resale** — such as by real estate dealers or developers

> Exam tip: Since 2018, Section 1031 is limited to real property only. If an exam question involves equipment or vehicle exchanges, the answer is that Section 1031 does not apply.

## Boot

**Boot** is any non-like-kind property received in the exchange. Common forms of boot include:

- Cash received
- Personal property received (e.g., a vehicle included in a real estate deal)
- Net debt relief (the other party assumes more of your liabilities than you assume of theirs)

### Gain Recognition with Boot

When boot is received, the taxpayer must recognize gain to the extent of the boot received, but **never more than the realized gain**. Losses are **never** recognized in a like-kind exchange, even when boot is received.

**Worked Example:**

Taylor exchanges a rental property (adjusted basis $200,000, FMV $350,000) for another rental property (FMV $300,000) plus $50,000 cash.

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| FMV of property received | | $300,000 |
| Cash (boot) received | | $50,000 |
| Amount realized | $300,000 + $50,000 | $350,000 |
| Adjusted basis of property given up | | ($200,000) |
| Realized gain | | $150,000 |
| Boot received | | $50,000 |
| **Recognized gain** | Lesser of realized gain or boot | **$50,000** |
| Deferred gain | $150,000 - $50,000 | $100,000 |

### Net Debt Relief as Boot

When both parties have liabilities assumed by the other, you net the liabilities. If the taxpayer receives **net debt relief** (their liabilities assumed exceed the liabilities they assume), the net relief is treated as boot.

**Worked Example:**

Adams exchanges property (basis $100,000, FMV $250,000, mortgage $80,000) for Baker's property (FMV $200,000, mortgage $30,000). Baker assumes Adams's $80,000 mortgage; Adams assumes Baker's $30,000 mortgage.

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| FMV of property received | | $200,000 |
| Mortgage relief (given up) | | $80,000 |
| Mortgage assumed (received) | | ($30,000) |
| Net debt relief (boot) | $80,000 - $30,000 | $50,000 |
| Amount realized | $200,000 + $50,000 | $250,000 |
| Adjusted basis | | ($100,000) |
| Realized gain | | $150,000 |
| **Recognized gain** | Lesser of $150,000 or $50,000 | **$50,000** |

## Basis of Replacement Property

The basis of the replacement property is calculated to preserve the deferred gain. The formula is:

**Basis of property received = FMV of property received - Deferred gain + Deferred loss**

An equivalent formula that may be easier to apply:

**Basis of property received = Adjusted basis of property given up - Boot given + Boot received + Gain recognized - Loss recognized**

**Continuing the first example above:**

| Calculation | Amount |
|------------|--------|
| FMV of property received | $300,000 |
| Deferred gain | ($100,000) |
| **Basis of replacement property** | **$200,000** |

**Verification:** If Taylor later sells the replacement property for $300,000:
- Gain = $300,000 - $200,000 = $100,000
- This equals the deferred gain, confirming the basis calculation preserves the deferral

## Timeline Requirements

Most like-kind exchanges are not simultaneous swaps. A **deferred exchange** (also called a Starker exchange) uses a **qualified intermediary** to facilitate the transaction. Strict timelines apply:

### 45-Day Identification Period

The taxpayer must **identify** potential replacement properties in writing within **45 days** of transferring the relinquished property. Identification rules include:

- **Three-property rule** — up to three properties may be identified regardless of value
- **200% rule** — any number of properties may be identified if their combined FMV does not exceed 200% of the FMV of the relinquished property
- **95% rule** — any number of properties may be identified if the taxpayer acquires at least 95% of the total identified value

### 180-Day Exchange Period

The taxpayer must **receive** the replacement property within the earlier of:

- **180 days** after transferring the relinquished property, or
- The **due date** (including extensions) of the taxpayer's tax return for the year of the transfer

> Exam tip: The 45-day and 180-day deadlines run concurrently (the 180 days starts on the same day as the 45 days). These are calendar days, not business days, and there are no extensions. Missing either deadline disqualifies the exchange entirely.

### Related Party Rules

If a like-kind exchange is made with a **related party** (as defined under Section 267), both parties must hold the property received for at least **two years** after the exchange. If either party disposes of the property within two years, the deferred gain is recognized.

## Other Nonrecognition Transactions

Beyond Section 1031, the CPA exam tests several other deferral and exclusion provisions for property transactions.

### Installment Sales (Section 453)

An **installment sale** allows a taxpayer to defer gain recognition by spreading it over the years in which payments are received. The gain is reported proportionally using the **gross profit percentage**.

**Gross profit percentage** = Gross profit / Contract price

- **Gross profit** = Selling price − Adjusted basis
- **Contract price** = Selling price − Mortgage assumed by buyer (but not below zero) + Excess mortgage over basis (if any)

Each payment received is split into three components:
1. **Return of basis** (nontaxable)
2. **Gain** (gross profit percentage × payment received)
3. **Interest income** (reported separately as ordinary income)

**Worked Example:**

A taxpayer sells land (basis $60,000) for $100,000 — $20,000 down and $80,000 note payable over 4 years.

| Calculation | Amount |
|------------|--------|
| Gross profit | $100,000 − $60,000 = $40,000 |
| Contract price | $100,000 |
| Gross profit % | $40,000 / $100,000 = 40% |
| Gain per $20,000 payment | $20,000 × 40% = $8,000 |

The taxpayer recognizes $8,000 of gain in each year a $20,000 payment is received.

**Installment method not available for:**
- Sales of inventory or dealer property
- Sales of publicly traded securities
- Depreciation recapture — all Section 1245/1250 recapture is recognized in the year of sale, even if payments are received later
- Elected out by the taxpayer (reporting all gain in year of sale)

**Related party installment sales:** If a taxpayer sells property to a related party on the installment method and the related party resells within 2 years, the original seller must recognize the remaining deferred gain immediately (Section 453(e)).

### Involuntary Conversions (Section 1033)

An **involuntary conversion** occurs when property is destroyed, stolen, condemned, or disposed of under threat of condemnation. If the taxpayer receives insurance proceeds or a condemnation award, gain may be deferred under Section 1033.

**Deferral rules:**
- Gain is deferred if the taxpayer reinvests the proceeds in **similar or related-in-use property** (a narrower standard than "like-kind")
- For condemnations of real property used in a trade or business or held for investment, the replacement standard is relaxed to **like-kind** property (same as Section 1031)
- Gain recognized = Amount realized − Cost of replacement property (but only if replacement cost is less than amount realized)
- The basis of replacement property is reduced by the deferred gain

**Replacement period:**
- **General rule:** The end of the second taxable year after the year the gain is realized
- **Condemnation of real property:** The end of the third taxable year
- **Federally declared disasters:** The end of the fourth taxable year

**Worked Example:**

A taxpayer's rental building (basis $200,000) is destroyed by fire. Insurance proceeds are $350,000. The taxpayer purchases a replacement building for $320,000 within two years.

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| Realized gain | $350,000 − $200,000 | $150,000 |
| Amount not reinvested | $350,000 − $320,000 | $30,000 |
| Recognized gain | Lesser of gain or amount not reinvested | $30,000 |
| Deferred gain | $150,000 − $30,000 | $120,000 |
| Basis of replacement | $320,000 − $120,000 | $200,000 |

### Section 121 — Home Sale Exclusion

A taxpayer may exclude up to **$250,000** ($500,000 for married filing jointly) of gain on the sale of a **principal residence** if:
- The taxpayer owned and used the home as a principal residence for at least **2 of the 5 years** before the sale (the ownership and use tests need not be concurrent)
- The exclusion was not used within the prior **2 years**

**Partial exclusion:** If the taxpayer fails to meet the full requirements due to a change in employment, health, or unforeseen circumstances, a prorated exclusion is available based on the fraction of the 2-year requirement met.

### Related Party Rules (Section 267)

Transactions between related parties are subject to special restrictions:

**No loss recognition:** Losses on sales between related parties are **disallowed**. The buyer takes a cost basis but can offset the disallowed loss against any future gain when the buyer sells the property to an unrelated party.

**Related parties include:**
- Family members (siblings, spouse, ancestors, lineal descendants — but not aunts, uncles, cousins, or in-laws)
- An individual and a corporation in which the individual owns more than 50%
- Two corporations in the same controlled group
- A trust and its beneficiaries or grantor
- A partnership and a partner owning more than 50%

**Constructive ownership** applies — stock owned by family members and entities is attributed to the taxpayer for purposes of the 50% test.

**Matching rule (Section 267(a)(2)):** Accrual-basis related parties cannot deduct accrued expenses payable to cash-basis related parties until the payment is actually made and included in the recipient's income.

> Exam tip: The disallowed loss rule between related parties is one of the most frequently tested REG concepts. Remember that the loss is permanently disallowed to the seller, but the buyer gets a partial benefit — the disallowed loss can offset gain when the buyer eventually sells to an unrelated party.

## Key Terms

- **Like-kind property** — real property of the same nature or character, regardless of grade or quality, held for business use or investment
- **Boot** — non-like-kind property received in an exchange, including cash and net debt relief, which triggers gain recognition
- **Qualified intermediary** — a third party that facilitates a deferred like-kind exchange by holding proceeds between the sale and acquisition
- **Relinquished property** — the property given up by the taxpayer in a like-kind exchange
- **Replacement property** — the property received by the taxpayer in a like-kind exchange
- **45-day identification period** — the window in which the taxpayer must identify potential replacement properties in writing
