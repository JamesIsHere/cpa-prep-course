# Business Law: Contracts

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the elements required to form a valid contract
- Apply the Statute of Frauds to determine when a written agreement is required
- Distinguish between material and minor breach and their remedies
- Calculate expectation, reliance, and restitution damages in contract disputes

## Contract Formation

A **contract** is a legally enforceable agreement between two or more parties. To form a valid contract, four elements must be present:

1. **Offer** — a clear, definite proposal by the offeror indicating willingness to enter into a contract on specific terms
2. **Acceptance** — the offeree's unqualified agreement to the exact terms of the offer
3. **Consideration** — something of legal value exchanged by each party (money, services, a promise to act or refrain from acting)
4. **Legal capacity** — both parties must have the legal ability to contract (not minors, mentally incapacitated, or intoxicated)

### The Offer

An offer must be communicated to the offeree and must contain sufficiently definite terms. Under common law, essential terms include the parties, subject matter, price, and time for performance. Advertisements are generally **invitations to negotiate**, not offers — unless they are specific and leave nothing open for negotiation.

An offer can be terminated by:

- **Revocation** by the offeror (effective when received by the offeree)
- **Rejection** or **counteroffer** by the offeree
- **Lapse of time** specified in the offer or a reasonable time
- **Death or incapacity** of either party
- **Destruction** of the subject matter

> Exam tip: An option contract prevents the offeror from revoking the offer during the option period. Under the UCC, a merchant's **firm offer** is irrevocable for up to three months without separate consideration.

### Acceptance

Under the **mirror image rule** (common law), acceptance must match the offer exactly. Any modification is treated as a counteroffer. Under the UCC (for sales of goods), the rules are more flexible — additional or different terms in the acceptance may become part of the contract between merchants.

The **mailbox rule** provides that acceptance is effective when dispatched (mailed), while revocation is effective when received.

## Consideration and Its Exceptions

**Consideration** requires a bargained-for exchange. Each party must give up something of legal value. The following do not constitute valid consideration:

- **Past consideration** — a promise made in exchange for something already done
- **Pre-existing duty** — a promise to do what one is already legally required to do
- **Illusory promises** — statements that do not actually commit the promisor to anything

Exceptions where a promise is enforceable without traditional consideration include:

- **Promissory estoppel** — when a party reasonably relies on a promise to their detriment
- **Modification under the UCC** — good-faith modifications to contracts for the sale of goods need no new consideration
- **Promises to pay debts barred by the statute of limitations** — enforceable if in writing

## The Statute of Frauds

The **Statute of Frauds** requires certain contracts to be in writing and signed by the party to be charged. The common mnemonic is **MY LEGS**:

- **M**arriage — contracts made in consideration of marriage
- **Y**ear — contracts that cannot be performed within one year from the date of making
- **L**and — contracts involving the sale or transfer of an interest in real property
- **E**xecutor — promises by an executor to pay estate debts from personal funds
- **G**oods — sale of goods priced at $500 or more (UCC Section 2-201)
- **S**urety — promises to pay the debt of another

### What Satisfies the Writing Requirement?

The writing must identify the parties, describe the subject matter, state the essential terms, and be signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought. Under the UCC, the writing need only indicate that a contract was made, specify the quantity, and be signed by the party to be charged.

> Exam tip: Partial performance can take a contract out of the Statute of Frauds. For land contracts, partial performance (payment, possession, or improvements) may make the contract enforceable even without a writing.

## Breach of Contract and Remedies

### Types of Breach

- **Material breach** — failure to perform a substantial part of the contract, which excuses the non-breaching party from further performance and entitles them to damages
- **Minor breach** — failure to perform some part of the contract that does not defeat the purpose; the non-breaching party must still perform but may recover damages for the deficiency
- **Anticipatory breach** — one party clearly indicates before performance is due that they will not perform; the non-breaching party can immediately sue for damages

### Remedies

**Monetary damages** are the primary remedy for breach:

- **Expectation damages** — place the non-breaching party in the position they would have been in had the contract been performed (the "benefit of the bargain")
- **Reliance damages** — reimburse the non-breaching party for expenses incurred in reliance on the contract
- **Restitution** — return of any benefit conferred on the breaching party to prevent unjust enrichment
- **Consequential damages** — additional losses caused by the breach that were foreseeable at the time of contracting (e.g., lost profits)
- **Liquidated damages** — a pre-agreed amount in the contract, enforceable if it is a reasonable estimate of anticipated harm and actual damages are difficult to calculate

**Equitable remedies** are available when monetary damages are inadequate:

- **Specific performance** — court orders the breaching party to perform (typically for unique goods or real property)
- **Rescission** — the contract is canceled and both parties are restored to their pre-contract positions

**Worked Example:**

A contractor agrees to build a warehouse for $200,000. The owner has a signed contract to lease the warehouse for $30,000 per year. The contractor breaches and the owner must hire another builder for $240,000.

- Expectation damages: $240,000 - $200,000 = **$40,000** (the additional cost to complete)
- Consequential damages: If the delay causes the owner to lose one year of lease income, **$30,000** may be recoverable if the lost lease was foreseeable at the time of contracting

## Defenses to Contract Enforcement

Even when all formation elements are present, a contract may be unenforceable due to:

- **Fraud** — a material misrepresentation of fact made knowingly with intent to deceive
- **Duress** — coercion that overcomes a party's free will (physical threats, economic duress)
- **Undue influence** — abuse of a position of trust or authority over the other party
- **Mistake** — a mutual mistake of material fact may make a contract voidable; a unilateral mistake generally does not unless the other party knew of the error
- **Unconscionability** — terms so one-sided that enforcement would be unjust (procedural or substantive)
- **Illegality** — contracts for illegal purposes are void and unenforceable

## UCC Article 2 — Sale of Goods (Detail)

The Uniform Commercial Code Article 2 governs the sale of **goods** (tangible, movable personal property). The CPA exam tests several UCC concepts beyond basic contract formation.

### Warranties

UCC Article 2 provides three types of warranties:

**Express warranties (Section 2-313)** are created by the seller's:
- Affirmation of fact or promise about the goods (e.g., "This engine produces 300 horsepower")
- Description of the goods (e.g., "Grade A organic wheat")
- Sample or model shown to the buyer

The seller does not need to use the word "warranty" or intend to create one. However, statements of opinion or commendation ("This is the best truck on the market") are **not** express warranties — they are **puffery**.

**Implied warranty of merchantability (Section 2-314)** arises automatically in every sale by a **merchant** seller. The goods must:
- Pass without objection in the trade under the contract description
- Be fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used
- Be adequately contained, packaged, and labeled
- Conform to promises made on the container or label

**Implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose (Section 2-315)** arises when:
1. The seller knows the buyer's particular purpose for the goods
2. The seller knows the buyer is relying on the seller's expertise to select suitable goods
3. The buyer actually relies on the seller's judgment

This warranty can arise from any seller, not just merchants.

### Disclaimer of Warranties

- Express warranties cannot be disclaimed if the disclaimer is inconsistent with the warranty
- Merchantability can be disclaimed by using the word "merchantability" (must be conspicuous if written)
- Fitness for a particular purpose can be disclaimed by a conspicuous written statement
- **"As is"** or **"with all faults"** language disclaims all implied warranties

### Buyer's Remedies

When the seller breaches, the buyer may:

- **Reject nonconforming goods** under the **perfect tender rule** — goods must conform exactly to the contract
- **Revoke acceptance** if a substantial nonconformity impairs the value and the buyer accepted based on the assumption the seller would cure, or the nonconformity was difficult to discover
- **Cover** — buy substitute goods and recover the difference (cover price − contract price)
- **Recover damages** — market price minus contract price if the buyer does not cover
- **Specific performance** — for unique goods when monetary damages are inadequate

### Seller's Remedies

When the buyer breaches, the seller may:

- **Withhold delivery** of undelivered goods
- **Stop goods in transit** if the buyer is insolvent
- **Resell the goods** and recover the difference (contract price − resale price)
- **Recover damages** — contract price minus market price plus incidental damages
- **Recover the price** if the goods cannot be resold at a reasonable price

### Risk of Loss

When goods are damaged or destroyed, risk of loss rules determine who bears the financial burden:

**With a carrier (shipment and destination contracts):**
- **Shipment contract (FOB shipping point):** Risk passes to the buyer when goods are delivered to the carrier
- **Destination contract (FOB destination):** Risk passes to the buyer when goods are tendered at the destination

**Without a carrier:**
- If the seller is a merchant, risk passes when the buyer takes physical possession
- If the seller is not a merchant, risk passes when the seller tenders delivery

> Exam tip: The default UCC rule is a shipment contract (FOB shipping point). If the contract is silent on delivery terms, risk passes to the buyer when goods are delivered to the carrier.

## Key Terms

- **Consideration** — a bargained-for exchange of legal value required for a binding contract
- **Statute of Frauds** — rule requiring certain categories of contracts to be evidenced by a writing
- **Material breach** — a failure of performance significant enough to excuse the other party from performing
- **Expectation damages** — monetary award that places the non-breaching party in the position they would have occupied had the contract been performed
- **Specific performance** — an equitable remedy ordering a party to fulfill their contractual obligations, typically for unique property
- **Promissory estoppel** — a doctrine enforcing a promise without consideration when the promisee reasonably relied on it to their detriment
