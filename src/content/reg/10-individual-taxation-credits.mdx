# Individual Taxation: Credits, AMT, and Payments

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish between refundable and nonrefundable tax credits and their eligibility requirements
- Calculate the child tax credit, earned income credit, and education credits
- Understand the purpose and mechanics of the Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT)
- Apply estimated tax payment rules and the safe harbor provisions for avoiding penalties

## Tax Credits Overview

A **tax credit** is a dollar-for-dollar reduction in tax liability, making credits more valuable than deductions of the same amount. A $1,000 deduction reduces taxable income by $1,000 (saving $220 for a taxpayer in the 22% bracket), while a $1,000 credit reduces the tax owed by the full $1,000.

Credits are classified as either:

- **Nonrefundable credits** — can reduce tax liability to zero but cannot generate a refund. Any excess credit is lost unless a carryforward provision applies.
- **Refundable credits** — can reduce tax liability below zero, resulting in a refund to the taxpayer. These effectively function as direct payments.

> Exam tip: The distinction between refundable and nonrefundable credits appears regularly on the exam. If a question asks whether a credit can result in a refund, the answer depends on its classification.

## Major Individual Tax Credits

### Child Tax Credit

The **Child Tax Credit (CTC)** provides up to **$2,000 per qualifying child** under age 17 at the end of the tax year.

**Qualifying child requirements:**
- Under age 17 at the end of the tax year
- U.S. citizen, national, or resident alien
- Claimed as a dependent on the taxpayer's return
- Lived with the taxpayer for more than half the year

**Phase-out:** The credit begins phasing out at AGI of $200,000 ($400,000 MFJ), reduced by $50 for each $1,000 (or fraction thereof) over the threshold.

Up to **$1,700** of the child tax credit is **refundable** as the Additional Child Tax Credit (ACTC) for taxpayers with earned income exceeding $2,500. A $500 nonrefundable credit is available for other dependents (children 17 and older, dependent parents) who do not qualify for the full CTC.

### Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)

The **Earned Income Tax Credit** is a fully **refundable** credit designed to benefit low- and moderate-income working taxpayers, particularly those with children.

**Key features:**
- Amount varies based on earned income, filing status, and number of qualifying children
- Investment income must be below a threshold (approximately $11,600)
- Both earned income and AGI must be below specified limits
- The credit increases with income (phase-in), reaches a maximum, and then decreases (phase-out)

**Maximum EITC amounts (approximate 2026):**
- No qualifying children: ~$650
- One qualifying child: ~$4,200
- Two qualifying children: ~$6,900
- Three or more: ~$7,800

> Exam tip: The EITC is one of the most important refundable credits. Remember that it requires earned income (wages, self-employment), has an investment income limitation, and the maximum credit increases with the number of qualifying children.

### Education Credits

**American Opportunity Tax Credit (AOTC):**
- Up to **$2,500 per student** for the first four years of post-secondary education
- 100% of the first $2,000 + 25% of the next $2,000 in qualified expenses
- **40% refundable** (up to $1,000), 60% nonrefundable
- Student must be enrolled at least half-time
- Phase-out: AGI $80,000-$90,000 (Single), $160,000-$180,000 (MFJ)

**Lifetime Learning Credit (LLC):**
- Up to **$2,000 per return** (not per student) — 20% of up to $10,000 in qualified expenses
- **Nonrefundable**
- No limit on number of years; available for undergraduate, graduate, and professional development courses
- No half-time enrollment requirement
- Phase-out: AGI $80,000-$90,000 (Single), $160,000-$180,000 (MFJ)

A taxpayer cannot claim both credits for the same student in the same year.

### AOTC vs. Lifetime Learning Credit Comparison

| Feature | AOTC | Lifetime Learning |
|---------|------|-------------------|
| Maximum credit | $2,500 per student | $2,000 per return |
| Refundable? | 40% ($1,000 max) | No |
| Years available | First 4 years of post-secondary | Unlimited |
| Enrollment | At least half-time | One or more courses |
| Felony drug conviction | Disqualifies | No restriction |
| Qualified expenses | Tuition, fees, course materials | Tuition and fees only |
| Per student or per return? | Per student | Per return |
| Graduate school? | No | Yes |

**Qualified expenses** for the AOTC include tuition, required enrollment fees, and course materials (books, supplies, equipment needed for a course). Room, board, insurance, transportation, and personal expenses do not qualify for either credit.

> Exam tip: The AOTC is per student — a family with three children in college could claim up to $7,500. The Lifetime Learning Credit is per return — no matter how many students, the maximum is $2,000. A felony drug conviction disqualifies a student from the AOTC but not the LLC.

### Other Important Credits

**Foreign Tax Credit (Section 901)** — Allows taxpayers to credit foreign income taxes paid against U.S. tax liability on foreign-source income, preventing double taxation. The credit is **nonrefundable** but can be carried back one year and forward ten years.

**Foreign tax credit limitation:**

FTC Limit = U.S. tax liability × (Foreign-source taxable income / Worldwide taxable income)

The limitation is computed separately for each category (or "basket") of income — **general category** and **passive category** are the two main baskets. If the taxpayer paid more foreign tax than the limit allows, the excess carries over.

**Worked Example:**

A taxpayer has worldwide taxable income of $200,000, of which $50,000 is foreign-source general category income. U.S. tax liability is $40,000. Foreign taxes paid: $12,000.

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| Foreign-source income | | $50,000 |
| Worldwide taxable income | | $200,000 |
| FTC limitation | $40,000 × ($50,000 / $200,000) | **$10,000** |
| Foreign taxes paid | | $12,000 |
| **Allowable credit** | Lesser of $12,000 paid or $10,000 limit | **$10,000** |
| Excess credit carryforward | $12,000 − $10,000 | **$2,000** |

Alternatively, taxpayers with $300 or less ($600 MFJ) in creditable foreign taxes from passive income only (e.g., foreign dividends) may claim the credit directly on Form 1040 without filing Form 1116.

> Exam tip: The foreign tax credit is an alternative to the foreign tax deduction (Schedule A). The credit is almost always more beneficial. A taxpayer cannot claim both a credit and a deduction for the same foreign taxes.

**Child and Dependent Care Credit** — Available for expenses paid for the care of a qualifying child under 13 or a disabled dependent to enable the taxpayer to work. The credit ranges from 20% to 35% of qualifying expenses (up to $3,000 for one qualifying individual, $6,000 for two or more). Nonrefundable. Both spouses must have earned income (or be full-time students or disabled) to claim the credit on a joint return.

**Retirement Savings Contributions Credit (Saver's Credit)** — A nonrefundable credit of up to $1,000 ($2,000 MFJ) for low-income taxpayers who contribute to a retirement plan. The credit rate is 50%, 20%, or 10% depending on AGI.

## Kiddie Tax

The **kiddie tax** prevents parents from shifting investment income to children in lower tax brackets. Under the kiddie tax rules, a child's **unearned income** above a threshold is taxed at the **parent's marginal tax rate** rather than the child's rate.

**Applies to:**
- Children under age 19, or full-time students under age 24
- Who have unearned income (interest, dividends, capital gains) exceeding approximately **$2,500** (indexed annually — first $1,250 is tax-free, next $1,250 taxed at child's rate)
- Who do not file a joint return

**How it works:** The child's unearned income above the threshold is added to the parent's return for purposes of determining the tax rate. The child's earned income (wages, self-employment) is always taxed at the child's own rate.

> Exam tip: The kiddie tax applies to unearned income only. A 16-year-old who earns $30,000 from a summer job pays tax at their own rate. But if that same child has $10,000 in dividend income, the amount above the threshold is taxed at the parent's rate.

## Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT)

The **Alternative Minimum Tax** is a parallel tax system designed to ensure that taxpayers with substantial income cannot eliminate their tax liability through deductions, credits, and exclusions.

### AMT Calculation

1. Start with **taxable income**
2. Add back **AMT preference items** and **adjustments**
3. Arrive at **Alternative Minimum Taxable Income (AMTI)**
4. Subtract the **AMT exemption** amount
5. Apply AMT rates: **26%** on the first $232,600 (2026 approximate), **28%** on amounts above
6. Compare to regular tax — the taxpayer pays the **higher** of regular tax or tentative minimum tax

### Common AMT Adjustments

Items added back to taxable income for AMT purposes:

- **State and local tax deduction (SALT)** — fully added back
- **Private activity bond interest** — tax-exempt interest from certain private activity municipal bonds becomes taxable
- **Incentive stock option (ISO) exercise** — the spread between exercise price and FMV at exercise is an AMT adjustment (though not regular income)
- **Standard deduction** — added back (if claimed instead of itemizing)
- **Depreciation differences** — AMT may require slower depreciation methods
- **Medical expenses** — the 7.5% AGI floor applies for both regular tax and AMT (previously differed)

### AMT Preference Items vs. Adjustments

**Preference items** are always added back (they only increase AMTI):
- Private activity bond interest
- Excess intangible drilling costs
- Percentage depletion in excess of adjusted basis

**Adjustments** can increase or decrease AMTI:
- SALT deduction (added back entirely)
- ISO exercise spread (added back in the year of exercise)
- Depreciation timing differences (may increase or decrease depending on method used)
- Standard deduction (added back if claimed)

> Exam tip: The most commonly tested AMT items are (1) state and local tax deduction, (2) ISO exercise spread, and (3) private activity bond interest. Under current law with the high exemption amounts, AMT primarily affects taxpayers who exercise large ISO grants or have significant private activity bond portfolios.

### AMT Exemption Amounts (approximate 2026)

| Filing Status | Exemption | Phase-out Begins |
|--------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Single | $88,000 | $609,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly | $137,000 | $1,218,000 |
| Married Filing Separately | $68,500 | $609,000 |

The exemption phases out at 25 cents per dollar of AMTI above the threshold.

> Exam tip: Under current law, the high AMT exemption amounts mean fewer taxpayers are affected by AMT. However, the concept and calculation method are still tested. The most common AMT triggers are large SALT deductions, ISO exercises, and private activity bond interest.

## Estimated Tax Payments and Penalties

Taxpayers who do not have sufficient withholding must make **estimated tax payments** quarterly on April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15 of the following year.

### Underpayment Penalty

A penalty applies if the taxpayer has not paid, through withholding and estimated payments, at least the lesser of:

**Safe harbor rules (to avoid the underpayment penalty):**
- **90%** of the current year's tax liability, or
- **100%** of the prior year's tax liability (110% if prior year AGI exceeds $150,000)

**Worked Example:**

A taxpayer had a $20,000 tax liability last year and expects to owe $30,000 this year. Prior year AGI was $120,000.

- 90% of current year: $30,000 x 90% = $27,000
- 100% of prior year: $20,000 x 100% = $20,000 (since AGI was under $150,000)
- **Safe harbor amount: $20,000** (the lesser of the two)

The taxpayer can avoid the penalty by paying at least $20,000 through withholding and estimated payments, even though the actual liability is $30,000.

### Annualized Income Installment Method

Taxpayers with uneven income throughout the year (seasonal businesses, large year-end bonuses, capital gains in a single quarter) can use the **annualized income installment method** to reduce or eliminate estimated tax penalties for earlier quarters when income was lower. This requires computing the tax based on income actually received through each quarterly period, annualized to a full year.

### Exceptions to the Penalty

The penalty does not apply if:
- The total tax owed after withholding and credits is less than **$1,000**
- The taxpayer had **no tax liability** in the prior year and was a U.S. citizen or resident for the entire year
- The underpayment was due to a casualty, disaster, or other unusual circumstance

### Estimated Tax Summary Table

| Rule | Threshold |
|------|-----------|
| Quarterly due dates | Apr 15, Jun 15, Sep 15, Jan 15 |
| Current year safe harbor | 90% of current year tax |
| Prior year safe harbor (AGI ≤ $150K) | 100% of prior year tax |
| Prior year safe harbor (AGI > $150K) | 110% of prior year tax |
| De minimis exception | Balance due < $1,000 |
| Zero prior year liability | No penalty if prior year tax was $0 |

> Exam tip: The 110% prior year safe harbor for high-income taxpayers (AGI > $150,000) is a favorite exam question. Many students forget the threshold jumps from 100% to 110% and choose the wrong answer.

## Key Terms

- **Refundable credit** — a tax credit that can reduce tax liability below zero, resulting in a refund to the taxpayer
- **Nonrefundable credit** — a tax credit that can reduce tax liability to zero but cannot generate a refund
- **Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT)** — a parallel tax calculation that adds back certain deductions and preferences to ensure a minimum level of tax
- **AMTI** — Alternative Minimum Taxable Income; the base on which the AMT is calculated
- **Safe harbor** — the minimum payment threshold through withholding and estimated payments that avoids the underpayment penalty
- **Qualified expenses** — education costs (tuition, fees, books for AOTC) that count toward education credit calculations
- **Kiddie tax** — rule taxing a child's unearned income above a threshold at the parent's marginal rate to prevent income shifting
- **Foreign tax credit limitation** — the maximum FTC allowed, calculated as U.S. tax multiplied by the ratio of foreign-source income to worldwide income
- **AMT preference items** — items always added back to taxable income for AMT (e.g., private activity bond interest); adjustments may increase or decrease AMTI
- **Annualized income installment method** — an alternative method for computing estimated tax payments when income is received unevenly throughout the year
