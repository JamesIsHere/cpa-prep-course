# Agency Relationships and Business Regulation

## Learning Objectives

- Identify how agency relationships are formed and the duties owed by agents and principals
- Distinguish between actual authority, apparent authority, and ratification
- Understand principal liability for agent actions in contract and tort
- Recognize the scope and key provisions of the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC)

## Agency Formation and Types

An **agency relationship** exists when one party (the **agent**) is authorized to act on behalf of another party (the **principal**) in dealings with third parties. Agency can be created by:

- **Express agreement** — oral or written authorization from the principal
- **Implied authority** — authority reasonably necessary to carry out express duties
- **Apparent authority** — the principal's conduct leads a third party to reasonably believe the agent has authority
- **Ratification** — the principal approves an unauthorized act after the fact, creating authority retroactively
- **Estoppel** — the principal is prevented from denying the agency where a third party has relied on the appearance of authority to their detriment

No consideration is required to create an agency relationship, distinguishing it from a contract. However, a **power of attorney** (a written agency authorization) must be in writing and may need to be notarized for certain transactions.

> Exam tip: Agency can be created without a written agreement or payment. The key requirement is that the principal consents to the agent acting on their behalf and the agent agrees.

## Duties in the Agency Relationship

### Agent's Duties to the Principal

- **Duty of loyalty** — the agent must act in the principal's best interest and avoid self-dealing or conflicts of interest
- **Duty of obedience** — the agent must follow the principal's reasonable and lawful instructions
- **Duty of care** — the agent must perform with the competence and diligence expected of a reasonable person in similar circumstances
- **Duty to account** — the agent must keep accurate records and not commingle the principal's funds with personal funds
- **Duty to notify** — the agent must inform the principal of all material facts related to the agency

### Principal's Duties to the Agent

- **Compensation** — the principal must pay the agent agreed-upon compensation (unless the agency is gratuitous)
- **Reimbursement** — the principal must reimburse the agent for authorized expenses incurred while performing duties
- **Indemnification** — the principal must indemnify the agent for losses suffered while acting within the scope of authority
- **Cooperation** — the principal must not interfere with the agent's proper performance

## Authority and Liability

### Types of Authority

Understanding authority types is critical because they determine when the principal is bound by the agent's actions:

**Actual authority** exists when the principal expressly or impliedly grants the agent the power to act. The principal is bound by acts within actual authority even if the third party is unaware of the agency.

**Apparent authority** arises from the principal's representations to third parties — not from what the principal tells the agent. If a reasonable third party believes the agent has authority based on the principal's conduct, the principal is bound.

**Worked Example:**

Parker (principal) tells Adams (agent), "You are no longer authorized to sign contracts on my behalf." However, Parker does not notify Taylor, a long-time supplier who has always dealt with Adams. Adams signs a purchase order with Taylor.

- Adams has **no actual authority** (it was revoked)
- Adams has **apparent authority** because Taylor reasonably believes Adams can still bind Parker
- Parker is bound by the contract with Taylor and must seek damages from Adams for acting without authority

### Principal's Liability

**Contract liability** depends on the disclosure status of the principal:

- **Disclosed principal** — third party knows the agent is acting for a specific principal. The principal is liable; the agent generally is not.
- **Partially disclosed principal** — third party knows an agency exists but not the principal's identity. Both the principal and agent may be liable.
- **Undisclosed principal** — third party has no knowledge of the agency. Both the principal and agent are liable; the third party can elect to hold either party responsible once the principal is discovered.

**Tort liability** — A principal is liable for the torts of an agent committed within the **scope of employment** under the doctrine of **respondeat superior**. Key factors include whether the act was authorized, occurred during work hours, was motivated by serving the principal, and whether the instrumentality was provided by the principal. Independent contractors generally do not create tort liability for the hiring party, with exceptions for inherently dangerous activities and non-delegable duties.

## Termination of Agency

An agency relationship terminates by:

**Acts of the parties:**
- Mutual agreement
- Revocation by the principal (effective immediately, but may create liability if it breaches a contract)
- Renunciation by the agent
- Fulfillment of the agency purpose
- Expiration of the stated term

**Operation of law:**
- Death of either party (terminates immediately without notice)
- Incapacity of either party
- Bankruptcy of the principal (and in some cases, the agent)
- Impossibility or illegality of the subject matter

The exception is an **agency coupled with an interest**, where the agent has a property interest or security interest in the subject matter. This type of agency is irrevocable by the principal and is not terminated by the principal's death or incapacity.

## Uniform Commercial Code Overview

The **Uniform Commercial Code (UCC)** is a set of standardized laws governing commercial transactions across states. For the CPA exam, the most important articles are:

### Article 2 — Sale of Goods

Article 2 applies to transactions involving the sale of **goods** (movable, tangible personal property). It does not apply to services, real estate, or intangibles.

Key differences from common law contracts:

- **Open terms** — a contract can be formed even if some terms (price, delivery, payment) are left open, as long as the parties intended to contract and there is a reasonably certain basis for remedy
- **Firm offers** — a written offer by a merchant to buy or sell goods, stating it will be held open, is irrevocable for up to three months without consideration
- **Battle of the forms (Section 2-207)** — acceptance with additional or different terms can still form a contract (unlike the common law mirror image rule)
- **Perfect tender rule** — the buyer may reject goods that fail to conform to the contract in any respect
- **Merchant rules** — heightened standards apply when one or both parties are merchants (dealers in goods of that kind)

### Article 3 — Negotiable Instruments

Covers checks, promissory notes, and drafts. A negotiable instrument must be in writing, signed, contain an unconditional promise or order to pay a fixed amount of money, be payable on demand or at a definite time, and be payable to order or bearer.

### Article 9 — Secured Transactions

Governs security interests in personal property. A creditor perfects a security interest (typically by filing a financing statement) to establish priority over other creditors. Perfection and priority rules are tested on the exam.

> Exam tip: Know the distinction between UCC Article 2 (goods) and common law (services, real estate). If the contract is for goods, UCC governs. If mixed, the **predominant purpose test** determines which body of law applies.

## Key Terms

- **Respondeat superior** — the doctrine holding a principal liable for the torts of an agent committed within the scope of employment
- **Apparent authority** — authority that exists when a principal's conduct leads a third party to reasonably believe the agent is authorized to act
- **Disclosed principal** — a principal whose identity is known to the third party at the time of the transaction
- **Firm offer** — a written, signed offer by a merchant to buy or sell goods, held open for a stated period up to three months without consideration
- **Perfect tender rule** — the buyer's right under UCC Article 2 to reject goods that fail to conform to the contract in any respect

## Practice Problems

**1.** Adams acts as an agent for Parker in purchasing supplies from Taylor. Taylor knows that Adams is acting as an agent but does not know Parker's identity. What type of principal is Parker?

a) Disclosed principal
b) Partially disclosed principal
c) Undisclosed principal
d) Gratuitous principal

**Answer:** b) Partially disclosed principal — Taylor knows an agency exists (Adams is acting for someone) but does not know the specific identity of the principal. This makes Parker a partially disclosed principal, and both Parker and Adams may be liable on the contract.

---

**2.** An agent's authority to do things reasonably necessary to carry out expressly authorized tasks is known as:

a) Apparent authority
b) Implied authority
c) Ratification
d) Estoppel

**Answer:** b) Implied authority — Implied authority is the power to perform acts that are incidental to or reasonably necessary to carry out the agent's express authority. Apparent authority arises from the principal's conduct toward third parties, not from the scope of the express grant.

---

**3.** Under UCC Article 2, a merchant's written offer to sell goods stating it will remain open for 60 days is:

a) Revocable at any time because no consideration was given
b) Irrevocable for 60 days as a firm offer
c) Irrevocable for a maximum of 30 days
d) Irrevocable only if the offeree pays separate consideration

**Answer:** b) Irrevocable for 60 days — A merchant's firm offer under UCC Section 2-205 is irrevocable for the stated period, up to a maximum of three months (90 days), without consideration. Since 60 days is within the three-month limit, the offer is irrevocable for the full 60 days.
