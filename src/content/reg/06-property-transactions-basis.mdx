# Property Transactions: Basis

## Learning Objectives

- Calculate the basis of property acquired by purchase, gift, inheritance, and conversion
- Apply adjustments to basis for improvements, depreciation, and casualty losses
- Determine the holding period for property under different acquisition methods
- Distinguish between the basis rules for different types of property transfers

## Cost Basis

The **basis** of property is the taxpayer's investment in the property for tax purposes. It is used to calculate gain or loss when the property is sold or otherwise disposed of.

For property acquired by **purchase**, the basis is the **cost basis** — the amount paid for the property, including:

- Cash paid
- Fair market value (FMV) of property or services exchanged
- Liabilities assumed by the buyer
- Settlement costs (for real estate: title insurance, legal fees, recording fees, surveys)
- Sales tax paid on the purchase

Items that are **not** included in basis:
- Financing costs (interest, loan origination fees) — these are generally deducted as expenses
- Property taxes (deductible as an itemized deduction)
- Insurance premiums

**Worked Example:**

Taylor purchases a rental property. The closing statement shows:

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Purchase price | $250,000 |
| Title insurance | $1,200 |
| Legal fees | $800 |
| Recording fees | $150 |
| Loan origination fee (1 point) | $2,500 |
| Property insurance (first year) | $1,400 |
| Real estate taxes (prorated) | $900 |

Taylor's cost basis: $250,000 + $1,200 + $800 + $150 = **$252,150**

The loan origination fee, insurance, and property taxes are not added to basis — they are treated as separate deductions.

## Gifted Property Basis

When property is received as a **gift**, the basis depends on whether the recipient sells the property at a gain or a loss.

### Gain Basis (Carryover Basis)

If the donee sells for **more than the donor's adjusted basis**, the donee's basis equals the **donor's adjusted basis** (carryover basis), potentially increased by any gift tax paid attributable to the appreciation.

### Loss Basis

If the donee sells for **less than the FMV at the date of gift**, the donee's basis equals the **FMV at the date of gift**.

### The "In-Between" Zone

If the donee sells for an amount **between** the donor's adjusted basis and the FMV at the date of gift (when FMV is lower than basis), there is **no gain or loss** recognized.

> Exam tip: The dual-basis rule for gifted property is one of the most tested basis concepts. Remember: use donor's basis for gains, use FMV at date of gift for losses, and if the selling price falls between the two, no gain or loss.

**Worked Example:**

A father gives stock to his daughter. The father's adjusted basis is $10,000 and the FMV on the date of gift is $7,000.

| Selling Price | Basis Used | Gain/Loss |
|--------------|-----------|-----------|
| $15,000 | $10,000 (donor's basis) | $5,000 gain |
| $5,000 | $7,000 (FMV at gift date) | ($2,000) loss |
| $8,500 | N/A | No gain or loss |

The $8,500 sale falls between the donor's basis ($10,000) and the FMV at the date of gift ($7,000), so no gain or loss is recognized.

### Gift Tax Adjustment

If the donor pays **gift tax** on the transfer, a portion of the gift tax is added to the donee's basis (for gain purposes only). The addition is limited to the gift tax attributable to the **net appreciation** in the property:

Gift tax addition = Gift tax paid x (FMV - Donor's basis) / (FMV - Annual exclusion)

## Inherited Property Basis

Property acquired from a **decedent** generally receives a **stepped-up basis** (or stepped-down basis) equal to the **fair market value on the date of death** (or the alternate valuation date, if elected).

- **Date of death** is the default valuation date
- **Alternate valuation date** (six months after death) may be elected if it reduces both the gross estate value and the estate tax liability

The stepped-up basis rule effectively eliminates the income tax on any appreciation that occurred during the decedent's lifetime. This is one of the most significant tax benefits in the Internal Revenue Code.

**Worked Example:**

Grandmother purchased stock for $5,000 in 1990. She dies in 2026 when the stock is worth $85,000. Her grandson inherits the stock.

- Grandson's basis: **$85,000** (FMV on date of death)
- If grandson sells for $90,000, gain is only $90,000 - $85,000 = **$5,000**
- The $80,000 of appreciation during grandmother's lifetime is never taxed

> Exam tip: Inherited property always gets a date-of-death FMV basis, regardless of the decedent's original cost. This differs from gifted property, which uses a carryover basis for gains. This distinction is heavily tested.

## Converted Property Basis

When property is converted from **personal use to business use** (e.g., a personal residence becomes a rental property), the basis for depreciation and loss purposes is the **lower of**:

- The adjusted basis at the time of conversion, or
- The FMV at the time of conversion

For determining **gain**, the basis is the **adjusted basis** at the time of conversion (the original cost basis, adjusted for any applicable items).

**Worked Example:**

A taxpayer purchased a home for $300,000 and converts it to a rental property when the FMV is $260,000.

- Basis for depreciation and loss: **$260,000** (lower of adjusted basis or FMV)
- Basis for gain: **$300,000** (adjusted basis at conversion)

## Adjusted Basis

After acquisition, basis is adjusted for certain events:

**Increases to basis:**
- Capital improvements (additions, renovations that extend useful life)
- Assessments for local improvements (sidewalks, roads)
- Legal fees to defend or perfect title
- Zoning costs

**Decreases to basis:**
- Depreciation or amortization allowed or allowable
- Casualty loss deductions taken
- Insurance reimbursements for losses
- Return of capital distributions
- Easements granted for consideration

The phrase **"allowed or allowable"** is critical for depreciation. Even if a taxpayer fails to claim depreciation deductions, the basis must still be reduced by the amount that could have been claimed. Basis can never be reduced below zero.

**Worked Example:**

A taxpayer purchases a rental building for $200,000 (excluding land). Over five years, the taxpayer claims $30,000 in depreciation and adds a $15,000 new roof.

Adjusted basis = $200,000 - $30,000 + $15,000 = **$185,000**

## Holding Period

The **holding period** determines whether a gain or loss is short-term or long-term:

- **Short-term:** held for one year or less (taxed at ordinary income rates)
- **Long-term:** held for more than one year (taxed at preferential capital gains rates of 0%, 15%, or 20%)

Holding period rules by acquisition method:

| Acquisition | Holding Period Start |
|------------|---------------------|
| Purchase | Day after acquisition |
| Gift (carryover basis used) | Includes donor's holding period ("tacks on") |
| Gift (FMV basis used for loss) | Starts on date of gift |
| Inheritance | Automatically long-term regardless of actual holding period |
| Like-kind exchange | Includes holding period of property given up |

> Exam tip: Inherited property is always treated as long-term, even if the decedent held it for one day. This rule eliminates the need to track the decedent's actual holding period.

## Key Terms

- **Adjusted basis** — the original basis of property increased by improvements and decreased by depreciation and other reductions
- **Carryover basis** — the basis rule for gifted property where the donee takes the donor's adjusted basis
- **Stepped-up basis** — the basis rule for inherited property where basis equals the FMV on the date of death
- **Holding period** — the length of time property is held, determining whether gain or loss is short-term or long-term
- **Allowed or allowable** — the depreciation standard requiring basis reduction for depreciation that was or could have been claimed

## Practice Problems

**1.** A taxpayer receives stock as a gift. The donor's adjusted basis is $20,000 and the FMV on the date of gift is $15,000. The taxpayer sells the stock for $17,000. What is the recognized gain or loss?

a) $2,000 loss
b) $3,000 gain
c) $2,000 gain
d) No gain or loss

**Answer:** d) No gain or loss — The selling price ($17,000) falls between the donor's adjusted basis ($20,000) and the FMV at the date of gift ($15,000). Under the dual-basis rule for gifts, no gain or loss is recognized when the selling price is in this range.

---

**2.** A taxpayer inherits land that the decedent purchased for $40,000. The FMV on the date of death is $120,000. The executor does not elect the alternate valuation date. What is the taxpayer's basis in the land?

a) $40,000
b) $80,000
c) $120,000
d) $160,000

**Answer:** c) $120,000 — Inherited property receives a stepped-up basis equal to the FMV on the date of death. The decedent's original cost of $40,000 is irrelevant for determining the heir's basis.

---

**3.** A taxpayer purchases equipment for $50,000 and claims $12,000 in depreciation over three years. The taxpayer then adds a $5,000 modification that extends the equipment's useful life. What is the adjusted basis?

a) $38,000
b) $43,000
c) $45,000
d) $55,000

**Answer:** b) $43,000 — Adjusted basis = $50,000 (cost) - $12,000 (depreciation) + $5,000 (capital improvement) = $43,000. Depreciation reduces basis while capital improvements increase it.
