# Partnerships

## Learning Objectives

- Understand partnership formation and the tax treatment of contributions under Section 721
- Calculate a partner's outside basis and the effect of partnership liabilities
- Apply the rules for guaranteed payments, special allocations, and distributive shares
- Identify the at-risk and passive activity loss limitations on partnership losses

## Partnership Formation

A **partnership** is an association of two or more persons carrying on a business as co-owners for profit. For tax purposes, a partnership is a **pass-through entity** — it files an informational return (Form 1065) but pays no entity-level tax. Each partner receives a **Schedule K-1** reporting their distributive share of income, deductions, gains, losses, and credits.

### Tax-Free Formation Under Section 721

Under **Section 721**, no gain or loss is recognized when a partner contributes property to a partnership in exchange for a partnership interest. Unlike Section 351 for corporations, there is **no control requirement** — any contribution of property for a partnership interest qualifies.

**Partner's basis in partnership interest (outside basis):** Equal to the adjusted basis of the property contributed, plus any gain recognized (if applicable), plus the partner's share of partnership liabilities.

**Partnership's basis in contributed property (inside basis):** Equal to the contributing partner's adjusted basis in the property (carryover basis).

**Worked Example:**

Adams contributes land (basis $40,000, FMV $100,000) for a 50% interest in AB Partnership. Baker contributes $100,000 cash for a 50% interest.

| Partner | Contribution Basis | Outside Basis | FMV |
|---------|-------------------|---------------|-----|
| Adams | $40,000 | $40,000 | $100,000 |
| Baker | $100,000 | $100,000 | $100,000 |

Partnership's inside basis in the land: $40,000 (carryover from Adams)
Partnership's inside basis in the cash: $100,000

### Exceptions to Nonrecognition

Gain may be recognized at formation if:

- A partner contributes **services** in exchange for a partnership interest — the FMV of the interest received is taxable as ordinary income
- A partner contributes property subject to a liability that exceeds their basis — the excess liability relief is treated as a deemed distribution, triggering gain
- **Disguised sales** (Section 707) — if a contribution and a related distribution are structured to function as a sale

> Exam tip: Contributing services for a partnership interest is always taxable. The partner recognizes ordinary income equal to the FMV of the interest received. This is different from contributing property, which is tax-free under Section 721.

## Partner's Basis (Outside Basis)

A partner's outside basis represents their tax investment in the partnership. It is essential for determining:

- Deductibility of partnership losses (losses limited to basis)
- Tax treatment of distributions
- Gain or loss on sale of the partnership interest

### Basis Adjustments

Outside basis is adjusted annually:

**Increases:**
- Distributive share of partnership income (ordinary and separately stated)
- Distributive share of tax-exempt income
- Additional capital contributions
- Increase in the partner's share of partnership liabilities

**Decreases:**
- Distributive share of partnership losses
- Distributions received from the partnership
- Distributive share of nondeductible, noncapital expenses
- Decrease in the partner's share of partnership liabilities

### Partnership Liabilities and Basis

A critical difference between partnerships and S corporations is that partners include their share of **partnership liabilities** in their outside basis. This can significantly increase the amount of losses a partner can deduct.

**Recourse liabilities** — liabilities for which a partner bears the economic risk of loss. Allocated to the partner(s) who would bear the loss if the partnership became worthless and could not pay the debt.

**Nonrecourse liabilities** — liabilities for which no partner bears personal risk (typically secured only by partnership property). Generally allocated among partners based on their **profit-sharing ratios**.

**Worked Example:**

Carter is a 40% partner. The partnership has $200,000 in recourse liabilities (Carter bears the economic risk for $80,000) and $300,000 in nonrecourse liabilities.

| Liability Type | Total | Carter's Share | Calculation |
|---------------|-------|---------------|-------------|
| Recourse | $200,000 | $80,000 | Economic risk of loss |
| Nonrecourse | $300,000 | $120,000 | 40% profit ratio |
| **Total liability share** | | **$200,000** | Added to outside basis |

## Guaranteed Payments

**Guaranteed payments** are payments to a partner for services or the use of capital that are determined without regard to partnership income. They function like a salary or interest payment.

**Tax treatment:**
- **To the partner:** Guaranteed payments are ordinary income, reported on the partner's K-1 and individual return. They are subject to self-employment tax.
- **To the partnership:** Guaranteed payments are deductible as an expense in computing partnership ordinary income, assuming they are for services or capital use related to the business.

**Worked Example:**

The DEF Partnership agreement provides that Partner D receives a guaranteed payment of $60,000 for services. After deducting the guaranteed payment, the partnership has ordinary income of $90,000. D has a 1/3 interest.

| Item | Partner D |
|------|----------|
| Guaranteed payment | $60,000 |
| Share of ordinary income (1/3 x $90,000) | $30,000 |
| **Total income from partnership** | **$90,000** |

The guaranteed payment is deducted in computing the partnership's ordinary income and then separately reported to the partner who earned it.

> Exam tip: Guaranteed payments are always ordinary income to the receiving partner and are always deductible by the partnership (if they are ordinary and necessary business expenses). They are included in the partner's self-employment income.

## Special Allocations

A partnership agreement may allocate specific items of income, gain, loss, or deduction differently from the general profit-and-loss sharing ratios. These are called **special allocations**. For example, a partnership might allocate all depreciation deductions to one partner while splitting operating income equally.

### Substantial Economic Effect (Section 704(b))

For a special allocation to be respected by the IRS, it must have **substantial economic effect**. This has two components:

**1. Economic effect** — the allocation must be consistent with the **economic arrangement** of the partners. The three requirements are:
- Capital accounts are maintained in accordance with Treasury Regulations
- Liquidating distributions are made in accordance with positive capital account balances
- Partners with deficit capital accounts must restore the deficit upon liquidation (**deficit restoration obligation**) or comply with the **qualified income offset** alternative

**2. Substantiality** — there must be a reasonable possibility that the allocation will **substantially affect** the dollar amounts received by the partners, independent of tax consequences. An allocation fails substantiality if:
- It is a **shifting allocation** — allocating one item to Partner A and an offsetting item to Partner B with the same net effect on capital accounts
- It is a **transitory allocation** — allocating an item one way in one year and reversing it in a later year

If a special allocation lacks substantial economic effect, the IRS will reallocate the item according to the partners' **interests in the partnership** — essentially, their economic arrangement determined under all facts and circumstances.

> Exam tip: The exam frequently tests whether a special allocation will be respected. The key concept is that the allocation must affect the partners economically (through capital accounts), not just shift tax benefits. An allocation of all depreciation to a high-bracket partner with no economic effect will be disregarded.

## Contributed Property Rules (Section 704(c))

When a partner contributes property with a built-in gain or loss (FMV differs from basis at contribution), **Section 704(c)** requires that the built-in gain or loss be allocated to the **contributing partner** when the partnership later sells or depreciates that property.

**Purpose:** Prevent the non-contributing partners from being taxed on gain that accrued before they joined the partnership.

**Worked Example:**

Adams contributes land with a basis of $40,000 and FMV of $100,000 to AB Partnership for a 50% interest. Later, the partnership sells the land for $120,000.

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Total gain on sale | $120,000 − $40,000 = $80,000 |
| Built-in gain (Section 704(c)) | $100,000 − $40,000 = $60,000 → allocated entirely to Adams |
| Post-contribution gain | $120,000 − $100,000 = $20,000 → split 50/50 |
| **Adams's gain** | $60,000 + $10,000 = **$70,000** |
| **Baker's gain** | **$10,000** |

Without Section 704(c), Baker would bear tax on $40,000 of gain ($80,000 × 50%) that accrued before Baker joined the partnership.

## Distributions

### Current (Nonliquidating) Distributions

General rule: A partner recognizes **no gain or loss** on a current distribution unless:

- **Cash distributed exceeds the partner's outside basis** — gain is recognized to the extent of the excess (characterized as capital gain from the sale of a partnership interest)
- Property distributions generally do not trigger gain

**Basis of property received in a current distribution:** The partner takes the lesser of the partnership's inside basis or the partner's remaining outside basis (after reducing for any cash distributed).

**Worked Example:**

A partner with an outside basis of $50,000 receives a current distribution of $20,000 cash and property with an inside basis of $40,000 (FMV $60,000).

| Step | Calculation | Basis |
|------|------------|-------|
| Beginning outside basis | | $50,000 |
| Cash distribution | ($20,000) | $30,000 |
| Basis of property received | Lesser of $40,000 inside basis or $30,000 remaining basis | **$30,000** |
| Remaining outside basis | | **$0** |

The partner's basis in the property is $30,000 (limited to remaining outside basis), not $40,000 or $60,000.

### Liquidating Distributions

In a liquidating distribution, the partner's entire interest is terminated. Basis of property received equals the partner's **remaining outside basis** (after cash distributions), regardless of the partnership's inside basis. This may result in a basis step-up or step-down.

Loss is recognized only in a liquidating distribution consisting solely of cash, unrealized receivables, and inventory — and only if the distribution is less than the partner's basis.

## Hot Assets (Section 751)

When a partner sells their partnership interest or receives a distribution that includes certain assets, **Section 751** overrides the general capital gain treatment and requires **ordinary income** recognition on the portion attributable to **hot assets**.

### What Are Hot Assets?

| Hot Asset Type | Description |
|---------------|-------------|
| **Unrealized receivables** | Accounts receivable for services rendered or goods delivered (not yet collected), plus recapture income (Section 1245/1250 depreciation recapture built into partnership assets) |
| **Inventory items** | Inventory held for sale to customers, plus any assets that would produce ordinary income if sold by the partnership (including property held less than the long-term capital gains holding period in certain contexts) |

### Sale of a Partnership Interest

When a partner sells their partnership interest, the gain is generally capital gain. However, Section 751 requires the selling partner to recognize **ordinary income** on the portion of the gain attributable to their share of hot assets.

**Worked Example:**

Partner C sells a 1/3 partnership interest (outside basis $100,000) for $150,000. The partnership's assets include:

| Asset | Partnership Basis | FMV | C's 1/3 Share of FMV |
|-------|------------------|-----|----------------------|
| Cash | $60,000 | $60,000 | $20,000 |
| Equipment (1245 property) | $90,000 | $150,000 | $50,000 |
| Goodwill | $0 | $150,000 | $50,000 |
| Accounts receivable | $0 | $90,000 | $30,000 |

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| Total gain | $150,000 − $100,000 | $50,000 |
| Hot asset gain — A/R (1/3 share) | $30,000 − $0 | **$30,000 ordinary** |
| Hot asset gain — 1245 recapture (1/3 share) | $20,000 (C's share of recapture) | **$20,000 ordinary** |
| **Remaining gain** | $50,000 − $30,000 − $20,000 | **$0 capital gain** |

### Distributions Involving Hot Assets

If a partner receives a distribution of hot assets disproportionate to their share, Section 751(b) treats the transaction as if the partner sold their share of hot assets back to the partnership in exchange for the non-hot assets received (or vice versa). This ensures ordinary income treatment is not converted to capital gain through distributions.

> Exam tip: Section 751 converts what would otherwise be capital gain into ordinary income when hot assets are involved. The two categories to remember are unrealized receivables (including recapture) and inventory. This applies to both sales of partnership interests and disproportionate distributions.

## Section 754 Elections

A **Section 754 election** allows the partnership to adjust the basis of its assets when a partner purchases an interest or receives a distribution. Without this election, the partnership's inside basis in its assets remains unchanged even when a new partner pays a premium for their interest.

### Section 743(b) — Transfer of Partnership Interest

When a partnership interest is sold or exchanged and a Section 754 election is in effect, the partnership adjusts the inside basis of its assets **with respect to the transferee partner only**.

**Adjustment = Transferee's outside basis − Transferee's share of partnership's inside basis**

**Worked Example:**

New Partner D purchases a 25% interest in ABCD Partnership for $250,000. The partnership's total inside basis in assets is $800,000 (D's 25% share = $200,000).

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| D's outside basis (purchase price) | | $250,000 |
| D's share of inside basis | $800,000 × 25% | $200,000 |
| **743(b) adjustment** | $250,000 − $200,000 | **$50,000 step-up** |

The $50,000 step-up is allocated among the partnership's assets and affects only D's share of depreciation, gain, or loss when those assets are sold or depreciated. The other partners are not affected.

### Section 734(b) — Distributions

When a Section 754 election is in effect and a distribution results in a gain recognized by the distributee partner or a basis adjustment in the distributed property, the partnership adjusts the basis of its **remaining** assets.

### Mandatory Adjustments

Even without a Section 754 election, a **mandatory basis adjustment** is required under Section 743(b) when the transferee would receive a basis step-down of more than **$250,000** (a substantial built-in loss). This prevents loss duplication.

> Exam tip: Section 754 is a partnership-level election that, once made, applies to all future transfers and distributions until revoked. The key concept is that it eliminates the mismatch between a new partner's outside basis and their share of inside basis. Questions typically test whether the election results in a step-up or step-down and how the adjustment is allocated.

## Loss Limitations

Partnership losses pass through to partners but are subject to three layers of limitations, applied in order:

### 1. Basis Limitation

A partner cannot deduct losses in excess of their **outside basis** (including their share of liabilities). Excess losses are **suspended** and carried forward indefinitely until basis is restored.

### 2. At-Risk Limitation (Section 465)

After passing the basis test, losses are further limited to the amount the partner has **at risk**. At-risk amount includes:

- Cash and adjusted basis of property contributed
- Recourse liabilities for which the partner is personally liable
- **Qualified nonrecourse financing** (certain real estate debt secured by the property)

Nonrecourse liabilities (other than qualified nonrecourse financing) are **not** included in the at-risk amount. Losses exceeding the at-risk amount are suspended.

### 3. Passive Activity Loss Limitation (Section 469)

After passing the basis and at-risk tests, losses from **passive activities** can only offset income from other passive activities. Passive activities include:

- Trade or business activities in which the taxpayer does not **materially participate**
- Rental activities (generally passive regardless of participation, with a $25,000 exception for active participation in rental real estate, phased out between $100,000-$150,000 AGI)

**Material participation** requires meeting one of seven tests, the most common being: participating more than **500 hours** during the year.

Suspended passive losses are released when the activity is disposed of in a fully taxable transaction.

**Worked Example: Three-Layer Loss Application**

A partner has:
- Outside basis: $60,000
- At-risk amount: $45,000
- The partner does not materially participate
- Distributive share of loss: $70,000

| Limitation | Allowed | Suspended |
|-----------|---------|-----------|
| Basis limitation ($60,000) | $60,000 | $10,000 |
| At-risk limitation ($45,000) | $45,000 | $15,000 |
| Passive activity limitation | $0 (passive loss, no passive income) | $45,000 |
| **Deductible loss** | **$0** | |

All three limitations are applied sequentially. Even though basis allows $60,000 and at-risk allows $45,000, the passive activity rules prevent any deduction because the partner has no passive income to offset.

## Key Terms

- **Outside basis** — a partner's tax basis in their partnership interest, used to determine loss limitations and distribution treatment
- **Inside basis** — the partnership's tax basis in its assets
- **Guaranteed payment** — compensation to a partner for services or use of capital, determined without regard to partnership income
- **At-risk amount** — the maximum loss a taxpayer can deduct under Section 465, generally limited to amounts the taxpayer could actually lose
- **Material participation** — active involvement in a business activity that removes it from passive activity classification
- **Distributive share** — each partner's allocated portion of partnership income, deductions, gains, losses, and credits
- **Special allocation** — an allocation of a specific income or deduction item that differs from the general profit-and-loss sharing ratio; must have substantial economic effect to be respected
- **Substantial economic effect** — the standard a special allocation must meet under Section 704(b): the allocation must affect partner capital accounts (economic effect) and change the amounts partners receive (substantiality)
- **Section 704(c)** — requires built-in gains or losses on contributed property to be allocated to the contributing partner, preventing tax shifting to other partners
- **Hot assets** — unrealized receivables and inventory items that trigger ordinary income (rather than capital gain) under Section 751 when a partnership interest is sold or disproportionate distributions are made
- **Section 754 election** — a partnership-level election allowing basis adjustments to partnership assets when interests are transferred (743(b)) or distributions are made (734(b))
- **Inside/outside basis disparity** — the mismatch between a partner's basis in their partnership interest (outside) and their proportionate share of the partnership's basis in its assets (inside); Section 754 corrects this
