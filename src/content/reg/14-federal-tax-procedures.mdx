# Federal Tax Procedures

## Learning Objectives

- Understand the IRS audit process including correspondence, office, and field audits
- Apply the statute of limitations rules for assessment (3-year, 6-year, and unlimited)
- Distinguish between the assessment and collection procedures available to the IRS
- Navigate the IRS appeals process and identify when to use alternative dispute resolution
- Compare Tax Court, District Court, and Court of Federal Claims for tax litigation
- Calculate accuracy-related penalties, fraud penalties, and failure-to-file/pay penalties

## IRS Audit Process

The IRS uses several types of examinations to verify the accuracy of filed tax returns. The type of audit selected depends on the nature and complexity of the issue identified.

### Correspondence Audit

A **correspondence audit** is the simplest form of examination. The IRS sends a letter requesting documentation to support a specific item on the return (such as a charitable contribution or education credit). The taxpayer responds by mail with the requested records. No in-person meeting occurs.

### Office Audit

An **office audit** is conducted at an IRS office. The taxpayer (or their representative) meets with an IRS examiner to discuss specific line items on the return. These audits typically involve issues such as income verification, deduction substantiation, or eligibility for credits.

### Field Audit

A **field audit** is the most comprehensive examination. An IRS revenue agent visits the taxpayer's home, place of business, or accountant's office to review books and records. Field audits are reserved for complex returns, including those involving business income, multi-entity structures, or large deductions relative to income.

**Worked Example:**

A sole proprietor reports $250,000 in gross receipts and $180,000 in business deductions on Schedule C. The IRS selects the return for a field audit. The revenue agent visits the taxpayer's office and requests:

| Document Requested | Purpose |
|-------------------|---------|
| Bank statements | Verify gross receipts |
| Invoices and receipts | Substantiate deductions |
| Mileage logs | Support vehicle expense deduction |
| Depreciation schedules | Verify asset basis and recovery periods |

The agent compares reported income against bank deposits and third-party information returns (1099s). Any discrepancies are noted and discussed with the taxpayer.

> Exam tip: The CPA exam frequently tests the distinction between the three audit types. Remember that correspondence audits are the least intrusive (mail only), office audits occur at the IRS, and field audits occur at the taxpayer's location. Field audits involve the broadest scope of examination.

## Statute of Limitations

The **statute of limitations** determines how long the IRS has to assess additional tax, and how long the taxpayer has to claim a refund. Mastering the time periods and exceptions is critical for the CPA exam.

### General Rule: 3 Years

The IRS generally has **3 years** from the later of (1) the date the return was filed or (2) the due date of the return (including extensions) to assess additional tax.

If a return is filed early (before the due date), the statute begins running on the due date, not the filing date.

### Extended Period: 6 Years

The statute extends to **6 years** if the taxpayer omits more than **25% of gross income** from the return. For this purpose:

- For a trade or business, gross income means **gross receipts** before deducting cost of goods sold
- Adequate disclosure of an item on the return prevents the 6-year statute from applying to that item

### Unlimited Period

The statute **never expires** in two situations:

- **Fraud** — if the taxpayer files a fraudulent return with the intent to evade tax, there is no time limit on assessment
- **No return filed** — if the taxpayer fails to file a return, the IRS can assess tax at any time

### Taxpayer Refund Claims

A taxpayer must file a refund claim within the **later of**:

- **3 years** from the date the return was filed, OR
- **2 years** from the date the tax was paid

The refund is limited to tax paid within the 3 years (plus any extension period) before the claim was filed. If the claim is filed under the 2-year rule, the refund is limited to tax paid within 2 years before the claim.

**Worked Example:**

| Scenario | Return Filed | Statute Expires |
|----------|-------------|-----------------|
| Return filed on time (April 15, 2025) | April 15, 2025 | April 15, 2028 |
| Return filed early (Feb 1, 2025; due April 15, 2025) | Feb 1, 2025 | April 15, 2028 (runs from due date) |
| Return filed late (Aug 10, 2025; no extension) | Aug 10, 2025 | Aug 10, 2028 |
| 25% gross income omission | April 15, 2025 | April 15, 2031 (6 years) |
| Fraudulent return filed | April 15, 2025 | Never expires |
| No return filed | N/A | Never expires |

> Exam tip: The 25% omission test is commonly tested. Remember it applies to gross income (gross receipts for businesses), not taxable income. If the taxpayer adequately disclosed the omitted item on the return, the 6-year statute does not apply even if the amount exceeds 25%.

## Assessment and Collection Procedures

### Assessment Process

After an audit concludes, the IRS follows a structured process:

1. **30-day letter** — the IRS sends a preliminary notice of proposed changes. The taxpayer has 30 days to agree, provide additional documentation, or request an Appeals conference.
2. **Statutory notice of deficiency (90-day letter)** — if the taxpayer disagrees, the IRS sends a formal notice of deficiency. The taxpayer has **90 days** (150 days if outside the U.S.) to file a petition with the Tax Court. This is a prerequisite to Tax Court jurisdiction.
3. **Assessment** — if the taxpayer does not petition the Tax Court within 90 days, the IRS formally assesses the tax and begins collection.

### Collection Powers

Once tax is assessed, the IRS has extensive collection powers:

- **Tax lien** — a legal claim against all of the taxpayer's property (real and personal) arising automatically upon assessment and demand for payment. A **federal tax lien** attaches to all property and rights to property. The IRS files a **Notice of Federal Tax Lien (NFTL)** to provide public notice and establish priority against other creditors.
- **Tax levy** — the actual seizure of property to satisfy a tax debt. Before levying, the IRS must provide a **Notice of Intent to Levy** at least 30 days in advance. The IRS can levy wages, bank accounts, accounts receivable, and other property.
- **Collection statute** — the IRS generally has **10 years** from the date of assessment to collect the tax.

> Exam tip: Do not confuse liens and levies. A lien is a legal claim (encumbrance) on property; a levy is the actual seizure of property. The lien arises automatically upon assessment and demand; the levy requires an additional 30-day notice.

## Appeals Process

Taxpayers who disagree with IRS findings can pursue administrative and judicial remedies.

### Administrative Appeals (IRS Appeals Office)

The IRS Independent Office of Appeals (formerly IRS Appeals) provides an impartial forum for resolving tax disputes without litigation. The Appeals officer can settle cases based on the **hazards of litigation** — the probability of the IRS prevailing in court.

A taxpayer may request Appeals consideration after receiving a 30-day letter by filing a **written protest** (for amounts exceeding $25,000) or a **small case request** (for amounts of $25,000 or less).

### Judicial Forums

If the dispute is not resolved through Appeals, the taxpayer can litigate in one of three courts:

| Feature | Tax Court | U.S. District Court | Court of Federal Claims |
|---------|-----------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| **Prepayment** | No (file without paying) | Yes (pay first, sue for refund) | Yes (pay first, sue for refund) |
| **Jury trial** | No | Yes (only court offering jury trials) | No |
| **Location** | National (judges travel to taxpayer's city) | Taxpayer's local district | Washington, D.C. only |
| **Appeals to** | U.S. Court of Appeals for taxpayer's circuit | U.S. Court of Appeals for taxpayer's circuit | U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit |
| **Expertise** | Specialized tax judges | General jurisdiction | Broad government claims |
| **Small case option** | Yes ($50,000 or less — no appeal) | No | No |

**Key decision factors:**

- Choose **Tax Court** if you want to litigate without paying the tax first
- Choose **District Court** if you want a jury trial
- Choose **Court of Federal Claims** if favorable precedent exists in the Federal Circuit

**Worked Example:**

A taxpayer receives a statutory notice of deficiency for $75,000. The taxpayer does not have the funds to pay the assessed amount before challenging it.

**Best option:** File a petition with the **Tax Court** within 90 days. The Tax Court is the only forum that does not require prepayment of the disputed tax.

> Exam tip: The three most commonly tested distinctions are: (1) Tax Court does not require prepayment, (2) District Court is the only court offering a jury trial, and (3) Tax Court and District Court appeals go to the geographic circuit court, while Court of Federal Claims appeals go to the Federal Circuit.

## Penalties

The IRS imposes various civil penalties for noncompliance. These penalties are in addition to the tax itself and any accrued interest.

### Accuracy-Related Penalty (Section 6662)

The accuracy-related penalty is **20% of the underpayment** attributable to:

- **Negligence** — failure to make a reasonable attempt to comply with the tax law or failure to exercise ordinary and reasonable care
- **Disregard of rules or regulations** — careless, reckless, or intentional disregard
- **Substantial understatement of income tax** — the understatement exceeds the greater of **10% of the correct tax** or **$5,000** ($10,000 for corporations)
- **Substantial valuation misstatement** — the claimed value or adjusted basis is 150% or more of the correct amount
- **Gross valuation misstatement** — the claimed value is 200% or more of the correct amount (penalty increases to **40%**)

**Defense:** The penalty does not apply if the taxpayer had **reasonable cause** and acted in **good faith**.

### Civil Fraud Penalty (Section 6663)

The civil fraud penalty is **75% of the underpayment** attributable to fraud. The IRS bears the burden of proving fraud by **clear and convincing evidence**. If the IRS proves any part of the underpayment is due to fraud, the **entire underpayment** is treated as fraudulent unless the taxpayer proves otherwise for specific portions.

The accuracy-related penalty (6662) and the fraud penalty (6663) **cannot both apply** to the same portion of an underpayment.

### Failure to File and Failure to Pay (Section 6651)

| Penalty | Rate | Maximum |
|---------|------|---------|
| Failure to file (FTF) | 5% of unpaid tax per month (or fraction) | 25% |
| Failure to pay (FTP) | 0.5% of unpaid tax per month (or fraction) | 25% |
| Both apply simultaneously | FTF reduced by FTP amount — net 4.5% FTF + 0.5% FTP = 5% per month for first 5 months | Combined 47.5% |
| Fraudulent failure to file | 15% per month | 75% |

**Worked Example:**

A taxpayer files 4 months late with $10,000 of unpaid tax:

| Penalty | Calculation | Amount |
|---------|------------|--------|
| Failure to file | 5% x $10,000 x 4 months = $2,000, reduced by FTP overlap | $1,800 |
| Failure to pay | 0.5% x $10,000 x 4 months | $200 |
| **Total penalties** | | **$2,000** |

For the months both penalties run concurrently, the FTF penalty is reduced by the FTP penalty, so the total rate is 5% per month (not 5.5%).

### Estimated Tax Penalty

Individuals must pay estimated taxes if they expect to owe $1,000 or more in tax after withholding and credits. Safe harbor: pay the lesser of **90% of current year tax** or **100% of prior year tax** (110% if prior year AGI exceeds $150,000).

> Exam tip: The most commonly tested penalty is failure to file vs. failure to pay. Remember that FTF (5% per month) is ten times the FTP rate (0.5% per month). The exam often tests the interaction rule: when both apply, the total is 5% per month for the overlap period, not 5.5%.

## Interest on Underpayments

Interest on tax underpayments accrues from the **due date** of the return (without regard to extensions) to the date the tax is paid. Key rules:

- **Rate:** The federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points, adjusted quarterly. For large corporate underpayments (exceeding $100,000), the rate is the short-term rate plus 5 percentage points.
- **Compounding:** Interest is compounded daily.
- **No waiver:** Unlike penalties, interest generally **cannot be abated** for reasonable cause. The IRS has limited authority to abate interest only if it was caused by unreasonable IRS delays (ministerial or managerial acts).
- **Netting:** When the taxpayer has both an overpayment and an underpayment for the same period, the IRS nets them to reduce interest charges.

> Exam tip: A critical distinction is that penalties may be waived for reasonable cause, but interest generally cannot be waived. Interest begins running from the original due date, not the extended due date.

## Key Terms

- **Correspondence audit** — the simplest IRS examination conducted entirely through mail, addressing specific return items
- **Office audit** — an IRS examination conducted at the IRS office, typically covering limited line items
- **Field audit** — the most comprehensive IRS examination, conducted at the taxpayer's location with a broad review of books and records
- **Statute of limitations (assessment)** — the time period during which the IRS may assess additional tax, generally 3 years from filing
- **Statutory notice of deficiency (90-day letter)** — a formal IRS notice required before assessment, giving the taxpayer 90 days to petition the Tax Court
- **Tax lien** — a legal claim against a taxpayer's property arising upon assessment and demand for payment
- **Tax levy** — the seizure of a taxpayer's property to satisfy a tax debt, requiring 30 days advance notice
- **Accuracy-related penalty (Section 6662)** — a 20% penalty on underpayments due to negligence, disregard of rules, or substantial understatement
- **Civil fraud penalty (Section 6663)** — a 75% penalty on underpayments attributable to fraud, with the IRS bearing the burden of proof
- **Hazards of litigation** — the standard used by the IRS Appeals Office to evaluate the likelihood of prevailing in court, which forms the basis for settlement offers
- **Prepayment requirement** — the requirement that a taxpayer pay the disputed tax before filing suit; applies to District Court and Court of Federal Claims but not Tax Court

## Practice Problems

**1.** A taxpayer filed a 2024 tax return on March 1, 2025. The return was due April 15, 2025. When does the statute of limitations for assessment expire?

a) March 1, 2028
b) April 15, 2028
c) March 1, 2029
d) April 15, 2029

**Answer:** b) April 15, 2028 — When a return is filed before the due date, the statute of limitations begins on the due date (April 15, 2025), not the actual filing date. The 3-year statute expires on April 15, 2028.

---

**2.** A taxpayer omitted $60,000 of gross income from a return reporting $200,000 in total gross income. What is the applicable statute of limitations?

a) 3 years
b) 5 years
c) 6 years
d) Unlimited

**Answer:** c) 6 years — The omission ($60,000) exceeds 25% of reported gross income ($200,000 x 25% = $50,000). When more than 25% of gross income is omitted, the statute extends to 6 years.

---

**3.** A taxpayer wants to challenge a $120,000 deficiency in court without paying the tax first. Which court should the taxpayer choose?

a) U.S. District Court
b) U.S. Tax Court
c) Court of Federal Claims
d) U.S. Court of Appeals

**Answer:** b) U.S. Tax Court — The Tax Court is the only judicial forum where a taxpayer can challenge a deficiency without first paying the tax. District Court and Court of Federal Claims require prepayment and a refund suit.

---

**4.** A taxpayer files a return 3 months late (no fraud) with $20,000 of unpaid tax. What is the total failure-to-file and failure-to-pay penalty?

a) $2,300
b) $3,000
c) $3,300
d) $1,000

**Answer:** b) $3,000 — The total penalty rate when both FTF and FTP apply is 5% per month: FTF is 5% reduced to 4.5% + FTP of 0.5% = 5% per month. $20,000 x 5% x 3 months = $3,000.

---

**5.** Which of the following is true regarding interest on tax underpayments?

a) Interest can be abated if the taxpayer demonstrates reasonable cause
b) Interest accrues from the extended due date of the return
c) Interest is compounded daily and generally cannot be abated
d) Interest stops accruing once the taxpayer enters into an installment agreement

**Answer:** c) Interest is compounded daily and generally cannot be abated — Unlike penalties, interest cannot be waived for reasonable cause. It accrues from the original due date (not the extended due date) and continues to accrue even during installment agreements.
