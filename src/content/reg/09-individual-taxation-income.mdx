# Individual Taxation: Income and Deductions

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the components of gross income and common exclusions
- Determine filing status and its impact on standard deductions and tax brackets
- Distinguish between above-the-line and below-the-line deductions
- Calculate adjusted gross income (AGI) and taxable income

## Gross Income

**Gross income** under Section 61 includes all income from whatever source derived, unless specifically excluded by another Code section. The definition is intentionally broad — if economic value is received, it is generally taxable.

### Common Inclusions

- Wages, salaries, tips, and bonuses
- Interest income (taxable and tax-exempt reported separately)
- Dividend income (ordinary and qualified)
- Business income (Schedule C)
- Capital gains
- Rental and royalty income
- Alimony received (for agreements executed before 2019)
- Retirement distributions (pensions, IRAs, 401(k) plans)
- Unemployment compensation
- Social Security benefits (up to 85% may be taxable — see taxability formula below)
- Gambling winnings
- Cancellation of debt income (with exceptions)
- Bartering income (FMV of goods or services received)
- **Schedule K-1 income** — a taxpayer's distributive share of income from partnerships (Form 1065 K-1), S corporations (Form 1120-S K-1), and trusts/estates (Form 1041 K-1)

### Social Security Benefit Taxability

The taxable portion of Social Security benefits depends on **provisional income** (also called combined income):

**Provisional income** = AGI (excluding Social Security) + tax-exempt interest + 50% of Social Security benefits

| Filing Status | Provisional Income | Taxable Portion |
|---------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Single        | Below $25,000     | 0%              |
| Single        | $25,000–$34,000   | Up to 50%       |
| Single        | Above $34,000     | Up to 85%       |
| MFJ           | Below $32,000     | 0%              |
| MFJ           | $32,000–$44,000   | Up to 50%       |
| MFJ           | Above $44,000     | Up to 85%       |

**Worked Example:**

A single retiree receives $20,000 in Social Security benefits, has $18,000 in pension income and $4,000 in tax-exempt bond interest.

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| AGI (excluding SS) | Pension income | $18,000 |
| Tax-exempt interest | | $4,000 |
| 50% of Social Security | $20,000 x 50% | $10,000 |
| **Provisional income** | $18,000 + $4,000 + $10,000 | **$32,000** |

Provisional income of $32,000 exceeds $34,000? No — it falls in the $25,000–$34,000 range, so up to **50%** of benefits ($10,000) may be taxable.

> Exam tip: Tax-exempt interest is added back for the Social Security taxability calculation even though it is otherwise excluded from gross income. This catches students who assume tax-exempt means it has no tax consequences anywhere.

### K-1 Income on the Individual Return

Income from pass-through entities is reported to the individual on **Schedule K-1** and flows to various lines of Form 1040:

| K-1 Item | Where It Goes on Form 1040 |
|----------|---------------------------|
| Ordinary business income/loss | Schedule E, Part II |
| Rental income/loss | Schedule E, Part II |
| Interest income | Schedule B |
| Dividends | Schedule B |
| Short-term capital gains/losses | Schedule D |
| Long-term capital gains/losses | Schedule D |
| Section 1231 gains/losses | Form 4797 |
| Guaranteed payments | Schedule E (also Schedule SE for self-employment tax) |
| Charitable contributions | Schedule A |
| Section 179 deduction | Form 4562 |
| Foreign taxes paid | Form 1116 or Schedule 3 |

K-1 income retains its character when reported by the individual — capital gains remain capital gains, ordinary income remains ordinary. The individual taxpayer must also apply the **three-tier loss limitation** rules (basis, at-risk, passive activity) to any losses from pass-through entities before deducting them.

### Common Exclusions

Not everything received is taxable. Key exclusions from gross income include:

- **Gifts and inheritances** — excluded under Section 102 (though the income earned on gifted property is taxable)
- **Life insurance proceeds** — death benefits received by a beneficiary (with exceptions for transfers for value)
- **Municipal bond interest** — interest on state and local government bonds
- **Qualified scholarships** — amounts used for tuition and required fees (room and board are taxable). Amounts received for teaching or research services are generally taxable as compensation, unless the services are required of all degree candidates
- **Employer-provided health insurance** — premiums paid by the employer
- **Workers' compensation** — benefits received for job-related injuries
- **Child support** — payments received for child support
- **Return of capital** — distributions that represent a return of the taxpayer's investment
- **Gain on sale of personal residence** — up to $250,000 ($500,000 for married filing jointly) under Section 121 if ownership and use tests are met

> Exam tip: Alimony treatment changed under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act. For divorce agreements executed after December 31, 2018, alimony is not deductible by the payer and not includable by the recipient. For pre-2019 agreements, the old rules still apply unless modified.

## Filing Status

Filing status determines the tax brackets, standard deduction amount, and eligibility for certain credits and deductions. The five filing statuses are:

**1. Single** — unmarried, legally separated, or divorced on the last day of the tax year

**2. Married Filing Jointly (MFJ)** — married couples who combine income and deductions on one return. Both spouses are jointly and severally liable for the tax.

**3. Married Filing Separately (MFS)** — married couples who file separate returns. This status generally results in higher tax but may be preferred to avoid joint liability or when one spouse has significant medical expenses or miscellaneous deductions.

**4. Head of Household (HOH)** — available to unmarried taxpayers who pay more than half the cost of maintaining a home for a qualifying person (child, parent, or other dependent). HOH provides wider tax brackets and a larger standard deduction than Single.

**5. Qualifying Surviving Spouse** — available for two years after a spouse's death if the taxpayer maintains a home for a dependent child. Uses the same brackets and standard deduction as MFJ.

> Exam tip: Head of Household status is a frequent exam topic. The three requirements are: (1) unmarried on the last day of the year, (2) paid more than half the cost of maintaining a home, and (3) a qualifying person lived in the home for more than half the year (a dependent parent does not need to live with the taxpayer but the taxpayer must pay more than half the parent's housing costs).

## Standard Deduction vs. Itemized Deductions

After calculating gross income and above-the-line deductions to arrive at AGI, the taxpayer subtracts either the **standard deduction** or **itemized deductions** — whichever is greater.

### Standard Deduction (2026 amounts are indexed for inflation)

| Filing Status | Approximate Amount |
|--------------|-------------------|
| Single | $15,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly | $30,000 |
| Married Filing Separately | $15,000 |
| Head of Household | $22,500 |

Additional standard deductions apply for taxpayers who are age 65 or older or blind.

### Itemized Deductions (Schedule A)

Common itemized deductions include:

**Medical and dental expenses** — Amounts exceeding **7.5% of AGI**. Only unreimbursed expenses qualify (insurance premiums, prescription drugs, doctor visits, long-term care).

**State and local taxes (SALT)** — State income taxes (or sales taxes), real property taxes, and personal property taxes. Capped at **$10,000** ($5,000 MFS) under current law.

**Home mortgage interest** — Interest on acquisition indebtedness up to $750,000 ($375,000 MFS) for mortgages taken after December 15, 2017. Home equity interest is only deductible if the funds were used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home.

**Charitable contributions** — Cash contributions to qualified organizations, generally limited to **60% of AGI** for cash. Non-cash contributions are generally limited to 30% of AGI. Contributions must be substantiated (written acknowledgment required for contributions of $250 or more).

**Casualty and theft losses** — Only deductible if attributable to a federally declared disaster. Subject to a $100 per-event floor and a 10% of AGI threshold.

## Calculating AGI and Taxable Income

The tax formula for individuals follows a specific structure:

**Worked Example:**

Jordan, a single taxpayer, has the following income and deductions:

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Salary | $85,000 |
| Interest income | $2,000 |
| Student loan interest paid | $2,500 |
| IRA contribution (deductible) | $6,500 |
| Mortgage interest paid | $9,000 |
| State income taxes paid | $5,500 |
| Charitable contributions | $3,000 |

**Step 1: Gross Income**
$85,000 + $2,000 = **$87,000**

**Step 2: Above-the-line deductions (adjustments)**
Student loan interest: $2,500
IRA contribution: $6,500
Total adjustments: **$9,000**

**Step 3: Adjusted Gross Income (AGI)**
$87,000 - $9,000 = **$78,000**

**Step 4: Greater of standard or itemized deductions**
Itemized: $9,000 + $5,500 + $3,000 = $17,500
Standard deduction (Single): ~$15,000
Greater amount: **$17,500** (itemized)

**Step 5: Taxable Income**
$78,000 - $17,500 = **$60,500**

## Above-the-Line Deductions (For AGI)

These deductions are subtracted from gross income to arrive at AGI and are available regardless of whether the taxpayer itemizes. Key above-the-line deductions include:

- **Educator expenses** — up to $300 for qualifying K-12 teachers
- **Self-employment tax** — the deductible portion (50%) of self-employment tax
- **Self-employed health insurance** — 100% of health insurance premiums for self-employed individuals
- **IRA contributions** — deductible traditional IRA contributions (subject to income phase-outs if covered by an employer plan)
- **Student loan interest** — up to $2,500, subject to income phase-outs
- **Health Savings Account (HSA) contributions** — for taxpayers covered by high-deductible health plans
- **Alimony paid** — for agreements executed before 2019
- **Moving expenses** — only for active-duty military members

> Exam tip: The distinction between above-the-line (for AGI) and below-the-line (from AGI) deductions matters because AGI is a threshold for many tax benefits. A lower AGI can increase eligibility for credits, deductions, and exclusions that phase out at higher income levels.

## Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI)

Many tax benefits phase out based on **Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI)**, which is AGI with certain items added back. The specific add-backs vary by provision:

| Tax Benefit | MAGI = AGI + ... | Phase-Out Range (Single) |
|-------------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| Roth IRA contribution | Student loan interest, tuition deduction, foreign earned income | $146,000–$161,000 |
| Traditional IRA deduction (if covered by employer plan) | Same add-backs | $77,000–$87,000 |
| Education credits (AOTC/LLC) | Foreign earned income exclusion | $80,000–$90,000 |
| Child tax credit | (AGI itself — no add-backs) | $200,000 ($400,000 MFJ) |
| Passive activity rental allowance | (AGI without passive losses) | $100,000–$150,000 |

> Exam tip: When an exam question says "MAGI" without specifying the provision, it typically means AGI with the foreign earned income exclusion and certain deductions added back. Always check which provision is being tested — the MAGI definition varies.

## At-Risk Rules (Section 465)

When an individual reports losses from a business activity (including losses passed through from partnerships and S corporations), the deductible loss cannot exceed the taxpayer's **amount at risk** in that activity.

**Amount at risk includes:**
- Cash invested in the activity
- Adjusted basis of property contributed
- Amounts borrowed for which the taxpayer is **personally liable** (recourse debt)
- **Qualified nonrecourse financing** for real estate activities (debt secured by the real property, from a qualified lender, where no person is personally liable)

**Not included in at-risk amount:**
- Nonrecourse loans (except qualified nonrecourse financing for real estate)
- Amounts protected against loss by guarantees, stop-loss agreements, or insurance
- Amounts borrowed from related parties who have an interest in the activity

Losses exceeding the at-risk amount are **suspended** and carry forward indefinitely until the at-risk amount increases.

## Passive Activity Loss Rules (Section 469)

After passing the basis and at-risk limitations, losses from **passive activities** can only offset income from other passive activities. Excess passive losses are suspended until the taxpayer either generates passive income or disposes of the entire activity in a fully taxable transaction.

### What Is a Passive Activity?

A passive activity is any trade or business in which the taxpayer does **not materially participate**, plus any **rental activity** (with limited exceptions).

### Seven Material Participation Tests

A taxpayer materially participates in an activity if they meet **any one** of these tests:

| Test | Description |
|------|-------------|
| 1 | Participated more than **500 hours** during the year |
| 2 | Participation constituted **substantially all** of the participation in the activity |
| 3 | Participated more than **100 hours** and no other individual participated more |
| 4 | Activity is a **significant participation activity** (100+ hours) and aggregate participation in all such activities exceeds **500 hours** |
| 5 | Materially participated in **any 5 of the prior 10 years** |
| 6 | Activity is a **personal service activity** and the taxpayer participated in any **3 prior years** |
| 7 | Based on all facts and circumstances, the taxpayer participated on a **regular, continuous, and substantial** basis (must exceed 100 hours) |

### Rental Activity Exception

Rental activities are generally passive regardless of participation level. However, a taxpayer who **actively participates** in rental real estate can deduct up to **$25,000** of rental losses against nonpassive income.

**Active participation** is a lower standard than material participation — it requires making management decisions (approving tenants, setting rental terms, approving repairs) but not day-to-day involvement.

The $25,000 allowance phases out by $1 for every $2 of AGI above **$100,000**, reaching zero at **$150,000** AGI.

### Real Estate Professional Exception

A taxpayer who qualifies as a **real estate professional** can treat rental real estate activities as nonpassive. Both requirements must be met:

1. More than **50%** of personal services performed during the year are in real property trades or businesses
2. More than **750 hours** of services in real property trades or businesses during the year

Each rental property is treated as a separate activity unless the taxpayer elects to aggregate all rental real estate activities.

### Disposition of Passive Activities

When a taxpayer disposes of their **entire interest** in a passive activity in a **fully taxable transaction** (typically a sale to an unrelated party), all suspended passive losses from that activity are released and become fully deductible against any type of income.

> Exam tip: The three-tier loss limitation order is heavily tested: (1) basis → (2) at-risk → (3) passive activity. Each tier must be satisfied before moving to the next. Memorize this sequence and the material participation 500-hour test — they appear on nearly every REG exam.

## Key Terms

- **Gross income** — all income from whatever source derived unless specifically excluded by the Internal Revenue Code
- **Adjusted Gross Income (AGI)** — gross income minus above-the-line deductions; a key threshold used throughout the tax code
- **Standard deduction** — a fixed dollar amount based on filing status that reduces taxable income for taxpayers who do not itemize
- **Itemized deductions** — specific expenses (medical, taxes, interest, charitable) that may be deducted in lieu of the standard deduction
- **Above-the-line deduction** — a deduction subtracted from gross income to arrive at AGI, available regardless of whether the taxpayer itemizes
- **SALT cap** — the $10,000 limit on the deduction for state and local taxes
- **Provisional income** — the measure used to determine taxable Social Security benefits, calculated as AGI (excluding SS) plus tax-exempt interest plus 50% of benefits
- **Modified AGI (MAGI)** — AGI with specific items added back, used as the threshold for various phase-outs (definition varies by provision)
- **At-risk amount** — the maximum loss deductible under Section 465, generally limited to the amount the taxpayer could actually lose economically
- **Passive activity** — a trade or business in which the taxpayer does not materially participate, or any rental activity (with limited exceptions)
- **Material participation** — sufficient involvement in a business activity (most commonly 500+ hours) that removes it from passive classification
- **Schedule K-1** — the form reporting a taxpayer's share of income, deductions, and credits from a pass-through entity (partnership, S corporation, trust/estate)

## Practice Problems

**1.** Which of the following is excluded from gross income?

a) Unemployment compensation
b) Cancellation of debt income
c) Workers' compensation benefits
d) Gambling winnings

**Answer:** c) Workers' compensation benefits — Benefits received for job-related injuries or illness are excluded from gross income. Unemployment compensation, cancellation of debt income, and gambling winnings are all includable in gross income.

---

**2.** A taxpayer pays $12,000 in state income taxes and $5,000 in real property taxes. What is the total deductible amount for state and local taxes on Schedule A?

a) $5,000
b) $10,000
c) $12,000
d) $17,000

**Answer:** b) $10,000 — The total SALT deduction (state income taxes plus property taxes) is capped at $10,000 under current law, even though the taxpayer paid $17,000 in combined state and local taxes.

---

**3.** Which of the following deductions is an above-the-line deduction (for AGI)?

a) Mortgage interest on a personal residence
b) Charitable contributions
c) Student loan interest
d) State income taxes

**Answer:** c) Student loan interest — Student loan interest (up to $2,500) is an above-the-line deduction subtracted from gross income to arrive at AGI. Mortgage interest, charitable contributions, and state income taxes are all itemized (below-the-line) deductions.
