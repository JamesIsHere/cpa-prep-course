# Filing Status and Dependents

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the five federal filing statuses and the requirements for each
- Determine whether a taxpayer qualifies for Head of Household or Qualifying Surviving Spouse status
- Apply the qualifying child and qualifying relative dependency tests
- Calculate standard deduction amounts for each filing status using 2025 figures
- Resolve tiebreaker rules when multiple taxpayers claim the same dependent

## Filing Status Overview

Every individual tax return begins with a filing status determination. Filing status affects the tax bracket thresholds, standard deduction amount, eligibility for credits, and phase-out ranges. There are five filing statuses under the Internal Revenue Code:

1. **Single** (S)
2. **Married Filing Jointly** (MFJ)
3. **Married Filing Separately** (MFS)
4. **Head of Household** (HOH)
5. **Qualifying Surviving Spouse** (QSS)

Filing status is determined as of **December 31** of the tax year. If a taxpayer is married on December 31, the taxpayer is considered married for the entire year — even if the marriage occurred on December 31 itself.

## Single

A taxpayer files as Single if, on the last day of the tax year, the taxpayer is:

- **Unmarried**, or
- **Legally separated** under a decree of separate maintenance or divorce

A taxpayer who is widowed during the year and does not remarry before year-end may file a joint return with the deceased spouse for the year of death. For subsequent years, the taxpayer may qualify as a Qualifying Surviving Spouse or must file as Single or Head of Household.

## Married Filing Jointly (MFJ)

A married couple may file a **joint return** reporting combined income, deductions, and credits. Both spouses are **jointly and severally liable** for the tax on a joint return — meaning the IRS can collect the entire tax from either spouse.

**Key rules:**

- Both spouses must agree to file jointly
- Both must have the same tax year (with limited exceptions)
- A joint return may be filed even if only one spouse has income
- An executor or administrator can file a joint return for a deceased taxpayer for the year of death, provided the surviving spouse has not remarried before year-end

**Innocent spouse relief** (Section 6015) may be available when one spouse claims an erroneous item on a joint return without the other spouse's knowledge.

## Married Filing Separately (MFS)

A married taxpayer may choose to file separately. MFS uses the **narrowest tax brackets** and **smallest standard deduction** among married statuses, and it disqualifies the taxpayer from several credits and deductions:

- Earned Income Credit — not available
- Child and Dependent Care Credit — generally not available
- Education credits — not available
- Student loan interest deduction — not available
- Standard deduction is $15,000 (2025) — half of MFJ

**When MFS may be beneficial:**

- One spouse has significant medical expenses (the 7.5% AGI floor is lower with only one spouse's income)
- One spouse has unpaid taxes, student loans in default, or other collection issues and wants to protect refunds
- The spouses want to limit joint and several liability

**Important rule:** If one spouse itemizes, the other spouse **must also itemize** — the standard deduction is not available to the other spouse.

## Head of Household (HOH)

Head of Household provides wider tax brackets and a larger standard deduction than Single. To qualify, a taxpayer must meet **all three** requirements:

1. **Unmarried** on the last day of the tax year (or considered unmarried — see below)
2. **Paid more than half** the cost of maintaining a home for the year
3. A **qualifying person** lived with the taxpayer in the home for more than half the year (exception: a dependent parent does not need to live with the taxpayer)

**Considered unmarried rule:** A married taxpayer may be treated as unmarried for HOH purposes if:

- The taxpayer lived apart from the spouse for the **last 6 months** of the tax year
- The taxpayer paid more than half the cost of maintaining a home
- A qualifying child lived in the home for more than half the year
- The taxpayer files a separate return

**Qualifying persons for HOH:**

- A qualifying child (as defined under the dependency tests)
- A qualifying relative who is the taxpayer's parent (the parent does not need to live with the taxpayer — paying more than half the cost of the parent's separate home qualifies)
- A qualifying relative (other than a parent) who lives with the taxpayer for more than half the year

**Worked Example — HOH Eligibility:**

Maria is unmarried and maintains a home where she and her 12-year-old son live all year. Maria's income is $60,000 and she paid all household expenses. Her son has no income.

- **Unmarried?** Yes.
- **Paid >50% of home costs?** Yes — she paid all of them.
- **Qualifying person?** Yes — her son is a qualifying child under age 19 who lived with her all year.
- **Result:** Maria qualifies for Head of Household status.

## Qualifying Surviving Spouse (QSS)

A taxpayer whose spouse died in one of the **two prior tax years** may file as a Qualifying Surviving Spouse if:

1. The taxpayer was eligible to file a joint return with the deceased spouse in the year of death
2. The taxpayer has **not remarried** before the end of the current tax year
3. The taxpayer maintains a home that is the **principal residence** of a dependent child (son, daughter, or stepchild) for the **entire year**
4. The taxpayer pays more than half the cost of maintaining the home

QSS uses the same tax brackets and standard deduction as MFJ ($30,000 in 2025). It is available for the **two years after** the year of death — not the year of death itself (in the year of death, the surviving spouse files a joint return).

**Timeline example:**

| Year | Event | Filing Status |
|------|-------|---------------|
| 2023 | Spouse dies June 15 | MFJ (joint return for year of death) |
| 2024 | Taxpayer has dependent child at home, not remarried | QSS |
| 2025 | Same situation continues | QSS |
| 2026 | Two-year window expired | HOH or Single (depending on whether qualifying person test is met) |

## Standard Deduction Amounts (2025)

| Filing Status | Standard Deduction | Additional (Age 65+ or Blind) |
|---------------|-------------------|-------------------------------|
| Single | $15,000 | $2,000 each |
| Married Filing Jointly | $30,000 | $1,600 each |
| Married Filing Separately | $15,000 | $1,600 each |
| Head of Household | $22,500 | $2,000 each |
| Qualifying Surviving Spouse | $30,000 | $1,600 each |

The additional standard deduction is $2,000 per qualifying condition for **unmarried** taxpayers (Single or HOH) and $1,600 for **married** taxpayers. A taxpayer who is both 65+ and blind receives **two** additional amounts.

**Dependent's standard deduction:** A taxpayer who can be claimed as a dependent on another return has a limited standard deduction equal to the **greater of** $1,350 or earned income + $450, but not exceeding the regular standard deduction for their filing status.

## Dependency Tests — Two Paths

The Internal Revenue Code provides two categories of dependents: **qualifying child** and **qualifying relative**. A person must meet all the tests in one category or the other to be claimed as a dependent.

### Qualifying Child Tests

Use the mnemonic **CARES** to remember the five tests:

| Test | Requirement |
|------|-------------|
| **C**lose relative | The child must be the taxpayer's son, daughter, stepchild, foster child, sibling, step-sibling, or a descendant of any of these |
| **A**ge | Under 19 at year-end, OR under 24 if a full-time student, OR any age if permanently and totally disabled |
| **R**esidency | Lived with the taxpayer for more than half the year (temporary absences for school, illness, or military service count as time lived in the home) |
| **E**liminate gross income test | Not applicable — there is no gross income test for a qualifying child |
| **S**upport (no self-support) | The child did NOT provide more than half of their own support |

**Important:** The qualifying child does **not** have a gross income test. A qualifying child can earn any amount and still be claimed — as long as the child did not provide more than half of their own support.

### Qualifying Relative Tests

A qualifying relative must meet **all four** tests — use **SNIG**:

| Test | Requirement |
|------|-------------|
| **S**upport | The taxpayer must provide more than 50% of the person's total support |
| **N**ot a qualifying child | The person cannot be a qualifying child of the taxpayer or any other taxpayer |
| **I**ncome (gross income test) | The person's gross income must be less than $5,050 (2025 threshold) |
| **G**eneral relationship or member of household | The person must be related to the taxpayer (parent, sibling, aunt, uncle, in-law, etc.) OR live with the taxpayer as a member of the household for the entire year |

### Joint Return Test (Applies to Both)

A dependent generally **cannot file a joint return** with their spouse. Exception: the joint return is filed solely to claim a refund of withholding, and neither spouse would have a tax liability filing separately.

### Citizenship Test (Applies to Both)

The dependent must be a U.S. citizen, U.S. national, U.S. resident alien, or a resident of Canada or Mexico.

## Tiebreaker Rules

When more than one taxpayer is eligible to claim the same person as a qualifying child, the **tiebreaker rules** determine who claims the dependent:

1. **Parent vs. non-parent:** The parent claims the child. If both parents are eligible but do not file jointly:
   - The parent with whom the child lived for the **longer period** during the year claims the child
   - If the child lived with each parent for an equal amount of time, the parent with the **higher AGI** claims the child
2. **Non-parent vs. non-parent:** The taxpayer with the **higher AGI** claims the child

A parent may release the claim to the noncustodial parent by signing **Form 8332** (Release/Revocation of Release of Claim to Exemption for Child by Custodial Parent).

**Worked Example — Tiebreaker:**

Jake (AGI $45,000) and his mother Linda (AGI $70,000) both live with Jake's 10-year-old daughter, Emma, for the entire year. Both meet the qualifying child tests.

- **Parent vs. non-parent:** Jake is the parent. Jake claims Emma as a qualifying child, regardless of AGI.
- Linda cannot claim Emma unless Jake (or any other eligible parent) does not claim her.

## Multiple Support Agreements

When no single taxpayer provides more than 50% of a person's support, but a group of taxpayers collectively provides more than 50%, a **multiple support agreement** (Form 2120) may be used. The taxpayer claiming the dependent must:

- Be one of the persons who would be eligible except for the 50% support test
- Provide more than **10%** of the person's support
- Obtain a signed statement from each other eligible person waiving their claim for that year

## Special Rules for Children of Divorced or Separated Parents

For divorced or separated parents, the **custodial parent** (the parent with whom the child lived for the greater number of nights) is generally entitled to claim the child. However, the custodial parent may release the claim using **Form 8332**, allowing the noncustodial parent to claim:

- The child as a dependent
- The Child Tax Credit

The **custodial parent** retains (regardless of Form 8332):

- Head of Household filing status
- Earned Income Credit
- Child and Dependent Care Credit

## Key Exam Tips

- Filing status is determined on **December 31** — a marriage or divorce on that date changes status for the entire year
- **HOH requires being unmarried** (or considered unmarried) — married taxpayers cannot file HOH unless they meet the considered-unmarried exception
- A **qualifying child** has no gross income test; a **qualifying relative** does ($5,050 in 2025)
- The **QSS** status is available for the two tax years **following** the year of death, not the year of death itself
- If one MFS spouse itemizes, the other **must also itemize** — they cannot take the standard deduction
- **Tiebreaker default:** parent wins over non-parent; among parents, longer residence wins; if equal, higher AGI wins
