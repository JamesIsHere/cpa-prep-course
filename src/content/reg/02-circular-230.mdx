# Treasury Circular 230

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the categories of practitioners authorized to practice before the IRS
- Understand the duties and restrictions imposed on practitioners under Circular 230
- Recognize the penalties for noncompliance and the disciplinary process
- Apply best practices for tax advisors in return preparation and tax opinions

## Who Can Practice Before the IRS?

**Treasury Circular 230** is the set of regulations (31 CFR Part 10) governing practice before the Internal Revenue Service. "Practice before the IRS" includes preparing and filing documents, communicating with the IRS on a taxpayer's behalf, and representing taxpayers in audits, appeals, and collections.

Three categories of practitioners have **unlimited practice rights**:

- **Attorneys** — licensed to practice law in any U.S. jurisdiction
- **Certified Public Accountants (CPAs)** — licensed in any U.S. jurisdiction
- **Enrolled Agents (EAs)** — individuals who pass a three-part IRS examination or have sufficient former IRS employee experience

Certain individuals have **limited practice rights**:

- **Enrolled Actuaries** — limited to matters involving retirement plans
- **Enrolled Retirement Plan Agents (ERPAs)** — limited to certain retirement plan forms
- **Annual Filing Season Program participants** — limited representation rights for returns they prepared
- **Unenrolled return preparers** — may only represent taxpayers during examinations of returns they prepared and signed

> Exam tip: The distinction between unlimited and limited practice rights is heavily tested. Remember that enrolled agents have the same unlimited rights as CPAs and attorneys — they are not limited preparers.

## Duties and Obligations of Practitioners

Circular 230 imposes specific duties on all practitioners:

### Due Diligence (Section 10.22)

Practitioners must exercise **due diligence** in preparing, approving, and filing tax returns, documents, affidavits, and other papers. This includes determining the correctness of oral or written representations made to the Department of the Treasury and to clients.

### Accuracy in Returns and Documents (Section 10.34)

A practitioner may not sign a return or advise a client to take a position on a return unless the position:

- Has a **reasonable basis** (the minimum threshold for undisclosed positions)
- Is supported by **substantial authority** if the position would otherwise trigger an accuracy-related penalty
- Is adequately disclosed on the return when relying on the reasonable basis standard

For tax shelters or reportable transactions, the practitioner must reasonably believe the position is **more likely than not** to be sustained on the merits.

### Conflicts of Interest (Section 10.29)

A practitioner may not represent a client if a **conflict of interest** exists unless:

1. The practitioner reasonably believes competent representation is possible
2. The representation is not prohibited by law
3. Each affected client gives **informed written consent**

### Other Key Duties

- **Return of client records** — A practitioner must promptly return all client records upon request, even if fees are unpaid. The practitioner may retain copies.
- **Prompt disposition** — Practitioners must not unreasonably delay resolution of any IRS matter.
- **Fees** — A practitioner may not charge an **unconscionable fee**. Contingent fees are generally prohibited for filing original returns but are permitted for amended returns claiming refunds and for services in connection with IRS examinations or proceedings.

> Exam tip: Contingent fees are a frequent exam topic. Remember the key rule — contingent fees are prohibited for original return preparation but allowed for amended return refund claims and for examination or litigation representation.

## Prohibited Conduct and Penalties

### Prohibited Actions

Circular 230 specifically prohibits practitioners from:

- Providing false or misleading information to the IRS
- Using threats, false accusations, or coercion against a client
- Soliciting employment through **in-person contact** or **uninvited written solicitations** (with exceptions for former clients and family members)
- Cashing refund checks for clients (with limited exceptions)
- Practicing while under suspension or disbarment

### Sanctions and Disciplinary Proceedings

The IRS Office of Professional Responsibility (OPR) enforces Circular 230. Sanctions include:

- **Censure** — a public reprimand
- **Suspension** — temporary loss of practice privileges
- **Disbarment** — permanent loss of practice privileges (may petition for reinstatement after five years)
- **Monetary penalties** — up to the gross income derived from the conduct giving rise to the penalty

Disciplinary proceedings begin with a complaint filed by the OPR. The practitioner is entitled to a hearing before an administrative law judge. Appeals go to the Secretary of the Treasury or a delegate.

### Incompetence and Disreputable Conduct (Section 10.51)

Examples of disreputable conduct include:

- Conviction of a criminal offense related to tax practice
- Giving false or misleading information to the IRS
- Willful failure to file a federal tax return
- Misappropriation of client funds
- Willfully assisting in a violation of any federal tax law

## Best Practices and Written Tax Advice

### Best Practices (Section 10.33)

Tax advisors should:

- Communicate clearly with clients about the terms of engagement
- Establish the relevant facts, evaluate the reasonableness of assumptions, and relate applicable law to the facts
- Advise clients regarding the importance of conclusions reached in any tax advice
- Act fairly and with integrity in practice before the IRS

### Written Tax Advice (Section 10.37)

When providing **written tax advice**, a practitioner must:

- Base the advice on reasonable factual and legal assumptions
- Reasonably consider all relevant facts the practitioner knows or should know
- Not rely on unreasonable factual or legal assumptions
- Not take into account the possibility that a return will not be audited

This standard applies to all written tax advice — Circular 230 no longer distinguishes between "covered opinions" and other written advice.

## Federal Tax Procedures

Beyond Circular 230 practitioner rules, the CPA exam tests federal tax procedure — how the IRS administers the tax system and how taxpayers resolve disputes.

### Statute of Limitations

The statute of limitations determines how long the IRS has to assess additional tax and how long taxpayers have to claim refunds:

**Assessment (IRS):**
- **General rule:** 3 years from the later of the filing date or the due date of the return
- **Substantial omission (>25% of gross income):** 6 years — applies when the taxpayer omits more than 25% of gross income stated on the return
- **Fraud or failure to file:** No statute of limitations — the IRS can assess at any time
- **Basis overstatement:** 6 years if the omission is attributable to an overstatement of basis that exceeds 25% of gross income

**Refund claims (Taxpayer):**
- **General rule:** The later of 3 years from filing or 2 years from payment
- If the claim is filed within the 3-year window, the refund is limited to the tax paid within the 3 years plus the extension period preceding the claim
- If filed after 3 years but within 2 years of payment, the refund is limited to the amount paid in the 2-year period

> Exam tip: The 25% omission rule is heavily tested. If a taxpayer reports $100,000 of gross income but actually earned $130,000, the $30,000 omission exceeds 25% of reported income, extending the statute to 6 years. Adequate disclosure of the omitted item on the return can prevent the extension.

### IRS Audit Process

The IRS selects returns for examination through several methods:

- **DIF scoring** — Discriminant Index Function scores flag returns with high audit potential based on statistical norms
- **Information matching** — Automated comparison of W-2s, 1099s, and K-1s against reported amounts
- **Related examinations** — Returns selected because of issues found on a related taxpayer's return
- **Random selection** — Occasional random audits for compliance research

**Types of examinations:**
- **Correspondence audit** — Conducted by mail for simple issues (missing documentation, math errors)
- **Office audit** — Conducted at an IRS office for more complex individual returns
- **Field audit** — Conducted at the taxpayer's place of business or home for business returns and complex matters

After examination, the IRS issues a **30-day letter** proposing adjustments. The taxpayer can:
1. Agree and sign the assessment
2. Request an **Appeals conference** (administrative review within the IRS)
3. Do nothing — the IRS issues a **90-day letter** (statutory notice of deficiency)

After receiving the 90-day letter, the taxpayer has 90 days (150 days if outside the U.S.) to file a petition with the **U.S. Tax Court** without paying the deficiency first. Alternatively, the taxpayer can pay the tax and sue for a refund in **U.S. District Court** or the **U.S. Court of Federal Claims**.

### Taxpayer Penalties

**Accuracy-related penalties (Section 6662):** 20% of the underpayment attributable to:
- Negligence or disregard of rules and regulations
- Substantial understatement of income tax (the greater of 10% of the correct tax or $5,000; $10,000 for corporations)
- Substantial valuation misstatement (150% or more of correct value)
- Gross valuation misstatement (200% or more) — penalty increases to 40%

**Failure to file (Section 6651(a)(1)):** 5% of unpaid tax per month, up to 25% maximum. If the return is filed more than 60 days late, the minimum penalty is the lesser of $510 or 100% of the tax due.

**Failure to pay (Section 6651(a)(2)):** 0.5% of unpaid tax per month, up to 25% maximum. When both penalties apply simultaneously, the failure to file penalty is reduced by the failure to pay penalty (net 4.5% per month for failure to file).

**Fraud penalty (Section 6663):** 75% of the portion of the underpayment attributable to fraud. The IRS bears the burden of proving fraud by clear and convincing evidence.

> Exam tip: Know the difference between the failure to file penalty (5%/month, max 25%) and the failure to pay penalty (0.5%/month, max 25%). The file penalty is 10x more expensive — always file on time, even if you cannot pay.

### Tax Return Preparer Penalties

**Section 6694(a) — Unreasonable positions:** $1,000 per return (or 50% of income derived, if greater) for undisclosed positions lacking substantial authority. For disclosed positions, the standard is reasonable basis.

**Section 6694(b) — Willful or reckless conduct:** $5,000 per return (or 75% of income derived, if greater) for willful attempts to understate tax liability or reckless/intentional disregard of rules.

**Section 6695 — Administrative failures:** $50 per failure for items such as failing to furnish a copy of the return to the taxpayer, failing to sign the return, or failing to include a PTIN. Each penalty is capped at $28,000 per year.

**Section 7216 — Unauthorized disclosure:** Criminal penalty (up to $1,000 fine and/or 1 year imprisonment) for knowingly or recklessly disclosing taxpayer information.

## Key Terms

- **Due diligence** — the standard of care requiring practitioners to exercise reasonable effort in preparing and reviewing tax returns and documents
- **Reasonable basis** — the minimum confidence standard for taking a position on a return; generally interpreted as approximately a 20% likelihood of being sustained
- **Substantial authority** — a higher confidence standard (approximately 40%) that avoids accuracy-related penalties without disclosure
- **More likely than not** — a standard requiring greater than 50% likelihood of being sustained; required for tax shelters
- **Disbarment** — permanent revocation of a practitioner's right to practice before the IRS
- **Contingent fee** — a fee arrangement where payment depends on the outcome; generally prohibited for original return preparation

## Practice Problems

**1.** Under Circular 230, which of the following fee arrangements is permitted?

a) A contingent fee for preparing an original individual income tax return
b) A contingent fee for preparing an original corporate income tax return
c) A contingent fee for representing a taxpayer in an IRS examination
d) A contingent fee based on a percentage of the refund shown on an original return

**Answer:** c) Representing a taxpayer in an IRS examination — Contingent fees are permitted for services in connection with IRS examinations, proceedings, and amended return refund claims, but are prohibited for the preparation of original returns.

---

**2.** A CPA has been asked to represent a new client whose interests may conflict with an existing client. Under Circular 230, the CPA may accept the engagement if:

a) The CPA obtains verbal consent from both clients
b) The CPA reasonably believes competent representation is possible and obtains informed written consent from each affected client
c) The CPA discloses the conflict to the IRS before proceeding
d) The new client signs a hold harmless agreement

**Answer:** b) Competent representation and informed written consent — Circular 230 requires the practitioner to reasonably believe competent representation is possible, that it is not prohibited by law, and that each affected client provides informed written consent.

---

**3.** Which of the following is considered disreputable conduct under Circular 230?

a) Charging a fee higher than the local average
b) Declining to represent a client in an IRS appeal
c) Willfully failing to file a federal tax return the practitioner is required to file
d) Advising a client to file an extension

**Answer:** c) Willfully failing to file a required federal tax return — This is specifically listed as disreputable conduct under Section 10.51. Charging high fees (unless unconscionable), declining representation, and filing extensions are not disreputable conduct.
