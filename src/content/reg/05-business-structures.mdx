# Business Structures

## Learning Objectives

- Compare the formation requirements, liability protections, and management structures of major business entities
- Identify the tax treatment differences between sole proprietorships, partnerships, LLCs, and corporations
- Analyze the advantages and disadvantages of each entity type for different business scenarios
- Understand the legal requirements for converting between entity types

## Sole Proprietorships

A **sole proprietorship** is the simplest business structure. It is not a separate legal entity — the owner and the business are one and the same.

**Formation:** No formal filing required. The business exists as soon as the owner begins conducting business. A fictitious name registration ("doing business as" or DBA) may be required by the state.

**Liability:** The owner has **unlimited personal liability** for all business debts and obligations. Personal assets (home, savings, vehicles) are exposed to business creditors.

**Tax treatment:** Business income and expenses are reported on **Schedule C** of the owner's Form 1040. The owner pays self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare) on net business income. There is no separate entity-level tax return.

**Key characteristics:**
- Easiest and least expensive to form
- Complete management control
- No continuity of life — the business ends at the owner's death
- Cannot bring in equity partners without changing structure

## Partnerships

A **partnership** is an association of two or more persons carrying on a business as co-owners for profit. The two primary forms are general partnerships and limited partnerships.

### General Partnership

**Formation:** Can be formed by oral agreement, written agreement, or simply by conduct. No state filing is required, although a written **partnership agreement** is strongly recommended. The Revised Uniform Partnership Act (RUPA) governs in most states.

**Liability:** Each general partner has **joint and several liability** for all partnership debts. Creditors can pursue any individual partner's personal assets for the full amount of a partnership obligation.

**Management:** Unless the partnership agreement provides otherwise, each general partner has an equal right to participate in management. Ordinary business decisions require a majority vote; extraordinary decisions (admitting new partners, amending the agreement) require unanimous consent.

**Tax treatment:** A partnership is a **pass-through entity**. It files an informational return (Form 1065) but pays no entity-level tax. Each partner receives a **Schedule K-1** reporting their distributive share of income, deductions, gains, losses, and credits.

### Limited Partnership

A limited partnership has at least one **general partner** (unlimited liability, management authority) and one or more **limited partners** (liability limited to their investment, no management role).

**Formation:** Requires filing a **certificate of limited partnership** with the state. This is a key distinction from general partnerships.

**Liability:** Limited partners risk only their capital contribution — unless they participate in management, which can expose them to general partner liability under some state laws.

> Exam tip: A limited partner who participates in the control of the business may lose their limited liability protection. This is a frequently tested concept.

## Limited Liability Companies

A **limited liability company (LLC)** combines the liability protection of a corporation with the tax flexibility of a partnership.

**Formation:** Requires filing **articles of organization** with the state. Members typically adopt an **operating agreement** that governs management, profit sharing, and transfer of interests.

**Liability:** Members enjoy **limited liability** — their personal assets are generally protected from business debts. This protection can be lost through personal guarantees or piercing the corporate veil (commingling funds, inadequate capitalization, fraud).

**Management:** An LLC can be **member-managed** (all members participate) or **manager-managed** (designated managers run operations). The operating agreement controls.

**Tax treatment:** The IRS applies **default classification rules**:
- Single-member LLC: Disregarded entity, taxed as a sole proprietorship (Schedule C)
- Multi-member LLC: Taxed as a partnership (Form 1065)
- Any LLC may elect to be taxed as a C corporation (Form 8832) or an S corporation (Form 2553)

This flexibility is known as **check-the-box** election and is a significant advantage of the LLC form.

## Corporations

### C Corporation

**Formation:** Requires filing **articles of incorporation** with the state. The corporation is governed by bylaws adopted by the board of directors. Shareholders elect directors, who appoint officers to manage daily operations.

**Liability:** Shareholders have **limited liability** — they can lose only their investment. Directors and officers may be personally liable for their own wrongful acts.

**Tax treatment:** A C corporation is a **separate taxable entity**. It pays corporate tax at a flat **21% rate** on taxable income (Form 1120). When after-tax earnings are distributed as **dividends**, shareholders pay tax on the dividends on their individual returns. This creates **double taxation** — once at the corporate level and again at the shareholder level.

**Worked Example:**

ABC Corp earns $100,000 of taxable income and distributes all after-tax income as dividends.

| Step | Calculation | Amount |
|------|------------|--------|
| Corporate tax | $100,000 x 21% | $21,000 |
| After-tax income | $100,000 - $21,000 | $79,000 |
| Dividend to shareholder | | $79,000 |
| Shareholder tax (assuming 15% rate) | $79,000 x 15% | $11,850 |
| **Total tax burden** | $21,000 + $11,850 | **$32,850** |

The effective combined tax rate is 32.85% due to double taxation.

### S Corporation

An **S corporation** is a corporation that has elected pass-through taxation under Subchapter S of the Internal Revenue Code. It provides the limited liability of a corporation without double taxation.

**Eligibility requirements:**
- No more than **100 shareholders**
- Shareholders must be individuals, estates, certain trusts, or tax-exempt organizations (no partnerships, corporations, or nonresident aliens)
- Only **one class of stock** (voting rights may differ)
- Must be a domestic corporation

**Tax treatment:** The S corporation files Form 1120-S but generally pays no entity-level tax. Income passes through to shareholders on Schedule K-1, similar to a partnership.

> Exam tip: Entity selection is one of the most heavily tested topics on REG. Build a comparison chart of formation requirements, liability exposure, tax treatment, and number-of-owner rules for each entity type.

## Comparing Entity Types

| Feature | Sole Prop | General Partnership | Limited Partnership | LLC | C Corp | S Corp |
|---------|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|-----|--------|--------|
| Formation filing | None | None | Certificate required | Articles of organization | Articles of incorporation | Articles + S election |
| Owner liability | Unlimited | Unlimited (joint & several) | GP unlimited; LP limited | Limited | Limited | Limited |
| Tax return | Schedule C | Form 1065 | Form 1065 | Flexible | Form 1120 | Form 1120-S |
| Entity-level tax | No | No | No | Flexible | Yes (21%) | Generally no |
| Max owners | 1 | No limit | No limit | No limit | No limit | 100 |

## Key Terms

- **Joint and several liability** — each partner can be held individually responsible for the full amount of a partnership obligation
- **Pass-through entity** — a business that does not pay entity-level tax; income flows to the owners' individual returns
- **Double taxation** — the taxation of corporate income at the entity level and again when distributed to shareholders as dividends
- **Check-the-box election** — an IRS rule allowing certain entities (such as LLCs) to choose their tax classification
- **Articles of incorporation** — the formal document filed with the state to create a corporation
- **Operating agreement** — the governing document of an LLC, specifying member rights, management structure, and profit-sharing

## Practice Problems

**1.** Which of the following business structures requires a state filing to be formed?

a) General partnership
b) Sole proprietorship
c) Limited partnership
d) Joint venture

**Answer:** c) Limited partnership — A limited partnership requires filing a certificate of limited partnership with the state. General partnerships, sole proprietorships, and joint ventures can be formed without any state filing (though a DBA may be required).

---

**2.** Alpha LLC has three members and has not made any tax classification election. How will the IRS classify Alpha for tax purposes?

a) As a C corporation
b) As an S corporation
c) As a partnership
d) As a disregarded entity

**Answer:** c) As a partnership — Under the default check-the-box rules, a multi-member LLC that has not elected otherwise is classified as a partnership for federal tax purposes.

---

**3.** Which of the following is a disadvantage of the C corporation form?

a) Shareholders have unlimited liability
b) The corporation cannot have more than 100 shareholders
c) Corporate income is subject to double taxation
d) The corporation must allocate income to shareholders annually

**Answer:** c) Double taxation — C corporation income is taxed at the entity level (21%) and again when distributed as dividends to shareholders. The 100-shareholder limit applies to S corps, not C corps. C corp shareholders have limited liability. C corps do not allocate income — that is a feature of pass-through entities.
