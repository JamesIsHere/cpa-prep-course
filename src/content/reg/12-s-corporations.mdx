# S Corporations

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the eligibility requirements and election process for S corporation status
- Calculate shareholder basis and the ordering of basis adjustments
- Apply the rules for S corporation distributions and their tax treatment
- Recognize when the built-in gains tax and other entity-level taxes apply

## S Corporation Eligibility

An **S corporation** is a corporation that has elected pass-through taxation under **Subchapter S** of the Internal Revenue Code. The corporation itself generally pays no federal income tax — instead, income, deductions, gains, losses, and credits pass through to shareholders.

To qualify for S corporation status, the entity must meet **all** of the following requirements:

1. **Domestic corporation** — must be incorporated in the United States
2. **Eligible shareholders only** — shareholders must be individuals, estates, certain trusts (grantor trusts, qualified Subchapter S trusts, electing small business trusts), and certain tax-exempt organizations. **Not eligible:** partnerships, corporations, and nonresident aliens
3. **Maximum 100 shareholders** — members of a family (up to six generations) can be treated as a single shareholder for counting purposes
4. **One class of stock** — only one class of stock is permitted, although differences in **voting rights** are allowed (voting and nonvoting common stock is acceptable)
5. **Not an ineligible corporation** — certain financial institutions, insurance companies, and domestic international sales corporations cannot elect S status

### Making the Election

The S election is made by filing **Form 2553** signed by all shareholders. The election must be filed:

- By the **15th day of the third month** of the tax year (March 15 for calendar-year corporations) to be effective for the current year
- At any time during the preceding tax year

If filed late, the election is effective for the following tax year.

> Exam tip: All shareholders must consent to the S election. If a new shareholder acquires stock in an existing S corporation, they do not need to consent — the election continues. But if the election is being made, every current shareholder must sign Form 2553.

### Termination of S Status

S corporation status terminates if:

- **Shareholders revoke** the election (requires shareholders holding more than 50% of shares to consent)
- An **eligibility requirement is violated** (e.g., a corporation becomes a shareholder, more than 100 shareholders, second class of stock issued)
- **Passive investment income** exceeds 25% of gross receipts for three consecutive years and the corporation has accumulated C corporation E&P

After involuntary termination, the corporation generally cannot re-elect S status for **five years** without IRS consent.

## Pass-Through Taxation

An S corporation files **Form 1120-S** (informational return) and issues **Schedule K-1** to each shareholder. Items are divided into two categories:

### Separately Stated Items

Certain items must be separately stated because they may affect each shareholder's tax liability differently:

- Capital gains and losses (short-term and long-term)
- Section 1231 gains and losses
- Charitable contributions
- Tax-exempt interest income
- Investment interest expense
- Rental income or loss
- Foreign taxes paid
- Section 179 deduction

### Nonseparately Stated Income (Ordinary Business Income)

All remaining items of income and deduction are netted into a single figure — **ordinary business income or loss** — which passes through to shareholders.

Items are allocated to shareholders based on their **per-share, per-day ownership** during the tax year. If a shareholder owns stock for only part of the year, their share is prorated.

**Worked Example:**

An S corporation with two equal shareholders has the following results:

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Gross receipts | $500,000 |
| Operating expenses | ($350,000) |
| Long-term capital gain | $20,000 |
| Charitable contributions | $8,000 |
| Tax-exempt interest | $3,000 |

**Ordinary business income:** $500,000 - $350,000 = $150,000
Each shareholder reports on their K-1:
- Ordinary business income: $75,000
- Long-term capital gain: $10,000
- Charitable contributions: $4,000
- Tax-exempt interest: $1,500

## Shareholder Basis

Shareholder basis in S corporation stock is critical because it determines:

- The deductibility of pass-through losses
- The tax treatment of distributions
- Gain or loss on sale of stock

### Initial Basis

Basis begins with the shareholder's investment:
- Cash contributed
- Adjusted basis of property contributed
- FMV of stock purchased from another shareholder

### Annual Basis Adjustments

Basis is adjusted annually in a specific order. The ordering matters because losses are limited to basis:

**Increases (in order):**
1. Income items (ordinary business income and separately stated income items)
2. Tax-exempt income
3. Excess depletion

**Decreases (in order):**
1. Distributions (nontaxable to the extent of basis)
2. Nondeductible, noncapital expenses (e.g., 50% of meals, penalties, life insurance premiums)
3. Deductible losses and deductions (limited to remaining basis)

> Exam tip: The ordering of basis adjustments is heavily tested. Basis is increased by all income (including tax-exempt) before it is decreased by distributions and losses. This ordering maximizes the amount of losses a shareholder can deduct.

**Worked Example:**

A shareholder begins the year with a stock basis of $30,000. During the year, the S corporation reports:

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Ordinary business income (share) | $20,000 |
| Tax-exempt interest (share) | $2,000 |
| Distribution received | $15,000 |
| Ordinary business loss (share) | — |
| Long-term capital loss (share) | ($40,000) |

| Adjustment | Calculation | Basis |
|-----------|------------|-------|
| Beginning basis | | $30,000 |
| Add: ordinary income | | $50,000 |
| Add: tax-exempt interest | | $52,000 |
| Less: distribution | | $37,000 |
| Less: capital loss (limited to basis) | ($40,000, but limited to $37,000) | **$0** |
| **Suspended loss** | | **$3,000** |

The $3,000 suspended loss carries forward indefinitely and is deductible when the shareholder's basis is restored.

### Debt Basis

Unlike partnerships, S corporation shareholders get basis only from **direct loans** they make to the corporation. Loans from third parties (even those personally guaranteed by the shareholder) do **not** increase shareholder basis.

If stock basis is reduced to zero, losses may be deducted against **debt basis** (the shareholder's basis in loans to the corporation). Distributions, however, cannot reduce debt basis.

## Distributions

S corporation distributions follow specific ordering rules that depend on whether the corporation has accumulated **earnings and profits (E&P)** from a prior C corporation existence.

### S Corporation with No Accumulated E&P

Distributions are treated as:
1. **Tax-free return of basis** — reduces stock basis (not below zero)
2. **Capital gain** — any excess is treated as gain from the sale of stock

### S Corporation with Accumulated C Corporation E&P

Distributions are applied against the **Accumulated Adjustments Account (AAA)** first:

1. **AAA** — distributions from AAA are tax-free return of basis (AAA tracks cumulative S corporation income minus distributions)
2. **Accumulated E&P** — distributions from accumulated E&P are taxed as **dividends**
3. **Remaining basis** — tax-free return of basis
4. **Excess** — capital gain

## Built-in Gains Tax

When a C corporation converts to S corporation status, the **built-in gains (BIG) tax** prevents the corporation from avoiding corporate-level tax on appreciation that existed at the time of conversion.

**Application:**
- Applies to assets held at the time of S election with FMV exceeding basis
- Triggered when the S corporation sells those assets within the **five-year recognition period** after conversion
- Tax rate: **21%** (the corporate rate) on the net recognized built-in gain
- The tax is an entity-level tax paid by the S corporation, reducing the amount that passes through to shareholders

**Worked Example:**

A C corporation with an asset (basis $100,000, FMV $250,000 at conversion) elects S status. Three years later, the S corporation sells the asset for $280,000.

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Selling price | $280,000 |
| Basis at time of sale | $100,000 |
| Total gain | $180,000 |
| Built-in gain at conversion ($250,000 - $100,000) | $150,000 |
| Post-conversion appreciation ($280,000 - $250,000) | $30,000 |
| **BIG tax** (21% x $150,000) | **$31,500** |

The $31,500 BIG tax is paid at the entity level. The remaining gain passes through to shareholders.

> Exam tip: The BIG tax only applies when a C corporation converts to an S corporation. A corporation that has always been an S corporation is not subject to this tax. The five-year recognition period is the key window.

## Key Terms

- **Form 2553** — the IRS form filed to elect S corporation status, requiring all shareholders' consent
- **Schedule K-1** — the form reporting each shareholder's share of S corporation income, deductions, and credits
- **Accumulated Adjustments Account (AAA)** — an S corporation account tracking cumulative income minus distributions, used to determine tax treatment of distributions when accumulated E&P exists
- **Built-in gains tax** — a corporate-level tax on pre-conversion appreciation when a C corporation converts to S corporation status
- **Debt basis** — a shareholder's basis in direct loans to the S corporation, which may allow additional loss deductions beyond stock basis

## Practice Problems

**1.** Which of the following may be a shareholder of an S corporation?

a) A partnership
b) A nonresident alien
c) A C corporation
d) A grantor trust

**Answer:** d) A grantor trust — Grantor trusts, qualified Subchapter S trusts (QSSTs), and electing small business trusts (ESBTs) are eligible S corporation shareholders. Partnerships, C corporations, and nonresident aliens are not eligible.

---

**2.** A shareholder has a stock basis of $25,000 at the beginning of the year. The S corporation reports ordinary income of $10,000 (shareholder's share) and makes a $30,000 distribution. What is the tax treatment of the distribution? (The corporation has no accumulated E&P.)

a) $30,000 tax-free return of capital
b) $35,000 tax-free, no gain
c) $25,000 tax-free, $5,000 capital gain
d) $35,000 tax-free, $0 capital gain (basis increases first)

**Answer:** d) $35,000 tax-free, $0 capital gain — Basis is increased by income ($10,000) before distributions, bringing basis to $35,000. The $30,000 distribution is a tax-free return of basis, reducing basis to $5,000. No gain is recognized.

---

**3.** An S corporation shareholder personally guarantees a $50,000 bank loan to the corporation. Does this increase the shareholder's basis?

a) Yes, by $50,000
b) Yes, by $25,000
c) No, only direct loans from the shareholder increase basis
d) Yes, if the shareholder is a more-than-2% shareholder

**Answer:** c) No, only direct loans increase basis — Unlike partnerships, S corporation shareholders only receive basis from direct loans they make to the corporation. Third-party loans guaranteed by the shareholder do not increase basis.
