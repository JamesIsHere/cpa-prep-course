# Prospective Analysis and Forecasting

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish between financial forecasts and financial projections
- Prepare pro forma financial statements using percentage-of-sales and other methods
- Apply regression analysis and time-series methods to financial data
- Identify the attestation standards governing prospective financial information

## Forecasts vs. Projections

The AICPA distinguishes two types of **prospective financial statements**:

**Financial forecast** — Presents expected financial position, results of operations, and cash flows based on the entity's **expected conditions and expected course of action**. This is the entity's best estimate of what will happen.

**Financial projection** — Presents financial results based on one or more **hypothetical assumptions** (what-if scenarios). Projections are useful for planning but are not predictions — they answer "what would happen if" rather than "what will happen."

| Feature | Forecast | Projection |
|---------|----------|------------|
| Basis | Expected conditions | Hypothetical assumptions |
| Distribution | General use (any third party) | Limited use (specified parties) |
| Exam answer | "Best estimate" | "What-if scenario" |
| Attestation | Examination or agreed-upon | Examination, agreed-upon, or compilation |

> **Exam tip:** Forecasts are for general use; projections are restricted to limited use. A forecast given only to a bank is still a forecast — the distinction is based on the assumptions, not the audience. But projections must never be distributed for general use.

## Pro Forma Financial Statements

Pro forma statements are forward-looking projections of a company's financial performance. The most common method is the **percentage-of-sales approach**.

### Percentage-of-Sales Method

This method assumes that most income statement and balance sheet items maintain a **stable relationship to revenue**. Steps:

1. Project future revenue (growth assumption)
2. Express each line item as a % of current revenue
3. Apply those percentages to projected revenue
4. Identify items that do **not** scale with sales (debt, equity, fixed charges)
5. Calculate the **external financing needed (EFN)**

**External Financing Needed:**

EFN = (A/S)(ΔS) - (L/S)(ΔS) - PM(S₁)(1 - d)

Where:
- A/S = Assets that vary with sales / Current sales
- L/S = Liabilities that spontaneously vary with sales / Current sales
- ΔS = Change in sales
- PM = Profit margin
- S₁ = Projected sales
- d = Dividend payout ratio

**Worked Example:**

Current sales: $1,000,000. Projected sales: $1,200,000. Total assets: $800,000 (all vary with sales). Spontaneous liabilities: $200,000. Net profit margin: 5%. Dividend payout: 40%.

- A/S = $800,000 / $1,000,000 = 0.80
- L/S = $200,000 / $1,000,000 = 0.20
- ΔS = $200,000
- PM x S₁ x (1 - d) = 5% x $1,200,000 x 0.60 = $36,000

EFN = 0.80($200,000) - 0.20($200,000) - $36,000 = $160,000 - $40,000 - $36,000 = **$84,000**

The company needs $84,000 in external financing (new debt or equity) to support the sales growth.

## Regression Analysis

**Simple linear regression** models the relationship between a dependent variable (Y) and one independent variable (X):

Y = a + bX

Where:
- a = Y-intercept (fixed component)
- b = Slope (variable component per unit of X)

**Multiple regression** extends this to two or more independent variables:

Y = a + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ + ... + bₙXₙ

### Key Statistics

- **R² (coefficient of determination)** — Percentage of variation in Y explained by the model. R² of 0.85 means the model explains 85% of the variation.
- **Standard error** — Measures the average distance of observed values from the regression line. Lower = better fit.
- **t-statistic / p-value** — Tests whether each coefficient is significantly different from zero. A p-value below 0.05 generally indicates significance.

> **Exam tip:** R² ranges from 0 to 1. A high R² does not prove causation — it only measures correlation. Also remember that adding more independent variables always increases R², so use adjusted R² when comparing models with different numbers of predictors.

**Worked Example:**

A company uses regression to predict overhead costs (Y) based on machine hours (X). Results: Y = $50,000 + $12X, R² = 0.92.

Interpretation: Fixed overhead is approximately $50,000 per period, and each machine hour adds $12 in variable overhead. The model explains 92% of the variation in overhead costs. At 10,000 machine hours: Y = $50,000 + $12(10,000) = **$170,000**.

## Time-Series Methods

### Moving Averages

A **simple moving average** smooths data by averaging the most recent N periods. A 3-month moving average forecast for April uses the average of January, February, and March.

A **weighted moving average** assigns greater weight to more recent observations, making the forecast more responsive to recent trends.

### Exponential Smoothing

Assigns exponentially decreasing weights to older observations using a **smoothing constant (α)**, where 0 < α < 1:

New forecast = α(Actual most recent) + (1 - α)(Previous forecast)

- Higher α (closer to 1) = more responsive to recent changes
- Lower α (closer to 0) = smoother, slower to react

**Worked Example:**

Previous forecast: 100 units. Actual demand: 120 units. α = 0.3.

New forecast = 0.3(120) + 0.7(100) = 36 + 70 = **106 units**

### Seasonal Adjustments

When data exhibits predictable seasonal patterns, analysts apply **seasonal indices** to adjust forecasts. The index represents how much a season deviates from the annual average:

- Index of 1.20 for Q4 means Q4 sales are typically 20% above the quarterly average
- Deseasonalized value = Actual / Seasonal index

## Sensitivity Analysis and Scenario Analysis

**Sensitivity analysis** changes **one variable at a time** to measure its impact on the outcome. Also called "what-if" analysis.

**Scenario analysis** changes **multiple variables simultaneously** to model different states of the world (best case, base case, worst case).

Both techniques help management and analysts understand the range of possible outcomes and identify which assumptions drive the most risk.

## Attestation Standards for Prospective Information

Under AT-C Section 305, a CPA can perform:

- **Examination** — Provides positive assurance (opinion) on whether the assumptions are reasonable and the statements are prepared in conformity with AICPA guidelines
- **Agreed-upon procedures** — Report of findings based on specific procedures agreed to by the parties
- **Compilation** — Assembly of prospective statements without assurance (projections only)

A CPA **cannot** audit prospective financial statements — the nature of forward-looking information precludes an audit opinion.

## Key Terms

- **External financing needed (EFN)** — The additional debt or equity a company must raise to support projected sales growth after accounting for internally generated funds
- **R-squared** — The coefficient of determination; measures the proportion of variation in the dependent variable explained by the independent variable(s) in a regression model
- **Exponential smoothing** — A time-series forecasting method that weights recent observations more heavily, controlled by the smoothing constant alpha
- **Sensitivity analysis** — A technique that measures the effect of changing one input variable at a time on a projected outcome
