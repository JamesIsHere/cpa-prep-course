# Capital Structure and Valuation

## Learning Objectives

- Calculate the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) and its components
- Apply the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) to estimate the cost of equity
- Evaluate capital structure decisions using Modigliani-Miller propositions
- Analyze the trade-offs between debt and equity financing

## Cost of Capital

The **cost of capital** is the minimum return a company must earn on its investments to satisfy its investors. It reflects the blended cost of all financing sources — debt, preferred stock, and common equity.

### Cost of Debt

The cost of debt is the **after-tax** interest rate on borrowings:

Cost of debt = Interest rate x (1 - Tax rate)

The tax adjustment reflects the **tax deductibility** of interest expense. A company paying 6% interest with a 25% tax rate has an after-tax cost of debt of 6% x 0.75 = **4.5%**.

### Cost of Preferred Stock

Preferred stock pays a fixed dividend and does not offer a tax deduction:

Cost of preferred = Annual preferred dividend / Net issuance price

A preferred stock paying $5 per year, issued at $50 net of flotation costs, has a cost of $5 / $50 = **10%**.

### Cost of Equity (CAPM)

The **Capital Asset Pricing Model** estimates the required return on equity:

Cost of equity = Rᶠ + β(Rₘ - Rᶠ)

Where:
- Rᶠ = Risk-free rate (typically 10-year Treasury yield)
- β (beta) = Measure of the stock's systematic risk relative to the market
- Rₘ - Rᶠ = Market risk premium (expected market return minus risk-free rate)

**Worked Example:**

Risk-free rate: 4%. Market risk premium: 6%. Beta: 1.3.

Cost of equity = 4% + 1.3(6%) = 4% + 7.8% = **11.8%**

A beta of 1.3 means the stock is 30% more volatile than the market. Higher beta = higher required return.

> **Exam tip:** Beta measures systematic (market) risk only — not total risk. Diversifiable (unsystematic) risk is not compensated in CAPM because investors can eliminate it through portfolio diversification.

### Alternative: Dividend Growth Model

For dividend-paying stocks, the **Gordon Growth Model** provides an alternative:

Cost of equity = (D₁ / P₀) + g

Where:
- D₁ = Expected dividend next year
- P₀ = Current stock price
- g = Constant dividend growth rate

A stock priced at $50, expecting a $3 dividend next year, growing at 5%: Cost = ($3 / $50) + 5% = 6% + 5% = **11%**.

## Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)

WACC blends the costs of all capital sources weighted by their **market value proportions**:

WACC = wᵈ × rᵈ(1-T) + wᵖ × rᵖ + wᵉ × rₑ

Where:
- wᵈ, wᵖ, wᵉ = Weight of debt, preferred stock, and equity in the capital structure
- rᵈ = Cost of debt
- T = Corporate tax rate
- rᵖ = Cost of preferred stock
- rₑ = Cost of equity

**Worked Example:**

A company has a target capital structure of 40% debt, 10% preferred stock, and 50% equity. Cost of debt: 6%. Tax rate: 25%. Cost of preferred: 9%. Cost of equity: 12%.

WACC = 0.40(6%)(1 - 0.25) + 0.10(9%) + 0.50(12%)
WACC = 0.40(4.5%) + 0.90% + 6.00%
WACC = 1.80% + 0.90% + 6.00% = **8.70%**

Any project the company undertakes must earn at least 8.70% to create value.

> **Exam tip:** WACC uses market value weights, not book value weights. If the question gives both, use market values. Also remember that only debt gets the tax shield — equity and preferred dividends are not tax-deductible.

## Capital Structure Theory

### Modigliani-Miller (M&M)

**Proposition I (No taxes):** In a perfect market with no taxes, the value of a firm is independent of its capital structure. The total pie is the same regardless of how you slice it between debt and equity.

**Proposition I (With taxes):** When corporate taxes exist, the value of a levered firm exceeds that of an unlevered firm by the present value of the interest tax shield:

V_levered = V_unlevered + (Tax rate x Debt)

**Proposition II:** The cost of equity increases linearly with the debt-to-equity ratio. As a firm takes on more debt, equity becomes riskier, and shareholders demand a higher return. However, because debt is cheaper (especially after tax), WACC initially decreases.

### Trade-Off Theory

The **optimal capital structure** balances:

- **Tax benefits of debt** — Interest is tax-deductible, creating a tax shield
- **Costs of financial distress** — Too much debt increases bankruptcy risk, legal costs, loss of customers and suppliers, and agency costs

The optimal point minimizes WACC and maximizes firm value.

### Pecking Order Theory

Companies prefer financing in this order:

1. **Internal funds** (retained earnings) — No issuance costs or dilution
2. **Debt** — Lower information asymmetry costs than equity
3. **Equity** — Last resort, signals that management believes stock is overvalued

This theory explains why profitable companies often carry less debt — they don't need external financing.

## Financial Leverage

**Degree of financial leverage (DFL)** measures how sensitive EPS is to changes in EBIT:

DFL = EBIT / (EBIT - Interest expense)

A DFL of 2.0 means a 10% increase in EBIT produces a 20% increase in EPS — but also a 20% decrease if EBIT falls 10%.

**Degree of operating leverage (DOL)** measures how sensitive EBIT is to changes in sales:

DOL = Contribution margin / EBIT = (Sales - Variable costs) / EBIT

**Degree of total leverage (DTL)** = DOL x DFL — measures the sensitivity of EPS to changes in sales.

**Worked Example:**

A company has sales of $500,000, variable costs of $300,000, fixed costs of $120,000, and interest expense of $30,000.

- Contribution margin: $500,000 - $300,000 = $200,000
- EBIT: $200,000 - $120,000 = $80,000
- DOL: $200,000 / $80,000 = **2.50**
- DFL: $80,000 / ($80,000 - $30,000) = $80,000 / $50,000 = **1.60**
- DTL: 2.50 x 1.60 = **4.00**

A 10% increase in sales will produce a 40% increase in EPS.

## Key Terms

- **WACC** — Weighted average cost of capital; the blended cost of debt, preferred stock, and equity, used as the discount rate for corporate investment decisions
- **Beta** — A measure of a stock's systematic risk relative to the overall market; a beta above 1.0 indicates higher-than-market volatility
- **Tax shield** — The reduction in income taxes resulting from deducting interest expense; equals the interest payment multiplied by the tax rate
- **Financial distress costs** — Direct costs (legal fees, bankruptcy filings) and indirect costs (lost customers, restricted operations) of carrying excessive debt
